\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Bradwell v. Illinois}, 83 U.S. 130 (1873)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} A day after \textit{Slaughter-House}: no Privileges or Immunities right to a state law license, and Bradley's ``law of the Creator'' concurrence (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/83/130/}{83 U.S. 130}).

\textbf{Facts.} Myra Bradwell, Vermont-born editor of the \textit{Chicago Legal News} and a longtime Chicago resident, sought an Illinois law license. The Illinois Supreme Court refused her because she was a married woman and because the legislature never contemplated admitting women.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Bradwell sued out a writ of error to the U.S. Supreme Court.

\textbf{Votes.} Affirmed, 8--1. Miller wrote for five; Bradley, Swayne, and Field concurred in the judgment; Chase dissented without opinion.

\textbf{Holding.} Article IV, \S{} 2 does not protect a citizen against her own state, and admission to a state bar is not a privilege or immunity of United States citizenship, so states may set bar qualifications free of Fourteenth Amendment control.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Miller's doctrinal opinion noted that bar admission had never depended on U.S. citizenship and treated \textit{Slaughter-House} as conclusive. Bradley, a \textit{Slaughter-House} dissenter who protected the right to pursue a lawful occupation, reconciled his votes by asserting that the right had never been a fundamental privilege of women, citing coverture and ``nature herself.'' Chase dissented ``from the judgment of the Court and from all the opinions.''

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a Privileges or Immunities challenge to a hair-braiding license fails. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a state school's sex-based exclusion falls under Equal Protection (\textit{Hogan}). \emph{Fence-Sitter:} exclusion from a federal-court bar.

\textbf{Critique.} Feminists see constitutionalized patriarchy that delayed scrutiny of sex discrimination until \textit{Reed v. Reed}; originalists say the Clause's original meaning was mutilated, though they divide over whether it would have helped Bradwell.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Man is, or should be, woman's protector and defender. The natural and proper timidity and delicacy which belongs to the female sex evidently unfits it for many of the occupations of civil life.'' --- Justice Bradley, concurring in the judgment, 83 U.S. at 141.
