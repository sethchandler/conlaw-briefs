\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Wolford v. Lopez}, 609 U.S. 185 (2026)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Hawaii's ``vampire rule'' falls: states cannot flip the implied-license default to hobble public carry (\href{https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-1046_nmio.pdf}{Slip Op.}).

\textbf{Facts.} After \textit{Bruen}, Hawaii's Act 52 barred licensed carriers from bringing guns onto private property of another, including stores and restaurants open to the public, without the owner's express authorization. Maui permit holders sued the Attorney General.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The district court enjoined the rule; the Ninth Circuit reversed; the Supreme Court reversed.

\textbf{Votes.} 6--3. Alito wrote for the Court; Barrett concurred; Kagan and Jackson dissented.

\textbf{Holding.} A state may not require express owner consent before licensed carriers bring handguns onto private property open to the public; owners remain free to exclude guns themselves.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The rule burdens conduct the Second Amendment covers, and a carrier running errands could become a criminal many times over. Colonial anti-poaching laws, an 1893 Oregon law, and an 1865 Black Code are not relevantly similar. Barrett stressed state action; Jackson saw the case as about property rights, not gun rights.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a signage-only opt-in rule falls. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a store's own ``No Firearms'' sign stands. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} an opt-in default limited to nightclubs.

\textbf{Critique.} Supporters praise the end of post-\textit{Bruen} evasion that turned a right into a permission slip; Maureen Brady and other critics see a level-of-generality problem in \textit{Bruen}'s method and an unguided test for discarding tainted history.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``This regime hobbles what the Second Amendment protects: the right of Americans to carry arms for self-defense as they go about their daily lives. We hold that the law is unconstitutional.'' --- Justice Alito, Opinion of the Court.
