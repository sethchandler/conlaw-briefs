\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Friends of the Earth, Inc.\ v.\ Laidlaw Environmental Services (TOC), Inc.}, 528 U.S.\ 167 (2000)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Curtailed use of a polluted river is injury; deterrent penalties redress; voluntary cessation rarely moots (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/528/167/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} Laidlaw's incinerator violated its mercury discharge permit nearly 500 times. Environmental groups sued under the Clean Water Act for civil penalties payable to the Treasury.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The district court imposed a \$405,800 penalty; the Fourth Circuit held the case moot.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed 7--2: Ginsburg for the Court; Stevens and Kennedy concurred; Scalia, with Thomas, dissented.

\textbf{Holding.} Reasonable concerns that curtail use of an area are injury in fact; penalties for violations ongoing at filing redress through deterrence; and voluntary cessation moots a case only if recurrence cannot reasonably be expected.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The injury that matters is to the plaintiff, not the environment. Unlike \textit{Steel Co.}, violations here were ongoing. Mootness differs from standing: the defendant bears the burden. Kennedy flagged Article~II concerns; Scalia said injury became a ``mere pleading requirement.''

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a plant that complies mid-suit. \emph{Opposite-Side:} violations that ended before suit. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a closed plant that keeps its permit.

\textbf{Critique.} Standing may rest on subjective fear, and private suits for public fines may intrude on executive enforcement.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``[A] defendant claiming that its voluntary compliance moots a case bears the formidable burden of showing that it is absolutely clear the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur.'' --- Justice Ginsburg, 528 U.S.\ at 190.
