\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Tennessee Wine \& Spirits Retailers Ass'n v. Thomas}, 588 U.S. 504 (2019)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Durational residency for liquor retailers is protectionism that \S~2 of the Twenty-first Amendment does not shield (\href{https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/18pdf/18-96_5i36.pdf}{Slip Opinion}).

\textbf{Facts.} Tennessee required two years of residency for an initial retail liquor license, ten years to renew, and full resident ownership for corporations; its Attorney General thought the rules unconstitutional. When Total Wine (Maryland owners) and the Ketchums (newly arrived from Utah) applied, an association of incumbent stores threatened suit.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The district court and a divided Sixth Circuit held the rule unconstitutional; the Supreme Court affirmed.

\textbf{Votes.} 7--2: Alito for the Court; Gorsuch, joined by Thomas, dissented.

\textbf{Holding.} The rule discriminates against nonresidents, and \S~2 does not save laws whose predominant effect is protectionism rather than evidence-backed health or safety.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Section 2 constitutionalized the Wilson and Webb-Kenyon Acts' evenhanded regime; residency is not an ``essential feature'' of three-tier systems; agents, background checks, and inspections serve the State's goals. Gorsuch said the people displaced the dormant Commerce Clause for alcohol and that the new test is unworkable.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} in-state property-ownership rules. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a state retail monopoly. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} in-state premises rules for wine shipping.

\textbf{Critique.} The hybrid standard is indeterminate about mixed motives and about when higher prices are legitimate temperance rather than protectionism.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Section 2 gives the States regulatory authority that they would not otherwise enjoy, but as we pointed out in \textit{Granholm}, `mere speculation' or `unsupported assertions' are insufficient to sustain a law that would otherwise violate the Commerce Clause. \ldots{} Where the predominant effect of a law is protectionism, not the protection of public health or safety, it is not shielded by \S~2.'' --- Justice Alito, 588 U.S. at 539--540.
