\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Trump v. United States}, 603 U.S. 593 (2024)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Presidential immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/23-939}{603 U.S.\ 593}).

\textbf{Facts.} Donald Trump was indicted for conspiring to overturn the 2020 election, including pressuring state officials and Vice President Pence, organizing fraudulent electors, and trying to use the Justice Department to back false fraud claims.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The district court denied immunity; the D.C.\ Circuit affirmed.

\textbf{Votes.} Vacated, 6--3 (Roberts for the Court; Thomas and Barrett concurring; Sotomayor and Jackson dissenting, with Kagan).

\textbf{Holding.} A former President is absolutely immune for acts within his ``conclusive and preclusive'' authority, at least presumptively immune for other official acts, and not immune for unofficial acts. Courts may not probe motive, and evidence of immune acts is inadmissible. Trump's Justice Department dealings were immune.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Congress cannot regulate exclusive powers, and the threat of prosecution would chill an ``energetic'' Executive and risk ``an Executive Branch that cannibalizes itself.'' Barrett rejected the evidentiary bar. Sotomayor called the immunity ``atextual, ahistorical, and unjustifiable''; Jackson said it revives the idea that ``the King can do no wrong.''

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} ordering the Attorney General to drop a case. \emph{Opposite-Side:} business tax fraud. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} conditioning foreign aid on investigating a rival.

\textbf{Critique.} Scholars fault reliance on functional fears over text and see weakened accountability.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``We conclude that under our constitutional structure of separated powers, the nature of Presidential power requires that a former President have some immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts during his tenure in office. At least with respect to the President's exercise of his core constitutional powers, this immunity must be absolute. As for his remaining official actions, he is also entitled to immunity.'' --- Chief Justice Roberts, for the Court.
