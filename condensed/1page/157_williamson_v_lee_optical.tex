\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Williamson v. Lee Optical of Oklahoma, Inc.}, 348 U.S. 483 (1955)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The opticians case: rational basis review means any conceivable rationale suffices (\href{https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/105285/williamson-v-lee-optical-of-oklahoma-inc/}{348 U.S. 483}).

\textbf{Facts.} A 1953 Oklahoma law barred opticians from fitting lenses into new frames without a prescription, exempted ready-to-wear glasses, banned eyeglass advertising, and barred eye-care providers from retail stores. Lee Optical sued.

\textbf{Procedural History.} A three-judge District Court struck down most of the challenged provisions; both sides appealed directly.

\textbf{Votes.} 8--0. Douglas wrote for the Court; there were no separate opinions.

\textbf{Holding.} All provisions are valid. A business regulation stands if there is an evil at hand and the legislature might rationally think its measure a correction; economic classifications fail only if invidious.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The prescription rule may be ``needless'' and ``wasteful,'' but the legislature might have thought frequent eye examinations vital, and balancing is its role. Reform may proceed ``one step at a time.'' Frame advertising might lure lens sales, and keeping eye care out of stores frees the profession from ``all taints of commercialism.'' The \textit{Lochner} era was over.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} licensing casket sellers. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a racial bar on licenses. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} cosmetology training for hair braiders.

\textbf{Critique.} Libertarians call it a rubber stamp for cartels; defenders, a bar to reviving \textit{Lochner}.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``But the law need not be in every respect logically consistent with its aims to be constitutional. It is enough that there is an evil at hand for correction, and that it might be thought that the particular legislative measure was a rational way to correct it.'' --- Justice Douglas, Opinion of the Court, 348 U.S. at 487--488.
