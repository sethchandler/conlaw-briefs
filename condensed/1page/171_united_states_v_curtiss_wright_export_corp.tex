\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{United States v. Curtiss-Wright Export Corp.}, 299 U.S. 304 (1936)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Broad foreign-affairs delegations are valid; the President is the ``sole organ'' of external relations (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/299/304/}{299 U.S. 304}).

\textbf{Facts.} A 1934 Joint Resolution made arms sales to Bolivia or Paraguay, at war in the Chaco, a crime if the President found an embargo might restore peace and so proclaimed. Roosevelt did, and Curtiss-Wright Export Corp. was indicted for conspiring to sell 15 machine guns to Bolivia.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The district court quashed the indictment as resting on an invalid delegation; the United States appealed directly.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed 7--1. Sutherland wrote for the Court; McReynolds dissented without opinion; Stone did not participate.

\textbf{Holding.} Congress may give the President far broader discretion in external affairs than in domestic ones. The proclamation was sufficient, and its revocation did not end liability.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Enumerated powers are true ``only in respect of our internal affairs''; external sovereignty passed from the Crown to the United States collectively. Quoting John Marshall, Sutherland called the President ``the sole organ of the nation in its external relations,'' with the information, secrecy, and dispatch foreign affairs demand, and cited embargo statutes since 1794. Jackson's \textit{Youngstown} concurrence and \textit{Zivotofsky} (2015) later confined this language.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a chip-export ban on a strategic-threat finding. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a presidential order binding state courts (\textit{Medell\'in}). \emph{Fence-Sitter:} banning a foreign-owned app.

\textbf{Critique.} Historians show the States exercised external powers before 1789, and Marshall meant communicator, not policymaker.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``It is important to bear in mind that we are here dealing not alone with an authority vested in the President by an exertion of legislative power, but with such an authority plus the very delicate, plenary and exclusive power of the President as the sole organ of the federal government in the field of international relations.'' --- Justice Sutherland, 299 U.S. at 319--320.
