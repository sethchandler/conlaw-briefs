\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Duncan v.\ Louisiana}, 391 U.S.\ 145 (1968)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Incorporation of the criminal jury right against the states (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/391/145/}{391 U.S. 145}).

\textbf{Facts.} Gary Duncan, a young Black man in Plaquemines Parish, Louisiana, was accused of slapping a white boy's elbow while urging his cousins away from a confrontation. Charged with simple battery, punishable by two years, he was denied a jury because Louisiana allowed juries only for capital or hard-labor crimes, and was sentenced to 60 days and a \$150 fine.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The Louisiana Supreme Court denied review; Duncan appealed.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed, 7--2. White wrote for the Court; Black and Fortas concurred; Harlan dissented, joined by Stewart.

\textbf{Holding.} Due process guarantees a jury in every state prosecution that would require one federally. Petty offenses are exempt, but the maximum authorized penalty measures seriousness, and two years is serious.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The question is whether a safeguard is fundamental in the American common-law system, not in any conceivable fair system; the jury's long history and role as a check on oppressive prosecutors and judges make it so. Black reaffirmed total incorporation; Fortas rejected jot-for-jot incorporation of rules like unanimity. Harlan defended bench trials and warned of a ``constitutional straitjacket'' on the states.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a one-year shoplifting charge needs a jury. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a 90-day traffic offense is petty. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} stacked petty counts.

\textbf{Critique.} Critics question selective incorporation's logic and note that \textit{Williams} and \textit{Apodaca} diluted the jury until \textit{Ramos}.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Because we believe that trial by jury in criminal cases is fundamental to the American scheme of justice, we hold that the Fourteenth Amendment guarantees a right of jury trial in all criminal cases which --- were they to be tried in a federal court --- would come within the Sixth Amendment's guarantee.'' --- Justice White, Opinion of the Court, 391 U.S. at 149.
