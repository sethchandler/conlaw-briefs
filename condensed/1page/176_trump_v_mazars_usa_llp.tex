\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Trump v. Mazars USA, LLP}, 591 U.S. 848 (2020)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Congressional subpoenas for a President's personal records face a four-factor separation-of-powers test (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/591/19-715/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} Three House committees subpoenaed banks and the accounting firm Mazars USA for a decade of President Trump's financial records, citing money-laundering, foreign-influence, ethics, and financial-disclosure legislation. The Oversight Committee's subpoena to Mazars followed Michael Cohen's testimony.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The President sued personally, without asserting executive privilege; the D.C. and Second Circuits upheld the subpoenas over dissents by Judges Rao and Livingston.

\textbf{Votes.} Vacated and remanded, 7--2: Roberts for the Court; Thomas and Alito dissenting.

\textbf{Holding.} Courts must weigh alternative sources, breadth, evidence of legislative purpose, and burdens on the President before enforcing such subpoenas.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Two centuries of interbranch accommodation deserve weight. Executive-privilege rigor does not fit private papers, but the House's deference ignores that Congress and the President are rival branches, and concerns persist when papers are personal or held by third parties. Thomas would require impeachment; Alito found the subpoenas ``inherently suspicious.''

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a decade of presidential medical records. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a state grand jury subpoena under \textit{Vance}. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a narrow subpoena to a former President.

\textbf{Critique.} The open-ended test invites delay that outlasts a two-year House subpoena, and a county prosecutor in \textit{Vance} faced a lower bar than Congress.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``The President is the only person who alone composes a branch of government. As a result, there is not always a clear line between his personal and official affairs.'' --- Chief Justice Roberts.
