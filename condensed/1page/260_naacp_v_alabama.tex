\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{NAACP v. Alabama ex rel. Patterson}, 357 U.S. 449 (1958)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Compelled disclosure of the NAACP's members violated the freedom of association (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/357/449/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} Alabama sued in 1956 to oust the NAACP as an unregistered foreign corporation. The NAACP offered to qualify and produced its charter, officers, and records, but refused to name rank-and-file members.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Held in contempt and fined \$100,000; the Alabama Supreme Court refused review on procedural grounds.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed and remanded, 9--0 (Harlan for a unanimous Court).

\textbf{Holding.} Freedom of association is part of Fourteenth Amendment ``liberty''; compelled disclosure exposing members to reprisal, without a compelling interest substantially related to the names, is unconstitutional.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The NAACP could assert members' rights, since suing in their own names would destroy their anonymity. Curtailing association draws ``the closest scrutiny,'' and past disclosure brought ``economic reprisal, loss of employment, threat of physical coercion.'' Private reprisals counted because state compulsion triggered them, and the names had no ``substantial bearing'' on Alabama's registration inquiry. There were no separate opinions.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} teachers listing every association (\textit{Shelton}). \emph{Opposite-Side:} public reporting of campaign contributions (\textit{Buckley}). \emph{Fence-Sitter:} confidential donor filings aimed at foreign influence.

\textbf{Critique.} Built an unenumerated right; critics fear it now shields ``dark money.''

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``It is beyond debate that freedom to engage in association for the advancement of beliefs and ideas is an inseparable aspect of the `liberty' assured by the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment, which embraces freedom of speech.'' --- Justice Harlan, 357 U.S. at 460.
