\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife}, 504 U.S. 555 (1992)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The modern three-part standing test: ``some day'' plans are not imminent injury (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/504/555/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} Defenders of Wildlife challenged a rule limiting the Endangered Species Act's consultation duty to domestic actions, relying on two members: Joyce Kelly, who had visited Egypt to observe the habitat of the endangered Nile crocodile, and Amy Skilbred, who had visited Sri Lanka near the AID-funded Mahaweli project. Each intended to return ``in the future,'' but Skilbred had no current plans.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The Eighth Circuit found standing and ruled for Defenders on the merits.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed 7--2 (6--3 on standing): Scalia for the Court; Kennedy and Stevens concurred; Blackmun, with O'Connor, dissented.

\textbf{Holding.} Standing requires injury in fact, traceability, and redressability; Congress cannot confer standing to enforce an abstract procedural duty.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Past visits and indefinite intentions show no imminent injury, and nexus theories are speculation. The plurality added that the funding agencies were not parties. Letting Congress convert the public interest in executive compliance into an individual right would shift the President's Take Care duty to the courts. Kennedy stressed that Congress may still define new injuries; Blackmun called the plane-ticket demand ``an empty formality.''

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a ``someday'' traveler. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a researcher booked to study the affected site. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} an undisseminated credit error (\textit{TransUnion}).

\textbf{Critique.} ``Injury in fact'' is a modern invention, and early Congresses authorized informer suits.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Over the years, our cases have established that the irreducible constitutional minimum of standing contains three elements.'' --- Justice Scalia, 504 U.S. at 560.
