\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Champion v. Ames}, 188 U.S. 321 (1903)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The Lottery Case: the power to regulate interstate commerce includes the power to prohibit it (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/188/321/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} The Federal Lottery Act of 1895 made it a crime to cause lottery tickets to be ``carried from one state to another.'' Charles F. Champion was indicted for shipping Paraguayan lottery tickets by express from Dallas to Fresno.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Held by U.S. Marshal John C. Ames, Champion sought habeas corpus; the circuit court dismissed the writ, and the Supreme Court affirmed after three arguments.

\textbf{Votes.} 5--4: Harlan for Brown, White, McKenna, and Holmes; Fuller, with Brewer, Shiras, and Peckham, dissented.

\textbf{Holding.} Lottery tickets are articles of commerce, and Congress may prohibit their interstate carriage as injurious to public morals without violating the Tenth or Fifth Amendment.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Commerce includes ``traffic''; under \textit{McCulloch}, Congress may choose prohibition, as with diseased cattle; Congress supplemented state lottery bans. Fuller called this a national police power: tickets are no more commerce than ``an invitation to dine.'' \textit{Darby} later vindicated \textit{Champion}.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} interstate gun-kit shipments. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a federal public-intoxication ban (\textit{Lopez}). \emph{Fence-Sitter:} possession after interstate travel.

\textbf{Critique.} Once Congress may close channels for moral reasons, the ``harmful-in-itself'' limit collapses.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``As a state may, for the purpose of guarding the morals of its own people, forbid all sales of lottery tickets within its limits, so Congress, for the purpose of guarding the people of the United States against the `widespread pestilence of lotteries' and to protect the commerce which concerns all the states, may prohibit the carrying of lottery tickets from one state to another.'' --- Justice Harlan, 188 U.S. at 357.
