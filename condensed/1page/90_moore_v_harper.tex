\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Moore v. Harper}, 600 U.S.\ 1 (2023)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} No ``independent state legislature'': state courts may review congressional maps under state constitutions, within ordinary bounds (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/21-1271}{Cornell LII}).

\textbf{Facts.} North Carolina's legislature drew partisan congressional maps in 2021. Voters sued under the state constitution's Free Elections Clause; legislators argued the Federal Elections Clause gave them exclusive, unchecked power.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The North Carolina Supreme Court struck the maps (\textit{Harper I}), then, with a new majority, overruled that decision without vacating its injunction.

\textbf{Votes.} Affirmed, 6--3: Roberts for the Court; Kavanaugh concurring; Thomas, with Gorsuch and (on mootness) Alito, dissenting.

\textbf{Holding.} The case was live. The Elections Clause does not free legislatures from state constitutional limits or state judicial review, but state courts may not exceed the ordinary bounds of judicial review.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Judicial review was a Founding-era ``fundamental principle,'' and precedent treats election rules as lawmaking bound by state constitutions. Kavanaugh would use Rehnquist's \textit{Bush v. Gore} test; Thomas called the case moot and would bar substantive state limits.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} enforcing express compactness rules. \emph{Opposite-Side:} rewriting a clear ballot deadline on ``policy fairness'' alone. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a novel emergency deadline extension.

\textbf{Critique.} The undefined ``ordinary bounds'' limit may make federal judges supervisors of state election law.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``The Elections Clause does not insulate state legislatures from the ordinary exercise of state judicial review.'' --- Chief Justice Roberts.
