\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Shelby County v. Holder}, 570 U.S. 529 (2013)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The Voting Rights Act's \S~4(b) coverage formula is too outdated to justify \S~5 preclearance (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/12-96}{Cornell LII}).

\textbf{Facts.} Section 5 required covered jurisdictions to preclear every voting change; \S~4(b) defined coverage by 1964-era tests and turnout. Congress extended the scheme in 2006 for 25 years without updating the formula, after \textit{Northwest Austin} (2009) warned that ``[t]hings have changed in the South.''

\textbf{Procedural History.} Shelby County, Alabama, sought a declaration that \S\S~4(b) and 5 were facially unconstitutional; the lower courts upheld them, and the Supreme Court reversed.

\textbf{Votes.} 5--4: Roberts for Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito; Thomas concurred; Ginsburg, with Breyer, Sotomayor, and Kagan, dissented.

\textbf{Holding.} Section 4(b) is unconstitutional because it rests on 40-year-old data; \S~5 and \S~2 were left standing, but preclearance became inoperable.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The ``fundamental principle of equal sovereignty'' and States' power over elections require that current burdens be justified by current needs; ``things have changed dramatically,'' yet Congress kept the old formula. Thomas would void \S~5. Ginsburg answered that discrimination had evolved into ``second-generation barriers'' and that the Court ignored a 15,000-page record.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a poll-tax-history formula. \emph{Opposite-Side:} judicial ``bail-in'' after intentional discrimination. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} equal sovereignty outside voting.

\textbf{Critique.} ``Equal sovereignty'' is not in the text, and the Reconstruction Amendments altered federalism; the facial ruling also ignored the Act's severability clause and Alabama's own record.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Throwing out preclearance when it has worked and is continuing to work to stop discriminatory changes is like throwing away your umbrella in a rainstorm because you are not getting wet.'' --- Justice Ginsburg, dissenting, 570 U.S. at 590.
