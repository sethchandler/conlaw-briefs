\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Ward v. Rock Against Racism}, 491 U.S. 781 (1989)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} A content-neutral time, place, and manner rule need not be the least restrictive means (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/491/781/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} After noise complaints about Rock Against Racism's Central Park bandshell concerts, New York required all performers to use city sound equipment run by a city technician, who deferred to sponsors on mix.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The district court upheld the rule; the Second Circuit reversed, requiring the ``least intrusive'' means.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed, 6--3 (Kennedy for the Court; Blackmun in the result; Marshall, Brennan, and Stevens dissenting).

\textbf{Holding.} The guideline is a valid content-neutral regulation; narrow tailoring is met if the rule serves a substantial interest that would be achieved less effectively without it and does not burden substantially more speech than necessary.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The government's purpose controls content neutrality, and noise control is unrelated to message. Least-restrictive-means analysis is no part of time, place, and manner review; courts defer to reasonable choices. Ample alternatives remained since only volume was moderated. \emph{Separately:} Marshall said the Court ``eviscerat[ed]'' narrow tailoring and that city control of the knob was a prior restraint.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a sound limiter at an amphitheater beside a hospital. \emph{Opposite-Side:} lowering volume for ``offensive or hateful lyrics.'' \emph{Fence-Sitter:} banning amplification on a park's main rally lawn.

\textbf{Critique.} In tension with \textit{McCullen}; relies on unwritten narrowing practices.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Lest any confusion on the point remain, we reaffirm today that a regulation of the time, place, or manner of protected speech must be narrowly tailored to serve the government's legitimate, content-neutral interests, but that it need not be the least restrictive or least intrusive means of doing so.'' --- Justice Kennedy, 491 U.S. at 798--799.
