\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Shurtleff v. City of Boston}, 596 U.S. 243 (2022)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The City Hall flagpole case: a forum open to all is private speech, not government speech (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/596/20-1800/}{596 U.S. 243}).

\textbf{Facts.} Boston approved 284 private flag raisings on a City Hall Plaza flagpole without review, then refused Camp Constitution's ``Christian flag'' for fear of violating the Establishment Clause.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The District Court and the First Circuit held the flags government speech.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed, 9--0. Breyer wrote for the Court; Kavanaugh concurred; Alito and Gorsuch concurred in the judgment.

\textbf{Holding.} Weighing history, public perception, and active control, Boston's program was private speech, and refusing the flag for its religious viewpoint violated the Free Speech Clause.

\textbf{Reasoning.} History favored Boston and perception was mixed, but Boston controlled the flags' messages ``not at all,'' unlike \textit{Summum} or \textit{Walker}: it never inspected flags and had no written guidelines. With government speech gone, refusing a flag because it promoted a religion was viewpoint discrimination. Alito would ask whether government purposefully speaks through its agents; Gorsuch blamed \textit{Lemon}.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} rejecting an atheist sign from an open holiday display. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a closed-list flag policy. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a juried mural program.

\textbf{Critique.} Cities may simply close forums, and the control factor can reward censorship.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``In answering these questions, we conduct a holistic inquiry designed to determine whether the government intends to speak for itself or to regulate private expression. Our review is not mechanical; it is driven by a case's context rather than the rote application of rigid factors.'' --- Justice Breyer, Opinion of the Court, 596 U.S. at 252.
