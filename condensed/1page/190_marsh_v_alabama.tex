\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Marsh v.\ Alabama}, 326 U.S.\ 501 (1946)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} A company town performs a public function, so a Jehovah's Witness could not be convicted of trespass for leafletting on its sidewalk (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/326/501/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} Gulf Shipbuilding owned all of Chickasaw, Alabama---homes, streets, a business block, a post office---open to highway travelers. Grace Marsh distributed religious literature on its sidewalk despite a no-solicitation notice and refused to leave.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Convicted of criminal trespass; the Alabama Court of Appeals affirmed because Gulf held title.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed 5--3: Black for the Court; Frankfurter concurred; Reed, with Stone and Burton, dissented; Jackson did not participate.

\textbf{Holding.} A corporation governing a community with all the attributes of a municipality performs a ``public function''; the State may not punish leafletting on its open sidewalks.

\textbf{Reasoning.} An ordinary town could not ban the leafletting, and state-law title is not dispositive. Company-town residents are free citizens who need uncensored information, and press and religious freedom occupy a ``preferred position.'' Frankfurter: ``a company-owned town is a town.'' Reed decried a ``novel Constitutional doctrine.'' Later cases (\textit{Hudgens}, \textit{Halleck}) confined \textit{Marsh} to company towns.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a corporate ``tech town'' with deputized security. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a dominant social-media platform. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a developer-owned town with public police and schools.

\textbf{Critique.} \textit{Marsh} blends public-function and state-enforcement theories and balances where modern doctrine applies a threshold test.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Ownership does not always mean absolute dominion. The more an owner, for his advantage, opens up his property for use by the public in general, the more do his rights become circumscribed by the statutory and constitutional rights of those who use it.'' --- Justice Black, 326 U.S.\ at 506.
