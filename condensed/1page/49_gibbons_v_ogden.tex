\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Gibbons v. Ogden}, 22 U.S. (9 Wheat.) 1 (1824)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The steamboat monopoly case: navigation is commerce, and a federal license beats a state monopoly (\href{https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/85412/gibbons-v-ogden/}{22 U.S.\ 1}).

\textbf{Facts.} New York gave Livingston and Fulton an exclusive right to run steamboats in its waters; Aaron Ogden held the Elizabethtown--New York City franchise. Thomas Gibbons ran rival steamboats licensed under the federal Coasting Act of 1793, and Ogden sued to enjoin him.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Chancellor Kent enjoined Gibbons; New York's highest court affirmed.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed, 6--0 (Marshall for the Court; Johnson concurring in the judgment; Thompson not participating).

\textbf{Holding.} Commerce includes navigation. Commerce ``among the several States'' reaches into a state's interior, but not commerce ``completely internal'' to one state. The power is plenary, and the federal coasting license preempts the monopoly under the Supremacy Clause.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Rejecting strict construction, Marshall defined commerce as ``commercial intercourse'' in all its branches and ``among'' as ``intermingled with.'' State inspection and health laws rest on the police power. Declining to decide Webster's exclusivity theory, he held that the license authorized Gibbons's trade and voided the conflicting monopoly. Johnson would have held the commerce power exclusive even without the Coasting Act.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a state river-ferry monopoly against a federal licensee. \emph{Opposite-Side:} local livestock inspection laws. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} an intrastate ferry carrying out-of-state travelers.

\textbf{Critique.} Originalists debate how broadly ``commerce'' was understood; Norman Williams faults Marshall's statutory ``hedge'' for confusing the dormant commerce question until \textit{Cooley}.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``It is the power to regulate; that is, to prescribe the rule by which commerce is to be governed. This power, like all others vested in Congress, is complete in itself, may be exercised to its utmost extent, and acknowledges no limitations, other than are prescribed in the Constitution.'' --- Chief Justice Marshall, 22 U.S.\ at 196.
