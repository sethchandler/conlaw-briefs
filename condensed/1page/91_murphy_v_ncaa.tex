\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Murphy v.\ National Collegiate Athletic Association}, 584 U.S.\ 453 (2018)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Forbidding States to repeal their sports-gambling bans is commandeering; PASPA fell entirely (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/584/16-476/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} PASPA made it unlawful for States to ``authorize'' or ``license'' sports gambling. New Jersey repealed its bans for Atlantic City casinos and racetracks, and the NCAA and professional leagues sued.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The en banc Third Circuit, 9--3, held PASPA valid because it required no affirmative state action.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed 7--2 (commandeering) and 6--3 (severability): Alito for the Court; Thomas concurred; Breyer concurred in part; Ginsburg, with Sotomayor, dissented.

\textbf{Holding.} Congress cannot issue direct orders to state legislatures, whether commands or prohibitions; valid preemption regulates private actors. PASPA's anti-authorization provision is unconstitutional and the rest is not severable.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The anti-commandeering rule protects liberty, political accountability, and state budgets, and the line between commands and prohibitions is ``empty.'' Breyer would have saved the ban on private schemes; Ginsburg said the Court used ``an ax'' instead of a scalpel.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a federal ban on repealing state cannabis penalties. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a federal crime of running a sportsbook. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a federal drone ``right to operate'' preempting state zoning.

\textbf{Critique.} Critics say anti-commandeering lacks textual grounding and that striking all of PASPA was a policy choice.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``The basic principle --- that Congress cannot issue direct orders to state legislatures --- applies in either event.'' --- Justice Alito, for the Court.
