\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Missouri v. Holland}, 252 U.S. 416 (1920)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Congress may legislate to execute a valid treaty even beyond its Article~I powers, without violating the Tenth Amendment (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/252/416/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} After a 1913 federal bird law was struck down, the 1916 Migratory Bird Treaty with Great Britain (for Canada) and the 1918 implementing Act protected endangered migratory birds.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Missouri sued to enjoin a federal game warden; the district court dismissed, upholding the Act.

\textbf{Votes.} Affirmed, 7--2 (Holmes for the Court; Van Devanter and Pitney dissenting without opinion).

\textbf{Holding.} The treaty validly exercises the delegated treaty power, and the Act is a necessary and proper means of executing it, whatever Congress could do alone.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The Tenth Amendment reserves only undelegated powers, and treaties are supreme when made under the authority of the United States. Since no ``prohibitory words'' barred the treaty, only ``some invisible radiation'' from the Tenth Amendment could, and it did not. Missouri's title claim leaned on ``a slender reed,'' since birds that today are here may tomorrow be a thousand miles away, and saving migrating birds required national action with another sovereign. \emph{Later:} the \textit{Bond} (2014) concurrences questioned \textit{Holland}.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} protecting a migrating butterfly by treaty. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a treaty ban on insulting religion. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a treaty ban on school corporal punishment.

\textbf{Critique.} Faulted for textual slippage and no stopping point against pretextual treaties.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``It was enough for them to realize or to hope that they had created an organism; it has taken a century and has cost their successors much sweat and blood to prove that they created a nation. The case before us must be considered in the light of our whole experience, and not merely in that of what was said a hundred years ago.'' --- Justice Holmes, 252 U.S. at 433.
