\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Kennedy v. Bremerton School District}, 597 U.S. 507 (2022)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The praying-coach case that buried \textit{Lemon} (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/21-418}{597 U.S. 507}).

\textbf{Facts.} Coach Joseph Kennedy knelt at midfield after Bremerton High School football games to pray; players joined, and he gave religious talks. Told to keep on-duty religious expression ``nondemonstrative,'' he stopped the team prayers but, after publicizing his plans, prayed alone after three games. The District put him on leave while conceding no evidence of direct coercion.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Summary judgment for the District; the Ninth Circuit affirmed.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed, 6--3. Gorsuch wrote for the Court; Thomas and Alito concurred; Sotomayor dissented, joined by Breyer and Kagan.

\textbf{Holding.} A public employee's brief, quiet, personal prayer during a lull is protected by the Free Exercise and Free Speech Clauses, and the Establishment Clause, read by history rather than \textit{Lemon}, does not justify suppressing it absent coercion.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The policy targeted religion and was not generally applicable; the prayers were private speech, not part of Kennedy's duties; and no student was coerced, unlike the captive audiences of \textit{Lee} and \textit{Santa Fe}. Sotomayor said the majority ``misconstrues the facts'' and ignores coaches' subtle power over students.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a teacher's silent lunchtime prayer. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a principal's intercom prayer. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a silent prayer students join just before a mandatory huddle.

\textbf{Critique.} Supporters praise the end of \textit{Lemon}; critics call the ruling ``a sledgehammer to the bedrock of nonestablishment.''

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``In place of \textit{Lemon} and the endorsement test, this Court has instructed that the Establishment Clause must be interpreted by `reference to historical practices and understandings.' '' --- Justice Gorsuch, Opinion of the Court, 597 U.S. 507.
