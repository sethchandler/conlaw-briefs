\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Timbs v.\ Indiana}, 586 U.S.\ 146 (2019)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The Excessive Fines Clause binds the States, even in civil forfeitures (\href{https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4591916/timbs-v-indiana/}{CourtListener}).

\textbf{Facts.} Tyson Timbs used his \$42,000 Land Rover to sell small amounts of heroin. After his guilty plea, Indiana sought civil forfeiture of the vehicle, worth over four times the \$10,000 maximum fine.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The trial court found forfeiture ``grossly disproportionate''; the Indiana Supreme Court reversed, holding the Clause unincorporated.

\textbf{Votes.} Vacated, 9--0: Ginsburg for the Court; Gorsuch concurred; Thomas concurred in the judgment.

\textbf{Holding.} The Excessive Fines Clause is incorporated and applies to the States as to the federal government, including to partly punitive civil forfeitures.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The protection is deeply rooted, from Magna Carta to all 50 state constitutions, and Black Code fines and the revenue lure of fines show its importance. Incorporation turns on the right itself, not each application. Thomas and Gorsuch pointed to the Privileges or Immunities Clause instead.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} forfeiting a home for a minor offense. \emph{Opposite-Side:} forfeiting drug proceeds. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a fine that ruins an indigent defendant.

\textbf{Critique.} Critics fear the ruling is symbolic without an ability-to-pay test.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Incorporated Bill of Rights guarantees are `enforced against the States under the Fourteenth Amendment according to the same standards that protect those personal rights against federal encroachment.' Thus, if a Bill of Rights protection is incorporated, there is no daylight between the federal and state conduct it prohibits or requires.'' --- Justice Ginsburg, 586 U.S.\ at 150.
