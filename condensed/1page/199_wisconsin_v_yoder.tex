\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Wisconsin v. Yoder}, 406 U.S. 205 (1972)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The Amish schooling case: strict scrutiny protects religious parents from compulsory high school (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/406/205/}{406 U.S. 205}).

\textbf{Facts.} Amish parents Jonas Yoder, Wallace Miller, and Adin Yutzy withdrew their 14- and 15-year-olds after eighth grade, violating Wisconsin's attendance-until-16 law; experts said high school threatened Amish survival.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Convicted and fined \$5 each; the Wisconsin Supreme Court reversed.

\textbf{Votes.} Affirmed, 6--1: Burger for the Court; Stewart and White concurred; Douglas dissented in part.

\textbf{Holding.} Only interests ``of the highest order'' can overbalance Free Exercise claims; Wisconsin could not compel Amish children's attendance past eighth grade.

\textbf{Reasoning.} A secular way of life like Thoreau's would not qualify, but the Amish life is ``one of deep religious conviction.'' Belief and action ``cannot be neatly confined in logic-tight compartments,'' so facial neutrality is no defense. The Amish were law-abiding and self-sufficient, and the State's fears were ``highly speculative''; \textit{Prince v. Massachusetts} did not reach this record, and \textit{Pierce} ``stands as a charter of the rights of parents to direct the religious upbringing of their children.'' Douglas would hear the children; White called the case ``close.''

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a new attendance-until-18 law. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a secular commune. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} school vaccine rules with only medical exemptions.

\textbf{Critique.} Reliance on the Amish pedigree and crime rate risks favoring established faiths, excluding secular conscience strains Establishment Clause neutrality, and \textit{Mahmoud v. Taylor} (2025) expanded its reach.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``The essence of all that has been said and written on the subject is that only those interests of the highest order and those not otherwise served can overbalance legitimate claims to the free exercise of religion.'' --- Chief Justice Burger, Opinion of the Court, 406 U.S. at 215.
