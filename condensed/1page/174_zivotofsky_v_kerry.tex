\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Zivotofsky v.\ Kerry}, 576 U.S.\ 1 (2015)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Only the President recognizes foreign sovereigns; Congress could not require ``Israel'' on Jerusalem-born citizens' passports (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/576/1/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} Presidents since 1948 have not recognized any nation's sovereignty over Jerusalem, so passports list ``Jerusalem.'' Section 214(d) required ``Israel'' on request; Zivotofsky's parents were refused.

\textbf{Procedural History.} After \textit{Zivotofsky I} found the case justiciable, the D.C.\ Circuit struck \S~214(d).

\textbf{Votes.} Affirmed: Kennedy for five; Thomas concurred in part; Roberts and Scalia, with Alito, dissented.

\textbf{Holding.} The recognition power is exclusive, and Congress cannot make the Executive contradict it in passports.

\textbf{Reasoning.} In \textit{Youngstown} Category Three, the Reception Clause, the President's diplomatic role, the need for one voice, and historical acquiescence establish exclusivity; the Court rejected \textit{Curtiss-Wright}'s ``sole organ'' dicta. Thomas relied on the Vesting Clause; Roberts denied that a passport entry is recognition; Scalia called the leap from acts to statements ``worthy of the Mad Hatter.''

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} compelled official maps. \emph{Opposite-Side:} trade sanctions. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} customs labels.

\textbf{Critique.} The reach of control over ``official executive statements'' is unclear.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``This case is confined solely to the exclusive power of the President to control recognition determinations, including formal statements by the Executive Branch acknowledging the legitimacy of a state or government and its territorial bounds. Congress cannot command the President to contradict an earlier recognition determination in the issuance of passports.'' --- Justice Kennedy, 576 U.S.\ at 32.
