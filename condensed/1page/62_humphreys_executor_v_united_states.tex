\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Humphrey's Executor v.\ United States}, 295 U.S.\ 602 (1935)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} FTC Commissioners could be protected from at-will removal because their duties were ``quasi-legislative'' and ``quasi-judicial''; overruled by \textit{Trump v.\ Slaughter} (2026) (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/295/602/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} The FTC Act allowed removal of commissioners only ``for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office.'' President Roosevelt removed Commissioner William Humphrey in 1933 over policy differences, citing no statutory cause.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Humphrey's executor sued for back salary; the Court of Claims certified two questions.

\textbf{Votes.} Unanimous: Sutherland for the Court; McReynolds concurred by separate statement.

\textbf{Holding.} The Act barred removal except for the listed causes, and the limit was valid. Whether removal may be restricted depends on ``the character of the office''; \textit{Myers} reaches only purely executive officers.

\textbf{Reasoning.} \textit{Myers} involved a postmaster. The FTC carried out legislative policies and adjudicated; it was no ``arm or an eye of the executive.'' Unlimited removal power would let the President coerce the other branches.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a quasi-judicial claims commission (\textit{Wiener}). \emph{Opposite-Side:} a single-director enforcement agency (\textit{Seila Law}). \emph{Fence-Sitter:} the Federal Reserve (\textit{Trump v.\ Cook}).

\textbf{Critique.} Unitary-executive critics said all executive power belongs to the President; their view prevailed in \textit{Slaughter}.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``[O]ne who holds his office only during the pleasure of another cannot be depended upon to maintain an attitude of independence against the latter's will.'' --- Justice Sutherland, 295 U.S.\ at 629.
