\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Dames \& Moore v. Regan}, 453 U.S. 654 (1981)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The President may suspend private claims against Iran to end the Hostage Crisis, resting on IEEPA and congressional acquiescence (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/453/654/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} After the 1979 embassy seizure, Iranian assets were frozen under IEEPA, and creditors like Dames \& Moore obtained revocable attachments. The Algiers Accords freed the hostages and required nullifying attachments, transferring assets, and sending claims to an international tribunal; executive orders implemented them.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The district court dismissed Dames \& Moore's challenge; certiorari before judgment.

\textbf{Votes.} Affirmed: Rehnquist for the Court; Stevens and Powell concurred in part (Powell also dissenting in part).

\textbf{Holding.} IEEPA authorized nullifying attachments and transferring assets; congressional acquiescence in executive claims settlement supported suspending claims; a Tucker Act remedy remains.

\textbf{Reasoning.} \textit{Youngstown}'s categories form a spectrum. Neither IEEPA nor the Hostage Act covered suits, but their ``general tenor'' and a long, acquiesced-in practice did; suspension changed the law rather than stripping jurisdiction. The Court disclaimed ``plenary power'' to settle claims. Powell would not foreclose takings claims for nullified attachments.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} an executive claims agreement preempting state law (\textit{Garamendi}). \emph{Opposite-Side:} a presidential directive to state courts (\textit{Medell\'in}). \emph{Fence-Sitter:} IEEPA emergency tariffs.

\textbf{Critique.} The Court turned statutory silence into authority and treated congressional inaction as consent.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Past practice does not, by itself, create power, but `long-continued practice, known to and acquiesced in by Congress, would raise a presumption that the [action] had been [taken] in pursuance of its consent. . . .'\,'' --- Justice Rehnquist, 453 U.S. at 686.
