\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Taylor v. United States}, 579 U.S. 301 (2016)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Robbing a drug dealer of drugs or proceeds satisfies the Hobbs Act's commerce element as a matter of law (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/14-6166}{Cornell LII}).

\textbf{Facts.} David Taylor joined two 2009 Roanoke home invasions targeting marijuana dealers, leaving with jewelry, \$40, and cell phones; he was charged with Hobbs Act robbery.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Barred from showing the dealers sold only local marijuana, Taylor was convicted and sentenced to 28 years; the Fourth Circuit affirmed.

\textbf{Votes.} Affirmed, 7--1 (Alito for the Court; Thomas dissenting).

\textbf{Holding.} The government need not prove the drugs crossed state lines; proof that the defendant knowingly stole or tried to steal drugs or proceeds suffices, because under \textit{Raich} the drug market is commerce Congress may regulate. The holding is limited to robberies targeting drug dealers.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The Hobbs Act is ``unmistakably broad,'' and \textit{Raich} held local drug activity part of a regulable class. The jury finds the facts, but what counts as commerce is a question of law. \emph{Separately:} Thomas argued that robbery is not commerce, that the substantial-effects test has ``no stopping point,'' and that the ruling expands \textit{Raich} and dilutes proof beyond a reasonable doubt.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} robbing a local methamphetamine dealer. \emph{Opposite-Side:} burglarizing a home with no drug link. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} robbing a state-licensed cannabis dispensary.

\textbf{Critique.} Faulted for federalizing local crime and for a 28-year sentence for a ``bit of a tagalong.''

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Rather, to satisfy the Act's commerce element, it is enough that a defendant knowingly stole or attempted to steal drugs or drug proceeds, for, as a matter of law, the market for illegal drugs is `commerce over which the United States has jurisdiction.' '' --- Justice Alito, 579 U.S. at 309.
