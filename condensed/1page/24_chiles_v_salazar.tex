{\Large \textit{Chiles v. Salazar}, 607 U.S. \_\_\_ (2026)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} A ban on conversion talk therapy for minors is viewpoint discrimination subject to strict scrutiny (\href{https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/24-539_fd9g.pdf}{Slip Op.}).

\textbf{Facts.} Licensed counselor Kaley Chiles offers only talk therapy. Colorado's 2019 law bars efforts to change a minor's orientation or gender identity but permits ``[a]cceptance, support, and understanding'' and help with transition. She challenged it as applied to her talk therapy.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The district court denied a preliminary injunction under rational-basis review; the Tenth Circuit affirmed, 2--1.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed and remanded, 8--1 (Gorsuch for the Court; Kagan, with Sotomayor, concurring; Jackson dissenting).

\textbf{Holding.} As applied to pure talk therapy, the law regulates speech by viewpoint, not conduct, and triggers strict scrutiny; relabeling speech as ``treatment'' does not avoid it. The Court remanded for application of strict scrutiny.

\textbf{Reasoning.} ``[A]ll Ms. Chiles does is speak.'' The law lets her affirm but not help a minor pursue change. Others may give the same advice, so the speech-incident-to-conduct exception fails, and no historic tradition exempts ``substandard care''; professional consensus cannot become enforced orthodoxy. \emph{Separately:} Kagan reserved viewpoint-neutral healthcare rules; Jackson argued States regulate clinical treatment, where ``we insist upon competence, not debate.''

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a mirror-image ban on affirming talk therapy. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a ban on electric shocks or nausea-inducing drugs. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a viewpoint-neutral ban on steering toward any predetermined outcome.

\textbf{Critique.} Faulted as clinically incoherent in separating speech from procedure; praised for refusing to let ``medical consensus'' enforce orthodoxy.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Her speech does not become conduct just because the State may call it that. Nor does her speech become conduct just because it can also be described as a `treatment,' a `therapeutic modality,' or anything else. The First Amendment is no word game.'' --- Justice Gorsuch, slip op. at 12--13.
