\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Michael H. v. Gerald D.}, 491 U.S. 110 (1989)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The marital-presumption case and Footnote 6's ``most specific level'' of tradition (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/491/110/}{491 U.S. 110}).

\textbf{Facts.} Victoria D. was born to Carole D., married to Gerald D.; blood tests showed a 98.07\% probability that her lover, Michael H., was the father. Michael lived with and supported Victoria at times, but Carole returned to Gerald. California conclusively presumed a cohabiting wife's child to be the husband's.

\textbf{Procedural History.} California courts granted Gerald summary judgment and rejected Michael's constitutional claims.

\textbf{Votes.} Affirmed, 5--4, without a majority opinion; Scalia wrote the plurality, Stevens concurred in the judgment, and Brennan and White dissented.

\textbf{Holding.} A State may bar a biological father from establishing paternity of a child born into an intact, cohabiting marriage whose spouses wish to raise the child.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Scalia required a liberty interest ``traditionally protected by our society'' and found that tradition protected the marital family against an adulterous father; Footnote 6, joined only by Rehnquist, would define rights at the most specific tradition, which O'Connor and Kennedy rejected. Stevens found discretionary visitation adequate; the dissenters saw a liberty interest in biology plus relationship.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} an affair partner cannot override an intact marriage. \emph{Opposite-Side:} an unwed father of an unmarried mother's children is protected by \textit{Stanley}. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a known donor later cut off by a married couple.

\textbf{Critique.} Tribe and Dorf showed that choosing a level of generality is never value-neutral.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``As we view them, they rest not upon such isolated factors but upon the historic respect --- indeed, sanctity would not be too strong a term --- traditionally accorded to the relationships that develop within the unitary family.'' --- Justice Scalia, plurality opinion, 491 U.S. at 123.
