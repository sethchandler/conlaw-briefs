\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Ex parte Milligan}, 71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2 (1866)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The Civil War military-commission case: no military trials of civilians where courts are open (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/71/2/}{71 U.S. (4 Wall.) 2}).

\textbf{Facts.} Lambdin Milligan, an Indiana civilian, was sentenced to hang by a military commission for aiding a Confederate-sympathizing plot, though the federal court in Indianapolis was open and its grand jury had not indicted him.

\textbf{Procedural History.} His habeas petition divided the Circuit Court, which certified questions to the Supreme Court.

\textbf{Votes.} 9--0 for release; 5--4 on Congress's power. Davis wrote for the Court; Chase concurred in the judgment.

\textbf{Holding.} The commission lacked jurisdiction, and neither the President nor Congress may try civilians by military commission where the civil courts are open and unobstructed.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The Constitution governs in war as in peace; only the privilege of habeas corpus may be suspended, and suspension permits detention, not military trial. Civilians are guaranteed indictment and jury trial, and martial rule is confined to places where war has closed the courts. Chase would let Congress authorize commissions where courts are open but ``wholly incompetent.''

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a presidential tribunal for a rioter. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a citizen saboteur enlisted with the enemy (\textit{Quirin}). \emph{Fence-Sitter:} commissions where threats paralyze an open court.

\textbf{Critique.} The Court spoke only after the war; \textit{Quirin} later blurred its line.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``The Constitution of the United States is a law for rulers and people, equally in war and in peace, and covers with the shield of its protection all classes of men, at all times and under all circumstances. No doctrine involving more pernicious consequences was ever invented by the wit of man than that any of its provisions can be suspended during any of the great exigencies of government.'' --- Justice Davis, Opinion of the Court, 71 U.S. at 120--21.
