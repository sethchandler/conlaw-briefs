\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Dred Scott v. Sandford}, 60 U.S. 393 (1857)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Black Americans could not be U.S. citizens, and the Missouri Compromise was void; the Reconstruction Amendments repudiated the decision (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/60/393/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} Dred Scott's enslaver took him to free Illinois and to Fort Snelling, where the Missouri Compromise barred slavery. Back in Missouri, Scott sued for his family's freedom.

\textbf{Procedural History.} A St.\ Louis jury freed Scott, but Missouri's high court reversed; his federal diversity suit against John Sanford of New York failed before a jury.

\textbf{Votes.} 7--2 against Scott; all nine wrote (Taney for the Court; McLean and Curtis dissenting).

\textbf{Holding.} Descendants of enslaved Africans, free or not, cannot be ``citizens'' able to sue in diversity; the Missouri Compromise violated slaveholders' Fifth Amendment due process rights; and Missouri law governed Scott's status on return.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Taney read Founding-era opinion to exclude Black people from ``We the People,'' confined the Territory Clause to lands held in 1787, and held that excluding slave property from a territory denied due process. \emph{Separately:} Nelson relied on Missouri law alone; Curtis, dissenting, showed free Black men voted in five States at ratification and called the territorial ruling extrajudicial; McLean called slavery ``a mere municipal regulation.''

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a free Black merchant's diversity suit in 1858. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a modern denial of birthright citizenship. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a slaveholder in transit through New York.

\textbf{Critique.} The anticanon's ``greatest self-inflicted wound,'' resting on history Curtis refuted.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``They had for more than a century before been regarded as beings of an inferior order, and altogether unfit to associate with the white race, either in social or political relations; and so far inferior, that they had no rights which the white man was bound to respect; and that the negro might justly and lawfully be reduced to slavery for his benefit.'' --- Chief Justice Taney, Opinion of the Court.
