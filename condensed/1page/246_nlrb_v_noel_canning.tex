\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{NLRB v. Noel Canning}, 573 U.S. 513 (2014)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The Senate is in session when it says it is; recesses under 10 days are presumptively too short (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/12-1281}{Cornell LII}).

\textbf{Facts.} During a three-day gap between Senate ``pro forma session[s],'' President Obama recess-appointed three NLRB members. Noel Canning, a Pepsi-Cola bottler that lost before the Board, argued that the Board lacked a lawful quorum. The Senate's resolution provided for ``no business . . . transacted'' at those sessions, yet it passed a payroll-tax extension during one of them.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The D.C. Circuit vacated the Board's order on narrow textual grounds; the Supreme Court affirmed on different grounds.

\textbf{Votes.} 9--0 in judgment, 5--4 on scope: Breyer for the Court; Scalia, with Roberts, Thomas, and Alito, concurred in the judgment.

\textbf{Holding.} The Clause covers intra-session recesses and pre-existing vacancies, but not breaks of three days or fewer, and breaks under 10 days are presumptively too short. Pro forma sessions count, so the appointments were invalid.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Where text is ambiguous, two centuries of practice deserve ``great weight''; the Adjournments Clause supplies the short-break line; and courts accept the Senate's own record while it retains the capacity to act. Scalia read ``the Recess'' as only the inter-session break and attacked the majority's ``adverse possession'' theory.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a three-day gap between pro forma sessions. \emph{Opposite-Side:} an 18-day summer adjournment. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a catastrophe during a nine-day recess.

\textbf{Critique.} Functionalist on recesses yet formalist on pro forma sessions; no ``10 days'' in the text.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``We hold that, for purposes of the Recess Appointments Clause, the Senate is in session when it says it is, provided that, under its own rules, it retains the capacity to transact Senate business.'' --- Justice Breyer, 573 U.S. at 550.
