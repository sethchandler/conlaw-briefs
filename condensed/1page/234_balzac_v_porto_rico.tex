\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Balzac v. Porto Rico}, 258 U.S. 298 (1922)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} U.S. citizenship did not bring the jury right to unincorporated Puerto Rico; locality controls (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/258/298/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} After the 1917 Jones Act made Puerto Ricans citizens, editor Jesus M. Balzac published articles attacking the Governor and was charged with misdemeanor criminal libel, which local law tried without a jury.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Convicted without a jury over his Sixth Amendment demand; the Supreme Court of Porto Rico affirmed.

\textbf{Votes.} Affirmed, 9--0 (Taft for the Court; Holmes concurring in the result).

\textbf{Holding.} The Jones Act did not incorporate Porto Rico; only fundamental rights apply of their own force in unincorporated territories, the jury right is not one, and locality, not citizenship, governs.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Justice White's \textit{Downes} framework is ``the settled law of the court.'' Incorporation needs an express declaration, and the Jones Act's bill of rights omitted juries. Citizenship gave diplomatic protection and mainland rights, not island juries. Unlike Alaska, Porto Rico was distant and rooted in civil law, so residents could choose when to adopt juries. \emph{Separately:} Holmes concurred without opinion.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a mainland-born citizen tried in Porto Rico. \emph{Opposite-Side:} Fourth Amendment protection in a territory (\textit{Torres}). \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a felony bench trial in a territory today.

\textbf{Critique.} Textually baseless and stranded by \textit{Duncan v. Louisiana}'s holding that the jury right is fundamental.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``It is locality that is determinative of the application of the Constitution, in such matters as judicial procedure, and not the status of the people who live in it.'' --- Chief Justice Taft, 258 U.S. at 309.
