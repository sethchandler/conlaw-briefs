\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Craig v. Boren}, 429 U.S. 190 (1976)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Oklahoma's sex-based 3.2\% beer age fell, and sex classifications got intermediate scrutiny (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/429/190/}{429 U.S. 190}).

\textbf{Facts.} Oklahoma barred selling 3.2\% beer to men under 21 but women only under 18, and barred only sale, not drinking. Curtis Craig and vendor Carolyn Whitener sued; the State cited drunk-driving statistics and the Twenty-first Amendment.

\textbf{Procedural History.} A three-judge district court upheld the law; Craig's claim became moot when he turned 21.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed, 7--2: Brennan for the Court; Powell, Stevens, Blackmun, and Stewart concurring; Burger and Rehnquist dissenting.

\textbf{Holding.} The vendor had third-party standing; sex classifications must serve important objectives and be substantially related to them, and this one was not. The Twenty-first Amendment does not dilute equal protection.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Arrest data (2\% of young men, 0.18\% of young women) were an ``unduly tenuous `fit,''' flawed, and the law was easily evaded. Powell preferred \textit{Reed}'s test; Stevens rejected tiers. Rehnquist said the test came ``out of thin air'' and the relative disparity made the law rational.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} sex-based motorcycle license ages. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a uniform drinking age of 21. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} higher auto premiums for young men based on actuarial data.

\textbf{Critique.} The absolute-rate argument proved too much, and feminists fault a middle tier that leaves women weaker protection than strict scrutiny.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``To withstand constitutional challenge, previous cases establish that classifications by gender must serve important governmental objectives and must be substantially related to achievement of those objectives.'' --- Justice Brennan, 429 U.S. at 197.
