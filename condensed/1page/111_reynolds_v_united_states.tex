\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Reynolds v. United States}, 98 U.S. 145 (1879)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} Free exercise protects belief, not conduct; religious duty is no defense to a federal bigamy law (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/98/145/}{Justia}).

\textbf{Facts.} George Reynolds, a Latter-day Saint in Utah Territory, took a second wife as a religious duty, violating the Morrill Anti-Bigamy Act of 1862. The judge refused a religious-duty acquittal instruction.

\textbf{Procedural History.} Convicted and sentenced to two years at hard labor; the territorial supreme court affirmed.

\textbf{Votes.} Affirmed, 9--0 on free exercise, 8--1 on confrontation (Waite for the Court; Field dissenting in part); ``at hard labor'' later struck.

\textbf{Holding.} Congress may not regulate religious belief but may punish actions that violate social duties or subvert good order; religious duty neither exempts a defendant from a general criminal law nor negates intent.

\textbf{Reasoning.} Relying on Jefferson's Danbury letter that legislative powers ``reach actions only, and not opinions,'' Waite drew the belief-action line. Polygamy had long been criminal, marriage underlies social order, and no one could claim a religious right to human sacrifice. A defendant who procures a witness's absence forfeits his confrontation objection. \emph{Separately:} Field found no foundation for admitting the absent witness's testimony.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} sacramental drug use (\textit{Smith}). \emph{Opposite-Side:} a law punishing belief in plural marriage. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} Amish schooling (\textit{Yoder}).

\textbf{Critique.} Faulted for elevating Jefferson over Madison and for anti-Mormon bias.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``Can a man excuse his practices to the contrary because of his religious belief? To permit this would be to make the professed doctrines of religious belief superior to the law of the land, and, in effect, to permit every citizen to become a law unto himself.'' --- Chief Justice Waite, 98 U.S. at 166--167.
