\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{Employment Division v. Smith}, 494 U.S. 872 (1990)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The peyote case: no free exercise exemption from neutral, generally applicable laws (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/494/872/}{494 U.S. 872}).

\textbf{Facts.} Two Oregon drug counselors were fired for ingesting peyote at a Native American Church ceremony and denied unemployment benefits for ``misconduct''; Oregon law made peyote possession a felony.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The Oregon Supreme Court held that the criminal ban violated the Free Exercise Clause and awarded benefits; the Supreme Court granted certiorari a second time.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed, 6--3 (5--4 on the standard). Scalia wrote for the Court; O'Connor concurred in the judgment; Blackmun, Brennan, and Marshall dissented.

\textbf{Holding.} The Free Exercise Clause does not excuse compliance with a ``valid and neutral law of general applicability''; \textit{Sherbert}'s compelling-interest test does not apply, and exemptions are for the political process.

\textbf{Reasoning.} An incidental burden from a neutral law does not prohibit'' free exercise; \textit{Yoder} and \textit{Cantwell} were hybrid cases, and \textit{Sherbert} covered only individualized assessments. Strict scrutiny for every objection would invite anarchy and require judges to weigh religious centrality. O'Connor kept strict scrutiny but upheld the ban; Blackmun found Oregon's interest merely symbolic.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} a vaccination mandate with only medical exemptions. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a discretionary-exception foster-care contract, as in \textit{Fulton}. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a hybrid'' free exercise and compelled-speech claim.

\textbf{Critique.} The bipartisan backlash produced RFRA, and critics call the hybrid-rights and individual-exemptions distinctions unprincipled and unstable.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``We have never held that an individual's religious beliefs excuse him from compliance with an otherwise valid law prohibiting conduct that the State is free to regulate.'' --- Justice Scalia, Opinion of the Court, 494 U.S. at 878--879.
