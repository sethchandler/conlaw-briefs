\begin{center}
{\Large \textit{United States v. Carolene Products Co.}, 304 U.S. 144 (1938)\par}
\vspace{0.3em}
{\normalsize Case Brief --- One-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\textbf{Memory Jogger.} The ``filled milk'' case: rational-basis review for economic regulation, plus Footnote Four's blueprint for heightened scrutiny (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/304/144/}{304 U.S. 144}).

\textbf{Facts.} The Filled Milk Act of 1923 banned interstate shipment of skimmed milk blended with non-milk fats, declaring it injurious to health and a fraud on the public. Carolene Products was indicted for shipping ``Milnut,'' a mix of condensed skimmed milk and coconut oil.

\textbf{Procedural History.} The district court dismissed the indictment on demurrer; the United States appealed directly.

\textbf{Votes.} Reversed, 6--1. Stone wrote for the Court; Black joined all but Part Third, so Footnote Four drew only four votes; Butler concurred in the result; McReynolds dissented.

\textbf{Holding.} The Act is valid on its face. Congress may exclude articles it reasonably deems harmful from interstate commerce, and economic regulation is presumed constitutional unless the known facts preclude any rational basis. The Fifth Amendment has no Equal Protection Clause, and Congress need not ban all similar products (such as oleomargarine) at once.

\textbf{Reasoning.} The commerce power is plenary and does not offend the Tenth Amendment; the congressional record of nutritional harm and consumer confusion made the legislative judgment ``at least debatable,'' which is enough. \emph{Footnote Four} suggested ``narrower scope'' for the presumption when a law falls within a specific constitutional prohibition, restricts the political processes that repeal bad laws, or reflects prejudice against discrete and insular minorities. Butler would have let the company prove Milnut wholesome at trial.

\textbf{Examples.} \emph{Same-Side:} licensing interior designers is upheld as ordinary economic regulation. \emph{Opposite-Side:} a permit requirement for political leaflets triggers heightened scrutiny. \emph{Fence-Sitter:} a ban on a safe, labeled milk substitute backed only by incumbent farmers.

\textbf{Critique.} Libertarians see dairy-lobby protectionism and judicial abdication; John Hart Ely built representation-reinforcement on Footnote Four, while Bruce Ackerman questioned whether discrete and insular groups are really the least powerful.

\textbf{Key Quotation.} ``A legislature may hit at an abuse which it has found, even though it has failed to strike at another.'' --- Justice Stone, 304 U.S. at 151.
