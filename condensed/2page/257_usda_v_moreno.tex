\begin{center}
{\LARGE \textit{U.S. Department of Agriculture v. Moreno}, 413 U.S. 528 (1973)\par}
\vspace{0.5em}
{\large Case Brief --- Two-Page Edition\par}
\end{center}

\section{Memory Jogger}
Cutting off food stamps to households with unrelated members, aimed at ``hippie communes,'' fails rational-basis review: animus toward an unpopular group is not a legitimate interest (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/413/528/}{Justia}).

\section{Detailed Case Facts}
A 1971 amendment excluded from the food stamp program any household containing a person unrelated to the others; its legislative history targeted ``hippies.'' It cut off poor households like that of Jacinta Moreno, a diabetic who shared a home with an unrelated family to survive.

\section{Procedural History}
A three-judge district court held the exclusion violated equal protection; the Department of Agriculture appealed directly.

\section{Judicial Votes}
Affirmed 7--2. Brennan wrote for the Court; Douglas concurred; Rehnquist, joined by Burger, dissented.

\section{Holding}
The classification is irrelevant to the Act's purposes, rests on an illegitimate purpose, and is ``wholly without any rational basis'' as a fraud measure.

\section{Analysis of Opinions}
\textbf{Majority:} the Act already penalized fraud and refusal to work, abusers could evade the rule by splitting households, and in practice only the most desperate were excluded. \textbf{Douglas:} the rule burdened freedom of association and failed strict scrutiny. \textbf{Rehnquist:} the majority wrote ``a very persuasive congressional committee report''; Congress could rationally fund only related households.

\section{Examples: Future Applications}
\textbf{Same-Side:} a voucher ban on protest misdemeanants adopted amid attacks on ``radical campus agitators.'' \textbf{Opposite-Side:} a neutral zoning cap on unrelated occupants (\textit{Belle Terre}). \textbf{Fence-Sitter:} a vehicle-dwelling ban defended on sanitation grounds amid hostile remarks.

\section{Critique}
\textit{Moreno} applies heightened scrutiny under a rational-basis label, leaving courts unsure when ``bite'' applies, and proving legislative animus from stray remarks is fraught.

\section{Key Quotations}
\begin{quote}
``For if the constitutional conception of `equal protection of the laws' means anything, it must, at the very least, mean that a bare congressional desire to harm a politically unpopular group cannot constitute a legitimate governmental interest.''
\end{quote}
\noindent --- Justice Brennan, for the Court, 413 U.S. at 534.
