## 241_hayburns_case

### Memory Jogger

```latex
When Congress told federal circuit courts to decide veterans' pension claims subject to revision by the Secretary of War and Congress, five Justices on circuit refused, writing that Congress may not assign Article III courts non-judicial duties or make their decisions subject to executive or legislative revision; the Supreme Court itself never reached the merits (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/2/409/}{Justia}).
```

### Judicial Votes

```latex
The Supreme Court issued \textbf{no merits judgment}. It divided \textbf{3--3} and denied Attorney General Randolph's \textit{ex officio} mandamus motion, then held his renewed motion on Hayburn's behalf under advisement until Congress's Act of February 28, 1793, mooted it (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/2/409/}{Justia}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Opinion of the Court:} None; the report is a procedural entry by reporter Alexander Dallas, with a footnote reprinting the circuit-court letters to President Washington (2 U.S.\ at 410--414 n.*).
    \item \textbf{Views on the Merits:} Five of the six sitting Justices---Chief Justice Jay and Justices Cushing, Wilson, Blair, and Iredell---stated their constitutional objections in circuit-court letters: the New York Circuit (Jay, Cushing, and District Judge Duane), the Pennsylvania Circuit (Wilson, Blair, and District Judge Peters), and the North Carolina Circuit (Iredell and District Judge Sitgreaves).
    \item \textbf{Concurring and Dissenting Opinions:} None in the modern sense.
\end{itemize}
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Unconstitutional): Executive Veto of Court Awards.} Congress enacts the ``Cyber-Compensation Act,'' directing federal district courts to adjudicate private claims for federal cyber-attack relief and enter monetary awards, but providing that the Secretary of Homeland Security may ``review and vacate any district court award that the Secretary deems inconsistent with national fiscal priorities.'' Unconstitutional under \textit{Hayburn's Case} and \textit{Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm}. Congress may assign public-benefit claims to an executive agency in the first instance (with subsequent Article III judicial review), but it cannot vest an Article III court with adjudicatory authority while making the court's judgment subject to executive revision or veto.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Unconstitutional): Advisory Rulings on Pending Bills.} To reduce litigation over new laws, Congress directs the D.C. Circuit to review any bill passed by one House, at the request of a committee chair, and to issue a ruling on whether it would be constitutional if enacted. No party is injured, no one is sued, and the ruling binds no one. The duty is not ``properly judicial'' or performed ``in a judicial manner,'' in the words of the New York circuit judges: it asks a court to advise the legislature rather than decide a case between adverse parties. Under the advisory-opinion bar that \textit{Hayburn's Case} founded and \textit{Muskrat v.\ United States} applied, Congress cannot impose this assignment on an Article III court.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Constitutional): Terminating Prospective Injunctions After a Change in Law.} Suppose Congress enacts (a) a statute amending substantive federal environmental law prospectively and directing federal courts to terminate ongoing \textit{prospective} injunctions that no longer satisfy the new statutory standard. Under \href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/99-224}{\textit{Miller v. French}, 530 U.S. 327 (2000)}, modifying or terminating \textit{prospective} equitable decrees based on changed underlying law does not retroactively reopen final money judgments in violation of \textit{Hayburn's Case} or \textit{Plaut}.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Constitutional): Judges Serving Voluntarily on a Commission.} Suppose Congress enacts (b) a statute creating an independent advisory commission on federal judicial ethics on which three federal appellate judges may voluntarily serve in their individual capacities without adjudicating cases. Under \textit{Mistretta} and the New York circuit judges' ``commissioner'' distinction in \textit{Hayburn's Case}, voluntary extrajudicial service by individual judges on a non-adjudicatory commission does not assign non-judicial duties to Article III courts or compromise judicial finality.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): Judgments Payable Only if Congress Appropriates.} Congress allows veterans to sue in federal district court for wrongly denied disability benefits, but provides that any judgment may be paid only from funds Congress appropriates each year for that purpose. After a court enters a final judgment for a veteran, Congress declines to fund that award. On one view, the judgment itself remains final and binding, and Congress has merely exercised its power of the purse, which \textit{Hayburn's Case} did not address. On another, a judgment that Congress can decline to honor case by case is effectively ``liable to a reversion, or even suspension, by the Legislature itself,'' which the North Carolina judges said the Constitution forbids. Where the line falls is unsettled.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 242_flast_v_cohen

### Memory Jogger

```latex
Federal taxpayers challenged federal education grants that funded instruction and textbooks in religious schools; an 8--1 Court, narrowing \textit{Frothingham v.\ Mellon}, held that taxpayers have standing when they attack an exercise of Congress's taxing and spending power as exceeding a specific constitutional limit on that power---here, the Establishment Clause (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/392/83/}{Justia}).
```

### Judicial Votes

```latex
The Supreme Court \textbf{reversed} the three-judge District Court by an \textbf{8--1} vote. The case was argued on March 12, 1968, and decided on June 10, 1968 (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/392/83/}{Justia}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority Opinion:} Chief Justice Warren delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Justices Black, Douglas, Brennan, Stewart, White, Fortas, and Marshall.
    \item \textbf{Concurring Opinions:} Justice Douglas, Justice Stewart, and Justice Fortas each filed a concurring opinion.
    \item \textbf{Dissenting Opinion:} Justice Harlan filed a dissenting opinion.
\end{itemize}
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Standing Exists): Express Appropriation for Sanctuary Repairs.} Congress passes the ``Community Heritage Act'' under Article I, \S\ 8, expressly appropriating \$50 million to the Department of Housing and Urban Development with a statutory directive to distribute direct repair grants to churches and synagogues for sanctuary renovations. A federal income taxpayer with no other connection to the program sues in federal court under the Establishment Clause. \textit{Analysis:} The taxpayer \textit{has standing} under \textit{Flast} and \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/487/589/}{\textit{Bowen v. Kendrick}}. Both nexuses are met: (1) the challenge targets an express congressional spending enactment under Article I, \S\ 8 (not general executive overhead or a regulatory statute), and (2) the challenge invokes the Establishment Clause, the sole recognized specific constitutional limitation on taxing and spending.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Standing Exists): An As-Applied Challenge to a Grant Program Open to Religious Groups.} Congress enacts a spending statute authorizing the Secretary of Health and Human Services to award grants to public and private organizations, expressly including religious organizations, to provide counseling on adolescent health. Taxpayers allege that some religious grantees are using the federal money to teach religious doctrine and sue under the Establishment Clause. Although an agency selects the grantees, the challenge is still to the operation of a congressional spending program under Article I, \S\ 8, and it invokes the Establishment Clause, so both \textit{Flast} nexuses are satisfied. \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/487/589/}{\textit{Bowen v.\ Kendrick}, 487 U.S.\ 589 (1988)}, upheld taxpayer standing for exactly this kind of as-applied challenge (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/392/83/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Standing): Outside \textit{Flast}'s Window.} Suppose instead that (a) a federal taxpayer challenges a \$50 million congressional appropriation funding foreign military aid as violating Article I, \S\ 8's Declare War Clause or the Tenth Amendment, \textit{or} (b) a federal taxpayer brings an Establishment Clause challenge against either a new federal tax credit for donations to religious charities or a cabinet secretary's use of general agency operating funds to host sectarian prayer rallies. \textit{Contrast:} In every one of these scenarios, the suit must be dismissed for lack of Article III standing. Scenario (a) fails \textit{Flast}'s second nexus under \textit{Frothingham}, \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/418/166/}{\textit{Richardson}}, and \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/418/208/}{\textit{Schlesinger}} because no clause other than the Establishment Clause qualifies as a specific spending limitation. Scenario (b) fails \textit{Flast}'s first nexus under \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/563/125/}{\textit{Winn}} (tax credits do not ``extract and spend'' taxpayer funds) and \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/551/587/}{\textit{Hein}} (discretionary executive spending from general appropriations is not an express congressional spending mandate).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Standing): Donating a Surplus Federal Building to a Seminary.} Acting under a statute that lets agencies dispose of surplus federal property, the General Services Administration conveys a closed federal office building, without charge, to a religious seminary. A taxpayers' group sues, claiming the gift violates the Establishment Clause. The claim fails \textit{Flast}'s first nexus twice over: the conveyance is an executive decision rather than a congressional spending enactment, and the authority for it rests on the Property Clause of Article IV, not the Taxing and Spending Clause of Article I, \S\ 8. \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/454/464/}{\textit{Valley Forge Christian College}, 454 U.S.\ 464 (1982)}, denied taxpayer standing on materially identical facts.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Congressional Mandate or Executive Discretion?): A Recovery-Services Appropriation.} Congress appropriates \$20 million for grants to community organizations, including faith-based organizations, to provide addiction-recovery services; the statute does not require that any grantee be religious. The agency awards most of the money to churches whose programs incorporate worship, and taxpayers sue. If the grants are viewed as carrying out Congress's own spending program, \textit{Bowen} supports standing. If they are viewed as the agency's discretionary choices under a general appropriation, the plurality in \textit{Hein} would deny it. And with several Justices having urged that \textit{Flast} be overruled, the result is genuinely uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 243_friends_of_the_earth_v_laidlaw

### Memory Jogger

```latex
Environmental groups sued a polluter under the Clean Water Act for civil penalties; the Court held 7--2 that members who curtailed use of a river had standing even without proof of environmental harm, that penalties deterring ongoing violations provide redress, and that voluntary cessation moots a case only if the defendant shows it is absolutely clear the violations cannot reasonably be expected to recur (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/528/167/}{Justia}).
```

### Judicial Votes

```latex
The Supreme Court \textbf{reversed and remanded} the Fourth Circuit by a \textbf{7--2} vote. The case was argued on October 12, 1999, and decided on January 12, 2000 (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/528/167/}{Justia}; \href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/528/167}{Cornell LII}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority Opinion:} Justice Ginsburg delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices Stevens, O'Connor, Kennedy, Souter, and Breyer.
    \item \textbf{Concurring Opinions:} Justice Stevens filed a concurring opinion. Justice Kennedy filed a concurring opinion.
    \item \textbf{Dissenting Opinion:} Justice Scalia filed a dissenting opinion, joined by Justice Thomas.
\end{itemize}
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Live Case; Not Moot): Post-Filing Compliance by a Chemical Plant.} A state environmental group whose members regularly kayak on Buffalo Bayou sues a chemical plant under CWA \S~505 while the plant is actively exceeding its benzene permit limits, seeking civil penalties payable to the Treasury. Six months into discovery, the plant installs new scrubbers, achieves full compliance, and moves to dismiss as moot. Under \textit{Laidlaw} and \textit{FBI v. Fikre}, the group had standing at filing (curtailed recreational use plus ongoing violations redressable by deterrent civil penalties), and the plant's post-filing compliance does not moot the case unless the plant proves it is ``absolutely clear'' that benzene violations cannot reasonably be expected to recur.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Standing Exists): Birdwatchers Who Stay Away From a Marsh.} A paper mill upstream of a coastal marsh repeatedly exceeds the dioxin limits in its discharge permit. Members of a birding club submit affidavits that they used to visit the marsh monthly but have stopped because they fear the illegal discharges, and one says her lakeside cabin has become harder to rent. The mill shows that scientists have found no measurable harm to the marsh's wildlife. Under \textit{Laidlaw}, the relevant injury is to the plaintiffs, not the environment: a reasonable concern about ongoing unlawful pollution that leads members to give up specific, regular uses of an identified place is concrete injury in fact, unlike the vague some-day intentions in \textit{Lujan}.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Standing): Violations Ended Before Suit.} Suppose the same chemical plant cured all benzene violations and permanently surrendered its discharge permit \textit{before} the environmental group filed suit. Standing is measured when the complaint is filed, and at that moment nothing remained to deter. Civil penalties payable to the Treasury for wholly past violations cannot redress the members' injury, which is why \textit{Steel Co.\ v.\ Citizens for a Better Environment} found no standing. The voluntary-cessation doctrine never comes into play, because it applies only to a case that was live when filed and is later claimed to be moot.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Standing): An Undisclosed Credit-File Error.} Alternatively, suppose a consumer sues a credit bureau for statutory damages over an internal file error that was never disclosed to any third party. The consumer has a statutory cause of action, but under \textit{TransUnion} a bare statutory violation is not enough: the inaccurate file, never sent to anyone, causes no concrete harm with a close historical analogue such as defamation. Unlike the \textit{Laidlaw} members, who changed how they used a real place because of ongoing pollution, the consumer has suffered nothing Article III recognizes, so the suit must be dismissed.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): Closing the Plant but Keeping the Permit.} After a citizen suit is filed over ongoing permit violations, the defendant shuts down its facility, lays off its workers, and lists the property for sale, but keeps its discharge permit in force and says it has no present plans to reopen. That is essentially Laidlaw's own posture, which the Court remanded without deciding. The defendant argues that closure makes recurrence impossible; the plaintiffs answer that a retained permit leaves the defendant free to resume discharges at will, and \textit{Fikre} held that a bare assurance of no present intent does not carry the formidable burden. Whether the closure is truly permanent is a factual question that could go either way.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 244_trump_v_casa

### Memory Jogger

```latex
Challengers to the 2025 birthright-citizenship executive order won nationwide injunctions in three district courts; without reaching the order's legality, the Court held 6--3 that universal injunctions likely exceed federal courts' equitable authority under the Judiciary Act of 1789, limiting relief to what is needed to give each plaintiff with standing complete relief (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/606/24a884/}{Justia}).
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Universal Scope Stayed): Two Plaintiffs Challenge a Wire-Transfer Surcharge.} The President issues an executive order directing the Treasury to impose a 10\% surcharge on all international wire transfers sent by U.S.\ residents. Two individual plaintiffs sue in federal district court, alleging the order violates Article I's Taxing Clause, without moving for Rule 23 class certification or naming any agency rule under the APA. Finding the order patently unconstitutional, the district court enters a universal preliminary injunction barring enforcement nationwide. Under \textit{CASA}, the universal scope must be stayed. Enjoining the Treasury from collecting the surcharge on the two named plaintiffs' transfers affords them complete relief; extending protection to nonparties exceeds the district court's equitable authority under the Judiciary Act of 1789.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Relief Limited to the Plaintiff): A Contractor Challenges a Speech Condition.} An executive order bars all federal contractors from making public statements about a pending trade negotiation. One software contractor sues, alleging a First Amendment violation, and the district court, finding the order likely unconstitutional, enjoins its enforcement against every federal contractor in the country. Under \textit{CASA}, the injunction is broader than necessary: an order barring the Government from enforcing the condition against the plaintiff fully redresses \emph{its} injury, and nothing about the relief is indivisible. The injunction must be narrowed to the plaintiff. Other contractors may file their own suits or seek certification of a Rule 23(b)(2) class (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/606/24a884/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Pathways \textit{CASA} Leaves Open): APA Vacatur and a Certified Class.} Suppose instead that (a) a trade association sues under the Administrative Procedure Act, 5 U.S.C.\ \S\ 706(2), to ``hold unlawful and set aside'' a final Treasury regulation implementing the surcharge, OR (b) the individual plaintiffs satisfy Rule 23(a) and 23(b)(2) and obtain certification of a nationwide class of all U.S.\ senders subject to the surcharge before seeking a classwide injunction. Unlike \textit{CASA}, neither remedy is barred by \textit{CASA}'s holding. Footnote 10 explicitly carved out APA \S\ 706(2) vacatur (which lower courts through 2026 continue to apply universally), and the majority itself identified a properly certified Rule 23(b)(2) class action as the lawful modern descendant of the English bill of peace---provided the district court conducts the ``rigorous analysis'' demanded by \textit{Wal-Mart} and Justice Alito's concurrence.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Indivisible Relief): Noise From a Federal Firing Range.} A homeowner sues the Defense Department, alleging that a new nighttime live-fire training schedule at a neighboring base creates a nuisance and violates federal law. The district court enjoins nighttime firing altogether. The injunction benefits every neighbor of the base, not just the plaintiff, but \textit{CASA} does not require it to be narrowed. Justice Barrett explained that a court may award relief that incidentally benefits nonparties when indivisible relief is needed to redress the plaintiff's own injury, and she used a neighbor's nuisance as her example. There is no way to stop the noise at one house while letting it continue next door, so the scope of the injunction is consistent with the complete-relief principle.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (State Plaintiffs): A Benefits Rule and Cross-Border Students.} A State sues to block an executive order that would bar federal student-aid eligibility for children of certain noncitizens. It argues that an injunction limited to its own residents would not give it complete relief, because students constantly move into the State from elsewhere, and its universities would have to verify each student's eligibility under a patchwork of rules. \textit{CASA} acknowledged that the inquiry for state plaintiffs is ``more complicated'' and remanded for the lower courts to decide whether narrower relief would suffice. Justice Thomas, by contrast, warned that complete relief is a ``ceiling'' and that nonparty-benefiting remedies must remain rare, and Justice Alito flagged lax state standing as a loophole. Whether the State can justify relief extending beyond its borders is genuinely uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 245_medellin_v_texas

### Memory Jogger

```latex
The Court held 6--3 that neither the ICJ's \textit{Avena} judgment nor President Bush's memorandum directing state courts to give it effect was binding domestic law: a non-self-executing treaty obligation becomes enforceable in U.S. courts only through congressional implementing legislation (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/552/491/}{Justia}).
```

### Judicial Votes

```latex
The Supreme Court \textbf{affirmed} the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals by a \textbf{6--3} vote. The case was argued on October 10, 2007, and decided on March 25, 2008 (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/552/491/}{Justia}; \href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/06-984.ZS.html}{Cornell LII}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority Opinion:} Chief Justice Roberts delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Justices Scalia, Kennedy, Thomas, and Alito.
    \item \textbf{Concurring Opinion:} Justice Stevens filed an opinion concurring in the judgment.
    \item \textbf{Dissenting Opinion:} Justice Breyer filed a dissenting opinion, joined by Justices Souter and Ginsburg.
\end{itemize}
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Not Domestic Law): A Presidential Directive Enforcing an International Tribunal's Ruling.} The United States ratifies a multilateral Environmental Disputes Treaty stating that signatory states ``undertake to give effect'' to binding rulings of an International Environmental Tribunal, with noncompliance referred to a diplomatic council. Congress enacts no implementing statute. After the Tribunal rules that a California coastal zoning law violates the treaty, the President issues a Presidential Directive ordering California courts to enjoin enforcement of the zoning law to honor U.S. treaty obligations. Under \textit{Medell\'{i}n}, both the Tribunal's ruling and the Presidential Directive fail to preempt California law. The ``undertake to give effect'' text and diplomatic enforcement mechanism mark the treaty as non-self-executing, and under \textit{Youngstown} Category Three, the President cannot unilaterally transform a non-self-executing treaty into binding domestic law overriding state police-power regulations.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Not Domestic Law): A Human-Rights Treaty Ratified With a Non-Self-Executing Declaration.} The Senate consents to a human-rights treaty guaranteeing fair criminal procedures, but attaches a declaration that the treaty's substantive provisions are not self-executing, and Congress passes no implementing statute. A state prisoner petitions a state court to vacate his conviction, arguing that a state evidentiary rule violates the treaty and is preempted under the Supremacy Clause. Under \textit{Medell\'{i}n}, a treaty is domestic law only if Congress implements it or the treaty itself conveys an intention that it be self-executing and is ratified on those terms. Here the ratifying Senate expressly said the opposite. The treaty binds the United States internationally, but it gives the prisoner no rule of decision that state courts must apply.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Binding Domestic Law): Implementing Legislation or Explicit Self-Executing Text.} Return to the environmental-tribunal hypothetical, but change the legal backdrop. First, if Congress enacts implementing legislation directing federal and state courts to enforce Tribunal judgments (as Congress did for ICSID arbitral awards in 22 U.S.C. \S~1650a), or if the Senate ratifies a treaty whose text explicitly directs that ``domestic courts of each Party shall enforce Tribunal orders as final judgments,'' the obligation becomes supreme federal law binding California courts under Article VI.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Valid Executive Action): A Claims-Settlement Executive Agreement.} Alternatively, if the President resolves a longstanding diplomatic crisis by entering an executive agreement settling commercial civil claims of U.S. citizens against a foreign sovereign within a 200-year tradition of congressional acquiescence, the agreement preempts contrary state civil remedies under \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/453/654/}{\textit{Dames \& Moore v. Regan}} and \textit{Garamendi}.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unresolved): A Damages Suit for Failure to Give Consular Notice.} Police arrest a foreign national and never tell him of his right under Article 36 of the Vienna Convention to have his consulate notified. After his release, he sues the officers for damages under 42 U.S.C.\ \S~1983, claiming they violated rights secured by federal law. Unlike Medell\'{i}n's claim, this suit does not depend on an ICJ judgment or a presidential memorandum; it rests on the Convention itself, which Justices Stevens and Breyer viewed as self-executing. Whether Article 36 also confers individual rights enforceable in a private damages action is a separate question: the Court in \textit{Sanchez-Llamas v.\ Oregon}, 548 U.S.\ 331 (2006), assumed without deciding that it does, and \textit{Medell\'{i}n} did not resolve the point. The outcome therefore remains uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 246_nlrb_v_noel_canning

### Memory Jogger

```latex
The Recess Appointments Clause reaches intra-session as well as inter-session recesses and vacancies that arose before the recess, but a break of fewer than 10 days is presumptively too short, and the Senate is in session when it says it is; so President Obama's January 2012 NLRB appointments, made during a three-day gap between pro forma sessions, were invalid (9--0 in judgment) (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/573/513/}{Justia}).
```

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Deference): Defining a Statutory Term.} Suppose Congress empowers the EPA to set emissions limits for ``emerging synthetic compounds'' and directs the agency to promulgate rules ``as the Administrator deems reasonable and necessary to protect public health.'' \item \textbf{Same-Side (No Deference): Making Regulated Parties Pay for Inspectors.} A statute authorizes an agency to require inspectors aboard cargo barges and expressly allows the agency to charge barge operators for inspectors in two specified programs, but says nothing about charging operators in other programs. The agency issues a rule requiring all operators to pay for inspectors. Before \textit{Loper Bright}, a court might have found the silence ambiguous and upheld the rule as a permissible reading. Now the court must decide for itself the ``single, best meaning'' of the statute, and Congress's express authorization of fees in only two programs strongly suggests that the agency has no such power elsewhere. The agency's view receives at most \textit{Skidmore} respect (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/603/22-451/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Prior Holding Stands): Relitigating a Rule Upheld Under \textit{Chevron}.} In 2010 the Supreme Court upheld a specific agency rule, applying \textit{Chevron} and deferring to the agency's reading of an ambiguous statute. The challenge fails. \textit{Loper Bright} stated that it does not call into question prior cases that relied on \textit{Chevron}: their holdings that specific agency actions are lawful remain subject to statutory \textit{stare decisis}, and mere reliance on \textit{Chevron} is not a ``special justification'' for overruling them.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Uncertain): An Agency Reverses Course.} In 2012, a court of appeals upheld an FDA rule under \textit{Chevron} Step Two. In 2026, a new administration repeals that rule and adopts the opposite statutory interpretation. Under \textit{Loper Bright} (603 U.S. at 412), statutory \textit{stare decisis} protected the 2012 holding that the original rule was lawful---but without \textit{Chevron} and \textit{Brand X}, can the agency defend its new interpretation as merely ``permissible,'' or must the reviewing court now independently determine the single best meaning of the statute? The 2012 decision held only that the old reading was permissible, not that it was the best reading, so it may not settle whether the opposite reading is lawful. If the court concludes the old reading was best, the reversal fails; if the new reading is best, the prior precedent may not stand in its way. \textit{Loper Bright} did not say how such a precedent binds a later, contrary interpretation.

\end{enumerate}
```

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Within Congress's Power): Commitment of Violently Dangerous Federal Prisoners.} Congress amends federal law to allow civil commitment, at the end of a federal sentence, of any prisoner in Bureau of Prisons custody who suffers from a serious mental illness and, as a result, would pose a serious danger of violent conduct if released. Every \textit{Comstock} consideration points toward validity: the statute rationally serves the federal power to imprison and care for federal prisoners, extends a long tradition of federal commitment of dangerous mentally ill prisoners, reflects the government's role as a responsible custodian, accommodates state interests, and is confined to people already in federal custody.

A federal statute allows the Bureau of Prisons, after a hearing, to keep such an inmate in medical isolation beyond the end of his sentence until he is no longer contagious, unless the health authorities of his home State agree to take custody. Under \textit{Comstock}, Congress may act as a responsible custodian of those it lawfully holds, protecting the communities into which a prisoner would be released. The measure is narrow, limited to people already in federal custody, temporary, and deferential to state health authorities, so it is a necessary and proper incident of running federal prisons rather than a general police power.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Beyond Congress's Power): Commitment of State Felons via a Firearm Nexus.} Congress enacts a statute authorizing federal courts to impose lifetime federal civil commitment on any person who has ever been convicted of a \emph{state} violent felony if the person once purchased a firearm that traveled in interstate commerce, even if the person was never in federal custody and the state declines to commit him. Without that custodial link, the statute does not carry any enumerated power into execution; civil commitment of dangerous persons at large is the kind of general police power the Court said remains with the States.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): New Registration Duties for Long-Released Federal Offenders.} Congress requires every person ever convicted of a federal sex offense to register with federal authorities, including people who completed their sentences decades ago and were released without conditions, before any federal registration law existed. Here, no such continuing obligation exists, so the link to the power that supported the original conviction is weaker. ### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)
```

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Unconstitutional): District Residency and a Tax-Return Ballot Label.} Seeking to combat corruption and promote local accountability, State X amends its state constitution to (1) require every U.S. House candidate to reside inside the specific congressional district for at least two years before the election, and (2) print ``REFUSED TAX-RETURN DISCLOSURE'' beside the name of any congressional candidate who declines to release five years of federal tax returns. \textit{Analysis:} Both provisions are \textbf{unconstitutional} under \textit{Thornton} and \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/531/510/}{\textit{Cook v. Article I, \S~2, cl. 2 requires only that a Representative be an inhabitant of the \textit{State} ``when elected''; State X cannot add a durational district-residency qualification. Likewise, printing a pejorative ballot label to coerce tax disclosure is not a neutral ``Manner'' regulation under Article I, \S~4, cl. 1, but an unconstitutional effort to handicap a class of federal candidates and dictate electoral outcomes.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Unconstitutional): Congress Limits Its Own Members' Terms by Statute.} Responding to public pressure, Congress passes a statute providing that no person who has served six terms in the House or two terms in the Senate is eligible for election to that chamber again. A long-serving Representative sues to stay on the ballot. \textit{Thornton} reaffirmed \textit{Powell v.\ McCormack}'s holding that the Qualifications Clauses are exclusive and that Congress itself cannot add to them, and Stevens's opinion explained that any change to those qualifications must come through Article V. A statutory term limit is an added qualification for congressional service, so it is unconstitutional no matter which body enacts it (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/514/779/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Constitutional): State Term Limits, Resign-to-Run, and Neutral Ballot Rules.} Now suppose State X instead (1) imposes a two-term limit on \textit{State X's own Governor and state legislators}, (2) enforces a ``resign-to-run'' rule requiring sitting state judges to resign their state office upon declaring candidacy for Congress, and (3) enacts a uniform ballot-access rule requiring any independent congressional candidate to submit signatures from 2 percent of registered voters in the district by June 1 and prohibiting sore-loser candidates who lost a party primary from appearing on the general-election ballot. All three measures are \textbf{constitutional}. Term limits on \textit{state} officials rest squarely within State X's reserved Tenth Amendment sovereignty; ``resign-to-run'' laws permissibly regulate \textit{state officeholders} rather than imposing a qualification on federal office (which Stevens expressly distinguished in \textit{Thornton}, 514 U.S. at 835 n. 48, citing \textit{Clements v. Fashing}, 457 U.S. 957 (1982)); and neutral signature thresholds and sore-loser rules are valid Elections Clause ``Manner'' regulations designed to prevent voter confusion and ballot overcrowding rather than to exclude a substantive class of federal candidates.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Constitutional): A State Applies for an Article V Convention.} Frustrated by \textit{Thornton}, a state legislature adopts a resolution applying to Congress, under Article V, to call a convention for proposing a constitutional amendment imposing congressional term limits, and urges its congressional delegation to support such an amendment. Opponents argue that the State is again trying to impose term limits it may not adopt. Unlike Arkansas's Amendment 73, the resolution adds no qualification, keeps no candidate off the ballot, and does not handicap any class of candidates. It pursues precisely the route \textit{Thornton} identified as the only legitimate path to congressional term limits: amendment of the Constitution under Article V, in which the Constitution gives state legislatures an express role.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Neutral Information or a Handicap?): A Ballot Notation of Terms Served.} A State requires its ballots to list, beside each congressional incumbent's name, the number of terms the incumbent has served (for example, noting that a Representative is completing a sixth term). The notation is accurate and applies to every incumbent, and the State says it simply informs voters. But the measure was adopted by an initiative whose sponsors openly sought to promote congressional term limits, and the label may be designed to disadvantage long-serving incumbents. Under \textit{Thornton} and \textit{Cook v.\ Gralike}, a ballot rule is invalid if it is meant to handicap a class of candidates and dictate electoral outcomes, but neutral, factual ballot information is a familiar Elections Clause regulation. Whether this notation is the former or the latter is genuinely uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

```latex
A Wisconsin county social-services agency repeatedly documented signs that four-year-old Joshua DeShaney was being abused by his father but never removed him; the Court held 6--3 that the Due Process Clause imposes no duty to protect against private violence unless the State has taken the person into custody or created the danger (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/489/189/}{Justia}).
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (State Prevails): Slow Response to Protective-Order Violations.} City police receive three 911 calls reporting that a local resident is threatening his estranged spouse outside her apartment in violation of a state protective order. Dispatch logs the calls as low priority, no patrol car arrives for two hours, and the resident assaults the spouse. Under \textit{DeShaney} and \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/545/748/}{\textit{Castle Rock}}, a \S~1983 Due Process suit fails: the victim was not in state custody, police did not affirmatively create the danger, and state protective-order statutes confer no federal procedural property interest in arrest (remedies lie, if at all, in state tort law or Equal Protection if non-enforcement was discriminatory).

\item \textbf{Same-Side (State Prevails): Broken Promises of Extra Patrols.} A woman tells her local police department that a former boyfriend has threatened to kill her. An officer promises that patrol cars will drive past her house every hour that week, but none do, and on the third night the former boyfriend breaks in and injures her. She sues the officer and the city under \S~1983, arguing that the promise created a special relationship. Under \textit{DeShaney}, the claim fails. The State did not restrain her liberty, and the danger came from a private actor. The Court made clear that an affirmative duty arises not from the State's knowledge of a person's predicament or its ``expressions of intent to help him,'' but only from limits it has imposed on the person's freedom to act on her own behalf. Any remedy lies in state tort law (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/489/189/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Plaintiff Prevails): A Child Stranded on the Highway.} After arresting an intoxicated driver at 1:00 a.m. in sub-freezing weather on an unlit rural interstate, a state trooper confiscates the car keys and orders the driver's sober seven-year-old passenger out of the car to ``walk home'' along the highway shoulder rather than waiting for a guardian. The child is struck by a passing motorist. Unlike \textit{DeShaney}, the officer did not merely stand by while a preexisting private danger unfolded. By affirmatively removing the child from a safe vehicle and stranding her in an exposed, lethal environment she could not escape, the state actor affirmatively created the peril and rendered the child far more vulnerable to harm (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/489/189/}{489 U.S. at 201}; cf. \textit{White v. Rochford}, 592 F.2d 381 (7th Cir. 1979)). In circuits recognizing state-created danger (and arguably as a brief custodial deprivation of liberty), the plaintiff states a viable substantive due process claim.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Custody Triggers a Duty): A Prisoner Beaten by a Cellmate.} A state prisoner tells prison officers that his cellmate has threatened to kill him and asks to be moved. The officers laugh off the request, and that night the cellmate beats him severely. Unlike Joshua, the prisoner is in state custody: the State has locked him in a cell with his attacker and taken away his ability to protect himself or seek help. \textit{DeShaney} itself explained that when the State so restrains a person's liberty, as in \textit{Estelle v.\ Gamble} and \textit{Youngberg v.\ Romeo}, it assumes a duty to provide for his basic needs, including reasonable safety. The prisoner can state a constitutional claim if he shows the officers knew of and disregarded a substantial risk to his safety, the Eighth Amendment standard the Court applied to prisoners attacked by other inmates in \textit{Farmer v.\ Brennan}, 511 U.S.\ 825 (1994).

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Foster Care): Abuse in a State-Selected Foster Home.} A county child-welfare agency removes a six-year-old from her parents and places her with foster parents it has licensed and supervises. Caseworkers receive two reports that the foster father is hitting her but do not investigate, and she is seriously injured. The agency relies on \textit{DeShaney}: the abuser was a private person, not a state employee. The child answers that the State took her from her family and chose where she would live, a restraint on her liberty much closer to institutionalization than Joshua's situation. \textit{DeShaney} expressly left open whether a child in state-arranged foster care stands in a custodial relationship with the State (489 U.S.\ at 201 n.9), and the Supreme Court has not resolved the question, so the outcome is uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

### Memory Jogger

```latex
\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Taking): Denial of a Tower Atop a Landmark Warehouse.} The City of Harborview enacts a comprehensive Historic Waterfront Ordinance designating 180 Victorian warehouses, including Bayside Lofts (owned by Harbor Corp.\ since 1995), as architectural landmarks. Harbor Corp.\ wants to demolish the upper two floors and build a 30-story luxury condominium tower, which would triple the building's value. The Preservation Board denies the permit, though Bayside Lofts remains fully leased as commercial offices generating a steady 7\% annual return, and the ordinance allows Harbor Corp.\ to transfer unused floor-area ratios to three adjacent commercial lots it owns. This falls squarely under \textit{Penn Central}, not \textit{Lucas} or \textit{Cedar Point}, because there is no physical invasion and no total loss of value. Measuring the parcel as a whole (the entire Bayside Lofts lot), Harbor Corp.\ cannot sever its air rights. No taking occurred.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Taking): A Wetlands Buffer Covering Half a Lot.} An owner holds a two-acre residential lot with a house on the front acre. A county wetlands ordinance, part of a countywide program protecting watersheds, forbids any construction on the rear acre, which borders a protected marsh. The owner claims that the county has taken the rear acre outright because it can no longer be built on. Under \textit{Penn Central}, the owner cannot sever the rear acre and treat it as a separate parcel that has lost all value; the court measures the burden against the parcel as a whole. Viewed that way, the owner keeps the house and the full residential use of the property, the regulation is part of a general program adjusting benefits and burdens, and the economic impact, though real, is partial. No taking occurred.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Per Se Taking): A Public Easement and an Unconnected Permit Fee.} Modify the Harborview example: Harborview amends its ordinance to require every designated waterfront landmark owner (a) to grant the general public a permanent daytime easement along a 15-foot-wide strip of its private dock for sightseeing, and (b) if the owner applies for a routine interior renovation permit, to pay a flat \$250,000 statutory ``Harborwide Public Art Fee'' set by the City Council with no connection to the renovation's impact. Neither requirement is shielded by \textit{Penn Central} balancing. First, mandating a public sightseeing easement appropriates the owner's right to exclude and constitutes a \textit{per se} physical taking under \textit{Cedar Point Nursery v.\ Hassid}, 594 U.S.\ 139 (2021) (and \textit{Loretto}), regardless of how profitable the rest of the building remains. Second, under \textit{Sheetz v.\ County of El Dorado}, 601 U.S.\ 267 (2024), the City Council's legislatively enacted \$250,000 permit fee is not immune from \textit{Nollan}/\textit{Dolan} review; because a flat public-art fee bears no essential nexus or rough proportionality to an interior renovation's impacts, it is an unconstitutional exaction.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Per Se Taking): A Total Ban on Building on Beachfront Lots.} An owner buys two vacant beachfront lots zoned for single-family homes. Two years later, the State enacts a coastal setback law that forbids any permanent habitable structure on either lot, leaving them with no economically beneficial use. The State argues that \textit{Penn Central} balancing applies and that protecting the shoreline is a weighty public purpose. Under \textit{Lucas v.\ South Carolina Coastal Council}, 505 U.S.\ 1003 (1992), however, a regulation that deprives land of all economically beneficial use is a categorical taking, and \textit{Penn Central}'s ad hoc factors do not apply. The State can avoid paying compensation only by showing that background principles of its property or nuisance law already prohibited building homes on the lots.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): Individual Landmarking of a Small Building Without TDRs.} A developer buys a modest commercial building planning to replace it with a mid-rise apartment complex permitted by existing zoning. Shortly after the purchase, the city designates the building alone as a landmark and bars demolition. The building can still be leased, but its value falls by about 75 percent, and the city's program offers no transferable development rights at the site. The owner has strong arguments on economic impact and on frustrated investment-backed expectations, and Justice Rehnquist's concern about singling out a few owners resonates. The city answers that the building retains an economically viable use, that the designation belongs to a comprehensive preservation program like the one upheld in \textit{Penn Central}, and that diminution in value alone does not establish a taking. With the factors pulling in opposite directions and no fixed weights, the outcome is unpredictable.

\end{enumerate}
```

```latex
\item \textbf{Same-Side (Conditions Invalid): A Boardwalk Easement and an Arts Fee for a Warehouse.} A coastal city enacts an ordinance requiring any commercial developer seeking a building permit to expand floor space by more than 5,000 square feet to (a) deed a 20-foot public waterfront boardwalk easement across its lot, and (b) pay a flat \$250,000 ``Downtown Cultural Arts Fee.'' In scenario (1), no exaction is demanded, so \emph{Nollan/Dolan} does not apply and the zoning cap is evaluated under deferential \emph{Penn Central} balancing. No precise calculation is required, so the dedication stands.
```

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Taking Valid): A Regional Industrial-Park Plan.} After its largest factory closes, a county adopts, following two years of studies and public hearings, a comprehensive plan to redevelop 200 acres into an industrial park. Under \textit{Kelo}, job creation and an expanded tax base are a legitimate public purpose, the plan is judged as a whole rather than lot by lot, and no particular private beneficiary was identified in advance. Courts will not second-guess whether the projected benefits will materialize, so the takings satisfy the Public Use Clause (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/545/469/}{Justia}).

The landowner objects that the property is being transferred to a private company. \textit{Kelo} confirms this is an easy case: even before reaching the broader ``public purpose'' test, the Court treated transfers to a private common carrier whose services are open to the public as a settled example of public use. With just compensation paid, the taking is valid.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Taking Likely Invalid): A One-Parcel Transfer to a Disappointed Buyer.} Without any prior city-wide redevelopment study, a prosperous suburb receives a proposal from MegaTech Corp.\ to build a corporate campus if the city acquires an adjacent, profitable family-owned nursery whose owner refused MegaTech's private purchase offer. Unlike New London's multi-year, 90-acre integrated plan in a distressed city where most private tenants were initially unknown, this is an isolated, one-to-one transfer ($A$ to $B$) initiated \textit{after} a specific private beneficiary failed to buy the land on the open market, with no comprehensive plan and rushed procedures. Under Stevens's ``mere pretext'' prohibition (545 U.S.\ at 478, 487) and Kennedy's meaningful rational-basis concurrence (545 U.S.\ at 491--493), a court should invalidate the taking as an unconstitutional private-benefit transfer---and preserve an alternative argument to overrule \textit{Kelo} under Justice Thomas's originalist ``actual public use'' standard.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Taking Barred by State Law): A State Constitutional Amendment.} A city adopts a plan just like New London's and condemns unblighted homes for private office and retail development. The State's constitution, however, has been amended to forbid takings for transfer to private entities for the primary purpose of economic development or increased tax revenue. The owners sue in state court under the state constitution. The taking would satisfy the federal Public Use Clause under \textit{Kelo}, but \textit{Kelo} sets only a floor: the Court stressed that ``nothing in our opinion precludes any State from placing further restrictions on its exercise of the takings power.'' The state-law bar controls, and the condemnation fails.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Uncertain): A Downtown Arena for a Named Team.} A city negotiates with a professional sports team that wants a new arena, then adopts a 20-acre downtown redevelopment plan, after public hearings, that includes the arena, a public plaza, and housing, and condemns several unblighted businesses on the site. But the plan was prompted by, and is built around, a single identified private beneficiary, which is the scenario Justice Kennedy flagged as possibly warranting ``a more stringent standard of review.'' Whether a court finds a public purpose or a pretextual private transfer depends on how it weighs the planning process against the team's central role.

\end{enumerate}
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Per Se Taking): Wildlife Surveys on Fenced Timberland.} A State enacts a statute granting certified environmental nonprofits a right to enter private, fenced timberlands (closed to the public) for four hours per week during spring migration to conduct wildlife-habitat surveys, imposing civil penalties on landowners who refuse entry. Under \textit{Cedar Point}, the statute does not merely restrict how owners harvest timber; it appropriates a formal right of physical invasion for third parties. Intermittent duration (four hours weekly) affects only compensation, not liability. Because no background common-law privilege authorizes third-party wildlife surveys and the mandate is untethered to any government permit or benefit, it effects a \emph{per se} physical taking requiring just compensation.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Per Se Taking): Canvassers in a Gated Community.} A city ordinance requires the owners' association of every private, gated residential community to admit registered political canvassers two weekends a month during election season so they can go door to door, and fines associations that refuse. The streets are private and closed to the public. Under \textit{Cedar Point}, the ordinance does not regulate how owners use their land; it grants third parties a right to physically enter it, appropriating the owners' right to exclude. That the access is limited to a few weekends affects only the amount of compensation. \textit{PruneYard} does not help the city, because \textit{Cedar Point} confined it to property generally open to the public, and no exception applies: canvassing is not a background common-law privilege, and the access is not a condition on a government benefit.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Per Se Taking): Licensing Inspections and a Zoning Setback.} Now suppose a State requires commercial meatpacking plants, as a condition of obtaining a state food-processing license, to permit state agriculture inspectors to enter processing floors at reasonable business hours to test for bacterial contamination, and separately enacts a zoning ordinance barring new processing plants within 500 feet of residential schools. Here, the outcome flips completely: (1) the inspection requirement falls squarely within \textit{Cedar Point}'s third exception because physical access is conditioned on a regulatory license and bears an essential nexus and rough proportionality to preventing foodborne public-health risks (\textit{Nollan}/\textit{Dolan}); and (2) the 500-foot zoning setback appropriates no right of physical entry at all---it merely restricts the owner's \emph{use} of her own land, triggering deferential \textit{Penn Central} balancing rather than \textit{Cedar Point}'s \emph{per se} rule.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Per Se Taking): A Highway Crew's One-Time Intrusion.} While repaving a state highway, a state road crew mistakenly parks its trucks and stores gravel on an adjoining farmer's field for two days, damaging part of a crop, and then leaves. No statute or regulation gives the State any right of access to the field. The farmer claims a \emph{per se} physical taking under \textit{Cedar Point}. The claim fails under the Court's first limiting boundary: an isolated physical invasion not undertaken under a granted right of access is ordinarily a trespass, remedied in tort, not an appropriation of the right to exclude. If the intrusion recurred or became part of a deliberate government program, the farmer could argue for a taking, and courts would weigh factors like those in \textit{Arkansas Game and Fish Comm'n v. United States}, 568 U.S. 23 (2012).

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unsettled): Union Organizers at a Supermarket.} A state statute gives union organizers a right to stand on the private sidewalk and parking lot of any large supermarket, for limited hours, to speak with employees and customers. The store is open to the shopping public every day, but its owner bars all solicitation. The owner argues that \textit{Cedar Point} makes the statute a \emph{per se} taking because it grants third parties a right of access the owner never consented to. The State responds that \textit{Cedar Point} itself distinguished \textit{PruneYard}, which allowed access to a shopping center generally open to the public. Yet organizers are not customers using the property as invited, and the \textit{Cedar Point} majority treated the right to exclude as a fundamental element of property that cannot be balanced away. How far \textit{PruneYard} survives is uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Strict Scrutiny; District Invalid): A Barbell-Shaped Hispanic-Majority District.} After the 2030 census, a State with a 28\% Hispanic voting-age population enacts a congressional map containing a 210-mile, barbell-shaped District 4 that splits nine counties to connect two distant urban Hispanic enclaves, achieving a 52\% Hispanic voting-age population. Legislative emails show mapmakers set a ``50\%+ HVAP floor'' to preempt a threatened VRA \S~2 lawsuit, though no court had found intentional discrimination and no illustrative map could create the district while preserving the State's partisan-advantage goals. A white voter residing in District 4 sues under the Equal Protection Clause. The plaintiff has standing as a District 4 resident (\textit{Shaw II}) and states a strong \textit{Shaw} claim. Under \textit{Louisiana v. Callais} (2026), the State cannot establish a compelling interest in VRA \S~2 compliance because \S~2 requires a strong inference of intentional racial discrimination and an illustrative map that satisfies the State's legitimate political goals without using race; District 4 is therefore an unconstitutional racial gerrymander.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Strict Scrutiny Triggered): A Mechanical Racial Target in a Regular-Looking District.} A legislature redraws a congressional district that had elected the minority community's preferred candidates by comfortable margins for twenty years. Mapmakers testify that they were instructed to raise the district's Black voting-age population above 50 percent, and to do so they move tens of thousands of Black voters in and out of the district, splitting several precincts, although the district's outline is not especially strange. \textit{Shaw}'s logic does not depend on a bizarre shape alone: once race predominates over traditional principles, strict scrutiny applies. In \textit{Cooper v.\ Harris}, 581 U.S.\ 285 (2017), the Court struck down a North Carolina district drawn to a 50-percent racial target where the State lacked a strong basis in evidence that the VRA required it (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/509/630/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (\textit{Shaw} Claim Fails): Partisan Line-Drawing.} Now alter the facts: suppose the legislature draws a compact, county-respecting District 4 that moves 20,000 heavily Democratic Hispanic voters \textit{out} of District 4 into neighboring District 5 to lock in a safe Republican incumbent in District 4, without any racial target in the legislative record, and challengers fail to produce an alternative map that achieves the same Republican partisan margin while keeping those voters in District 4. Under \textit{Alexander v. South Carolina State Conference of the NAACP} (2024) and \textit{Rucho}, the presumption of legislative good faith and the adverse inference from the missing alternative map defeat the \textit{Shaw} racial-gerrymandering claim because the challengers failed to disentangle race from permissible partisan politics.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Strict Scrutiny): Awareness of Race in a Compact District.} A State's redistricting commission draws a compact, contiguous district that follows county and city lines and keeps a recognized metropolitan area together. The district happens to be 55 percent Black, and the commission's records show that members knew its racial makeup and discussed it, but every boundary choice is explained by population equality, compactness, and respect for political subdivisions. A voter alleges a racial gerrymander. \textit{Shaw} acknowledged that legislatures are always aware of race when they redistrict; what triggers strict scrutiny is a plan that subordinates traditional districting principles to race. Because traditional criteria, not race, explain these lines, the district is reviewed deferentially and stands.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Compelling Interest Uncertain): A Remedial District Ordered After a VRA Finding.} After a federal court finds that a State's map violates \S~2 of the Voting Rights Act by diluting Black votes, the legislature draws a new, somewhat irregular majority-Black district, consciously using race to meet the court's remedy. White and Black voters inside the district bring a \textit{Shaw} challenge. Race plainly predominated, so strict scrutiny applies. \textit{Shaw} left open whether VRA compliance can be a compelling interest; later cases assumed it could when the State has a strong basis in evidence, and a prior judicial finding of a violation is strong evidence. But \textit{Callais}, as described above, narrowed that interest to \S~2 applied to intentional discrimination. Whether this remedial district survives depends on whether the earlier \S~2 finding rested on proof that meets that narrower standard and on whether the district is narrowly tailored---a genuinely open question.

\end{enumerate}
```

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Fighting Words; Conviction Upheld): A Face-to-Face Challenge at a Protest.} During a heated sidewalk protest outside a city council meeting, an activist steps within two feet of a private counter-protester, clenches his fists, and screams a string of face-to-face personal profanities and a challenge to ``settle this right here in the street.'' \item \textbf{Same-Side (Fighting Words; Conviction Upheld): Slurs Hurled at a Neighbor's Door.} After a dispute over a property line, a man walks onto his neighbor's porch, stands inches from her husband when he opens the door, calls him a string of personal slurs, and shoves a finger in his face while daring him to step outside and fight. ## 261_tinker_v_des_moines
```

### Memory Jogger

```latex
Holding 7--2 that students do not ``shed their constitutional rights to freedom of speech or expression at the schoolhouse gate,'' the Court ruled that a school may not punish passive political expression, such as black armbands protesting the Vietnam War, unless it reasonably forecasts material and substantial disruption (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/393/503/}{Justia}).
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Student Wins): A ``Green Tyranny'' T-Shirt.} Following a polarizing local school-board vote on district climate policy, a 16-year-old public high school student wears a plain T-shirt to school reading ``Board Policy = Green Tyranny.'' She attends classes quietly, though two classmates argue loudly with her in the cafeteria during lunch, requiring a monitor to tell them to sit down. The principal orders her to turn the shirt inside out under a newly announced rule barring ``apparel criticizing school board policies,'' explaining that heated community protests over the vote might spill into the hallways. Meanwhile, students wearing ``Save Our Planet'' shirts are not disciplined. The T-shirt is passive, non-vulgar, non-school-sponsored political speech squarely governed by \textit{Tinker} (not \textit{Fraser}, \textit{Hazelwood}, or \textit{Morse}). A brief lunchtime argument resolved by a monitor falls far short of ``material and substantial interference,'' and the principal's generalized worry about community protests is precisely the ``undifferentiated fear or apprehension of disturbance'' \textit{Tinker} rejects. Moreover, permitting ``Save Our Planet'' shirts while banning anti-policy shirts mirrors Des Moines's unconstitutional viewpoint singling-out.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Student Wins): Lunchtime Leaflets About a City Council Meeting.} During the lunch period, a high school student hands classmates leaflets urging them to attend a city council meeting on local police policy. The leaflets are not vulgar, are not school-sponsored, and promote no illegal activity. A few students toss them aside; no class is interrupted. The principal suspends the student under a rule forbidding distribution of political material on campus, explaining that police policy is a divisive topic that could upset some families. Under \textit{Tinker}, the school must point to facts reasonably forecasting material and substantial interference with schoolwork or discipline, and a wish to avoid the discomfort of an unpopular viewpoint is not enough. Peaceful communication among students during lunch is the kind of intercommunication \textit{Tinker} treated as part of education, so the suspension violates the First Amendment.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (School Prevails): Documented Disruption and a Viewpoint-Neutral Ban.} Change two facts in the T-shirt example: suppose (1) prior weeks at the same school saw physical altercations, walked-out classes, and repeated hallway confrontations specifically triggered by competing climate-policy apparel, and (2) the school enforces a viewpoint-neutral ban on \textit{all} apparel referencing the climate dispute (barring both ``Green Tyranny'' and ``Save Our Planet'' shirts) while allowing students to debate the issue in social-studies class and after-school clubs. Under those facts, the school has concrete, documented evidence reasonably forecasting ``material and substantial disruption'' rather than mere undifferentiated fear, and has eliminated viewpoint discrimination---bringing the restriction squarely within \textit{Tinker}'s permissible exception (and paralleling \textit{Blackwell v. Issaquena County Bd. of Ed.}, 363 F.2d 749 (5th Cir. 1966), distinguished by Justice Fortas in \textit{Tinker}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (School Prevails): Editing the Journalism Class Newspaper.} Students in a high school journalism class write an article for the class's newspaper, which is produced with school funds under a teacher's supervision, describing named classmates' experiences with family divorce. The principal removes the article before publication, concerned that the families were not given a chance to respond and that the material is inappropriate for younger readers. \textit{Tinker}'s disruption test does not govern. Under \textit{Hazelwood School District v.\ Kuhlmeier}, 484 U.S.\ 260 (1988), a school may control the content of school-sponsored expressive activities, such as a curricular newspaper, when its actions are reasonably related to legitimate pedagogical concerns. Because readers would attribute the newspaper to the school, the principal may edit it without proving any forecast of disruption.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unsettled): A Weekend Post Mocking a Teacher.} On a Saturday, from home, a student posts on social media an edited photo of a teacher with a crude, demeaning caption, which spreads quickly among classmates. On Monday, students giggle and pass the image around during the teacher's class, and the teacher reports feeling humiliated. The school suspends the student. Under \textit{Mahanoy}, off-campus speech receives strong protection because the school's \textit{in loco parentis} role is diminished there, yet the Court left room for schools to address serious bullying or harassment of particular people. The school can point to some in-class distraction and to \textit{Tinker}'s undefined concern for the rights of others; the student can answer that the post was off-campus mockery that caused discomfort rather than material and substantial disruption. How courts strike that balance remains uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 262_shurtleff_v_city_of_boston

### Memory Jogger


### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Private Speech; Exclusion Invalid): The Library's Community Heritage Wall.} A municipal library installs a ``Community Heritage Wall'' in its main lobby and adopts a written ordinance stating that the wall is ``not a public forum'' and displays ``official city-endorsed messages celebrating local history.'' In practice, however, the library posts an online form inviting residents to submit plaques honoring local figures, approves 140 consecutive submissions without committee review---including plaques for a local brewery founder and a high-school mascot---and then rejects a mosque's plaque honoring a local Muslim philanthropist on the ground that it might offend secular patrons or violate the Establishment Clause. Under \textit{Shurtleff}, a self-serving written disclaimer does not settle the inquiry when actual practice reveals zero meaningful governmental control over content. Like Boston's 284 unvetted flag raisings, the library's come-one-come-all administration establishes a forum for private speech; excluding the mosque's plaque for its religious viewpoint violates the Free Speech Clause (\textit{Good News Club}), and under \textit{Kennedy v. Bremerton} and Gorsuch's \textit{Shurtleff} concurrence, neutral inclusion poses no Establishment Clause violation.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Private Speech; Exclusion Invalid): Holiday Displays in the Town Green.} For fifteen years a town has issued a permit to any resident group that asks to place a seasonal display on its town green in December, without reviewing designs; it has approved nativity scenes, menorahs, and a local scout troop's snowman. When an atheist association applies to erect a sign celebrating the winter solstice and questioning religious belief, the town refuses, saying the sign is offensive. As in \textit{Shurtleff}, history alone cannot carry the day when the town has exercised no meaningful control over the displays' messages, so the green hosts private speech. Rejecting one group's display because of its views on religion is viewpoint discrimination that the Free Speech Clause forbids (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/596/20-1800/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Government Speech): A Closed-List Flag Policy.} After \textit{Shurtleff}, a city adopts a written policy that its third flagpole will fly only the city flag or flags chosen by city council resolution to express the city's own messages, and it stops accepting applications from private groups. A religious group later asks to raise its flag and is refused. The policy is the kind of change Justice Breyer said nothing prevented Boston from making: the city now actively selects and controls every flag, the public has no access to the pole, and the history of flags at seats of government already favors the city. The flags are government speech, so the Free Speech Clause does not require the city to accept the group's flag.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Government Speech): Actively Reviewed Specialty License Plates.} A state motor-vehicle board reviews every proposed specialty license-plate design, routinely rejects designs it finds inconsistent with state messaging, and owns the designs it approves. The board rejects a proposed plate bearing an advocacy group's political slogan. Under \textit{Walker v.\ Texas Div., Sons of Confederate Veterans, Inc.}, 576 U.S.\ 200 (2015), which \textit{Shurtleff} distinguished, plates are traditionally used to convey state messages, observers associate them with the State, and the board actively controls their content. All three factors point to government speech, so the board may reject the design without satisfying viewpoint neutrality.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): A Juried Public Mural Program.} A city invites artists to propose murals for the walls of city-owned buildings. A city arts commission reviews each proposal for artistic quality and site compatibility and has approved about 90 percent of them, but it has no written guidelines on content. It rejects a proposal featuring religious imagery. The commission's review is more active than Boston's rubber stamp, and murals on public buildings may be read as the city's own message, which points toward government speech under \textit{Summum}. Yet the high approval rate, the absence of content standards, and the program's open invitation resemble \textit{Shurtleff}'s come-one-come-all forum. Because \textit{Shurtleff}'s holistic inquiry gives no fixed weight to each factor, the result could go either way.

\end{enumerate}
```
