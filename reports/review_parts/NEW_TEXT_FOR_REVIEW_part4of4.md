\item \textbf{Same-Side (Standing Exists): Express Appropriation for Sanctuary Repairs.}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Standing Exists): An As-Applied Challenge to a Grant Program Open to Religious Groups.} Congress enacts a spending statute authorizing the Secretary of Health and Human Services to award grants to public and private organizations, expressly including religious organizations, to provide counseling on adolescent health. The agency awards most of the money to churches whose programs incorporate worship, and taxpayers sue. If the grants are viewed as carrying out Congress's own spending program, \textit{Bowen} supports standing. If they are viewed as the agency's discretionary choices under a general appropriation, the plurality in \textit{Hein} would deny it. And with several Justices having urged that \textit{Flast} be overruled, the result is genuinely uncertain.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Disclosure Invalid): A Legislative Demand for an Advocacy Group's Members.} A state legislative committee investigating whether a small LGBTQ advocacy group in a rural county has lobbied without registering subpoenas the group's complete membership list. The group has already registered, produced its officers' names and its budget, and offered sworn testimony about its lobbying. It submits affidavits showing that members whose affiliation became known locally lost jobs and received threats. As in \textit{NAACP v.\ Alabama}, compelled disclosure would expose members to private reprisal made possible by state compulsion, and the rank-and-file names have no substantial bearing on the committee's stated purpose, which the group's other disclosures already serve. The subpoena cannot be enforced (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/357/449/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Disclosure Invalid): Teachers Required to List Every Association.} A State requires every public-school teacher, as a condition of employment, to file an affidavit listing every organization to which the teacher has belonged or contributed in the past five years. The State says it needs the information to evaluate teachers' fitness. Applying \textit{NAACP v.\ Alabama}, the Court struck down a nearly identical Arkansas law in \textit{Shelton v.\ Tucker}, 364 U.S.\ 479 (1960). The State may inquire into teachers' competence, but a demand for every association, with no limit to those relevant to fitness, reaches far beyond that interest and deters teachers from joining unpopular groups. Because disclosure is not closely tailored to the State's legitimate need, the requirement violates the freedom of association.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Disclosure Valid): Contributions to a Federal Candidate.} A federal statute requires every candidate's campaign committee to report the name and address of anyone who contributes more than \$200, and the reports are made public. A donors' group challenges the law on its face, invoking \textit{NAACP v.\ Alabama}, but offers no evidence that its supporters have faced threats or harassment. Under \textit{Buckley v.\ Valeo}, 424 U.S.\ 1 (1976), disclosure of contributions to candidates survives exacting scrutiny because it informs voters, deters corruption, and supports enforcement of contribution limits. \textit{NAACP v.\ Alabama} still matters at the margin: a minor party or group that shows a reasonable probability that disclosure will bring threats or reprisals can seek an as-applied exemption.

Unlike the NAACP's members, the signers have not shown that disclosure will expose them to reprisal; if a particular group can make that showing, an as-applied challenge remains available.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Private Speech; Exclusion Invalid): The Library's Community Heritage Wall.}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Private Speech; Exclusion Invalid): Holiday Displays in the Town Green.} For fifteen years a town has issued a permit to any resident group that asks to place a seasonal display on its town green in December, without reviewing designs; it has approved nativity scenes, menorahs, and a local scout troop's snowman. When an atheist association applies to erect a sign celebrating the winter solstice and questioning religious belief, the town refuses, saying the sign is offensive. As in \textit{Shurtleff}, history alone cannot carry the day when the town has exercised no meaningful control over the displays' messages, so the green hosts private speech. Rejecting one group's display because of its views on religion is viewpoint discrimination that the Free Speech Clause forbids (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/596/20-1800/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Government Speech): A Closed-List Flag Policy.} After \textit{Shurtleff}, a city adopts a written policy that its third flagpole will fly only the city flag or flags chosen by city council resolution to express the city's own messages, and it stops accepting applications from private groups. A religious group later asks to raise its flag and is refused. The policy is the kind of change Justice Breyer said nothing prevented Boston from making: the city now actively selects and controls every flag, the public has no access to the pole, and the history of flags at seats of government already favors the city. The flags are government speech, so the Free Speech Clause does not require the city to accept the group's flag.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Government Speech): Actively Reviewed Specialty License Plates.} A state motor-vehicle board reviews every proposed specialty license-plate design, routinely rejects designs it finds inconsistent with state messaging, and owns the designs it approves. The board rejects a proposed plate bearing an advocacy group's political slogan. Under \textit{Walker v.\ Texas Div., Sons of Confederate Veterans, Inc.}, 576 U.S.\ 200 (2015), which \textit{Shurtleff} distinguished, plates are traditionally used to convey state messages, observers associate them with the State, and the board actively controls their content. All three factors point to government speech, so the board may reject the design without satisfying viewpoint neutrality.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): A Juried Public Mural Program.} A city invites artists to propose murals for the walls of city-owned buildings. A city arts commission reviews each proposal for artistic quality and site compatibility and has approved about 90 percent of them, but it has no written guidelines on content. It rejects a proposal featuring religious imagery. The commission's review is more active than Boston's rubber stamp, and murals on public buildings may be read as the city's own message, which points toward government speech under \textit{Summum}. Yet the high approval rate, the absence of content standards, and the program's open invitation resemble \textit{Shurtleff}'s come-one-come-all forum. Because \textit{Shurtleff}'s holistic inquiry gives no fixed weight to each factor, the result could go either way.
