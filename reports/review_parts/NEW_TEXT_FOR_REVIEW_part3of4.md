### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Attainder): Barring Members of a Named Protest Group From Utility Boards.} After congressional hearings link an activist coalition, ``GridLock Now,'' to disruptive energy protests, Congress enacts a federal statute making it a felony for any current member---or anyone who belonged to GridLock Now during the past three years---to serve as an officer or board member of any interstate electric utility. A utility director who joined GridLock Now two years ago to support solar incentives, and never advocated disruption, is indicted. Under \textit{Brown}, the statute fails as a bill of attainder: it singles out an ascertainable political group by name, imposes a historical vocational bar backed by criminal punishment and a multi-year lookback, and substitutes legislative factfinding about group propensities for individualized judicial adjudication, regardless of Congress's preventive grid-security rationale.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Attainder): A Rider Cutting Off Named Officials' Salaries.} After a committee hearing in which members accuse three named career employees of an executive agency of disloyalty, Congress attaches a rider to an appropriations bill providing that no funds may be used to pay their salaries unless they are reappointed by the President with Senate confirmation. The employees sue for their pay. This is the pattern of \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/328/303/}{\textit{United States v. Lovett}}, 328 U.S. 303 (1946), on which \textit{Brown} relied: Congress identified specific people, found them blameworthy without a trial, and imposed a permanent bar from government employment, a vocational disqualification that \textit{Brown} recognized as classic attainder punishment. That Congress acted through its spending power, or claimed only to protect the agency in the future, does not help; \textit{Brown} held that preventive aims are still punishment.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Valid General Rule): Disqualification After an Individualized Hearing.} First, a statute barring any person from serving as an electric-utility officer if a federal agency or court finds, after an individualized hearing with judicial review, that the person conspired to sabotage critical infrastructure mirrors the general behavioral rulemaking Warren endorsed in \textit{Brown} (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/381/437/}{381 U.S. at 461}) and \textit{Communist Party v. SACB}.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Likely Valid): A Procurement Ban Naming a Foreign-Controlled Vendor.} A statute prohibiting federal procurement of grid-control software from a named foreign-adversary-controlled vendor (or requiring foreign divestiture) would likely be upheld under \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/433/425/}{\textit{Nixon}}, \href{https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/district-courts/texas/txedce/4:2019cv00159/188186/51/}{\textit{Huawei}}, and \href{https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/cadc/24-1113/24-1113-2024-12-06.html}{\textit{TikTok}} as nonpunitive, prospective national-security regulation rather than punitive disqualification of natural persons for political association.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): Defunding a Named Domestic Nonprofit.} After news reports that some employees of a national community-organizing nonprofit gave improper advice to clients, Congress enacts a law barring that organization, by name, and its affiliates from receiving any federal grants or contracts. No court or agency has found the organization liable for anything. Under \textit{Brown}, the law looks like an attainder: Congress named a specific group, made its own findings of wrongdoing, and imposed a sanction without a trial, and congressional statements condemning the group may show punitive motive. Under \textit{Nixon} and \textit{Selective Service}, however, denying discretionary federal funding is not a traditional attainder penalty like imprisonment or a bar from a profession, and Congress can point to a nonpunitive purpose of protecting public funds. Which test dominates, and how heavily the legislative record weighs, makes the outcome uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 217_nixon_v_administrator_of_general_services

### Memory Jogger

```latex
After Watergate, Congress seized former President Nixon's papers and tapes for archival screening; the Court held 7--2 that the Act was not a bill of attainder (Nixon was a legitimate ``class of one'' and was not punished) and did not violate separation of powers, presidential privilege, privacy, or association rights (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/433/425/}{Justia}).
```

### Judicial Votes

```latex
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority Opinion:} Justice Brennan delivered the opinion of the Court, joined in full by Justices Stewart, Marshall, and Stevens; by Justice White in all but Part VII; by Justice Powell in all but Parts IV and V; and by Justice Blackmun as to Part VII.
    \item \textbf{Concurring Opinions:} Justice Stevens filed a concurring opinion. Justices White, Blackmun, and Powell each filed an opinion concurring in part and concurring in the judgment.
    \item \textbf{Dissenting Opinions:} Chief Justice Burger and Justice Rehnquist each filed a dissenting opinion.
\end{itemize}
```

### Analysis of Opinions

```latex
\item \textbf{A Targeted Statute That Failed \textit{Nixon}'s Test:} The D.C. Circuit applied \textit{Nixon} in \href{https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/F3/351/1198/525998/}{\textit{Foretich v.\ United States}}, 351 F.3d 1198 (D.C. Cir.\ 2003).
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Bill of Attainder): A Procurement Ban Naming One Company.} Suppose Congress enacts a statute naming ``Apex AI Corp.'' and (a) prohibiting federal agencies from contracting with Apex due to documented foreign-intelligence vulnerabilities. Under \textit{Nixon}, provision (a) likely survives all three punishment prongs as prophylactic procurement regulation: declining to buy from a vendor is not a traditional attainder sanction, the ban reasonably serves the nonpunitive goal of protecting federal systems, and a record of documented security risks shows regulatory concern rather than legislative condemnation. Like Nixon, Apex may be a legitimate class of one if it alone presents the identified risk.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Bill of Attainder): Securing One Official's Records.} After a former Cabinet secretary leaves office, Congress learns that she conducted official business on a private email server and that her personal attorney plans to delete messages. Congress passes a statute naming her and directing the National Archives to take custody of the server, have career archivists separate official from personal messages, return personal messages to her, and pay just compensation for any property taken. As in \textit{Nixon}, she is a legitimate class of one because only her records face imminent destruction; custody and screening are not traditional punishments; the statute furthers the nonpunitive goal of preserving public records; and the compensation and return provisions negate any inference of punitive confiscation.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Bill of Attainder): Uncompensated Forfeiture Amid Condemnation.} Suppose the same statute also contains a provision (b) forfeiting all of Apex's U.S. patents to the Treasury without compensation after floor speeches branding Apex's founders ``traitors.'' Under \textit{Nixon}, provision (b) fails both the historical test (uncompensated punitive confiscation) and the motivational/functional tests, much as the Elizabeth Morgan Act failed in \textit{Foretich}. Unlike the PRMPA, which provided just compensation and served evidence-preservation goals, the forfeiture is a severe deprivation that does nothing to address the security risk and rests on legislative condemnation.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Separation-of-Powers Problem): Congress Takes the Records Itself.} Congress passes a statute transferring custody of a departing President's records to a House committee, which may review and publish any record it chooses, including privileged national-security advice. The incumbent President, who supports the outgoing President's privilege claims, objects. \textit{Nixon} upheld the PRMPA because the records stayed under Executive Branch control, screening was done by career archivists, Congress received no privileged access, and the incumbent supported the law. Each of those features is missing here. A court applying \textit{Nixon}'s disruption test would likely find that the statute prevents the Executive Branch from protecting confidential communications without an overriding need to justify it.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unresolved): Waiver by a Hostile Successor.} Suppose a newly elected President waives executive privilege over a predecessor's candid Oval Office national-security memos so a congressional committee can publish them, despite no active criminal trial or pending legislation. Students should analyze whether \textit{Nixon}'s diminished-weight rule for former Presidents (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/433/425/}{433 U.S. at 449}) controls, or whether Justice Blackmun's hostile-transition warning and modern Article II formalism (\textit{Trump v. Mazars}; \textit{Trump v. United States}) demand heightened judicial scrutiny. The incumbent's support favors disclosure under \textit{Nixon}, but unlike in \textit{Nixon}, publication rather than archival screening is at stake and no evidentiary need is present, so the outcome is genuinely uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 218_sveen_v_melin

### Memory Jogger

```latex
Minnesota's revocation-on-divorce statute, applied retroactively, cut an ex-wife out as beneficiary of a life-insurance policy bought before the law passed; an 8--1 Court held there was no Contracts Clause violation because the law did not substantially impair the contract---it tracks likely intent, does what a divorce court could do, and can be undone with a simple form (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/584/16-1432/}{Justia}).
```

### Judicial Votes

```latex
The Supreme Court \textbf{reversed and remanded} the Eighth Circuit by an \textbf{8--1} vote. The case was argued on March 19, 2018, and decided on June 11, 2018 (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/584/16-1432/}{Justia}; \href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/16-1432}{Cornell LII}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority Opinion:} Justice Kagan delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Thomas, Ginsburg, Breyer, Alito, and Sotomayor.
    \item \textbf{Concurring Opinions:} None.
    \item \textbf{Dissenting Opinion:} Justice Gorsuch filed a dissenting opinion.
\end{itemize}
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Substantial Impairment): Revocation on Divorce for a Payable-on-Death Account.} In 2010 a depositor opens a bank account payable on his death to his then-wife. In 2015 the State extends its revocation-on-divorce statute to payable-on-death accounts, applying it to existing accounts. The couple divorces in 2018, the depositor never changes the designation, and on his death in 2023 his children and his ex-wife both claim the funds. All three of \textit{Sveen}'s factors favor the State: the statute approximates what most divorced depositors would want, a divorce court could always have reallocated the account, and the depositor could have restored his ex-wife as beneficiary with a simple form. The law does not substantially impair the contract, so the inquiry ends at step one (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/584/16-1432/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Substantial Impairment): A Filing Requirement for Old Mineral Interests.} A State enacts a law providing that a severed mineral interest unused for twenty years lapses and reverts to the surface owner unless the owner files a short statement of claim in the county records within two years after the law's enactment. A mineral owner whose interest predates the statute misses the deadline and loses the interest. \textit{Sveen} relied on a long line of decisions upholding retroactive recording and filing requirements, including \textit{Texaco, Inc.\ v.\ Short}, 454 U.S.\ 516 (1982), which sustained a law of this kind. Because the owner could have preserved the right with minimal paperwork, the law does not substantially impair the contract.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Substantial Impairment): Retroactively Voiding Non-Competes and Guaranties.} Suppose a State enacts a statute retroactively voiding all pre-existing non-compete clauses and personal loan guaranties in commercial leases executed before 2026, with no opt-out or reaffirmation mechanism. \textit{Analysis:} Unlike \textit{Sveen}, this law fails all three step-one factors---it contradicts party intent, shatters core reliance expectations, and permanently bars parties from safeguarding or reinstating their rights. The court must proceed to step two (\textit{Energy Reserves} / \textit{Melendez}) to determine whether the permanent abrogation is reasonable and appropriate to a significant public purpose.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Law Invalid): A State Repeals Its Own Bond Covenant.} To raise money for mass transit, a State repeals a statutory covenant, made to purchasers of its bonds, that limited the use of pledged toll revenues to protect bondholders' security. Bondholders sue. Unlike Minnesota's neutral default rule for private beneficiary designations, the repeal destroys a core term of the bargain, defeats bondholders' reasonable expectations, and cannot be undone by any action of theirs, so it substantially impairs the contract. And because the State is impairing its own financial obligation, the step-two inquiry is not deferential: under \textit{United States Trust Co.\ v.\ New Jersey}, 431 U.S.\ 1 (1977), which struck down a repeal of this kind, the impairment must be reasonable and necessary, and less drastic alternatives were available.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Uncertain): A Revocation Rule That Is Hard to Undo.} A State's revocation-on-divorce statute applies retroactively to existing life-insurance policies but provides that a policyholder may re-designate a former spouse only by petitioning a court, paying a filing fee, and appearing at a hearing. The first two \textit{Sveen} factors still favor the State: the law reflects probable intent and does what a divorce court could have done. The third factor, however, now cuts the other way, because the policyholder can no longer undo the default ``with the stroke of a pen.'' \textit{Sveen} relied on the three features together and did not say whether the first two suffice alone, so whether this law substantially impairs the contract---and must face step-two review---is genuinely unclear.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 219_smith_v_doe

### Memory Jogger

```latex
Alaska applied its sex offender registration and Internet notification law to offenders convicted before it was enacted; the Court held 6--3 that the law was a civil, nonpunitive regulation under the two-step intent--effects test, so its retroactive application did not violate the Ex Post Facto Clause (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/538/84/}{Justia}).
```

### Judicial Votes

```latex
The Supreme Court \textbf{reversed and remanded} the Ninth Circuit by a \textbf{6--3} vote. The case was argued on November 13, 2002, and decided on March 5, 2003 (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/538/84/}{Justia}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority Opinion:} Justice Kennedy delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Rehnquist and Justices O'Connor, Scalia, and Thomas.
    \item \textbf{Concurring Opinions:} Justice Thomas filed a concurring opinion. Justice Souter filed an opinion concurring in the judgment.
    \item \textbf{Dissenting Opinions:} Justice Stevens filed a dissenting opinion. Justice Ginsburg filed a dissenting opinion, joined by Justice Breyer.
\end{itemize}
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Civil Regulation; No Ex Post Facto Violation): A Retroactive Financial-Fraud Registry.} Congress enacts the ``Investor Protection Act,'' codified in the civil securities title, requiring anyone convicted of securities fraud since 1995 to report all bank accounts quarterly for life and publishing their home address, photo, and employer on a searchable SEC ``Fraudster Watch'' website, with no off-ramp for rehabilitation. Under \textit{Smith}, the government argues civil intent (express investor-protection purpose, civil codification, administrative enforcement) and nonpunitive effects (no physical restraint, passive online archive of public convictions, rational fit). Challengers argue under \textit{Snyder} and Justice Ginsburg's dissent that lifetime reporting of private financial accounts and home addresses without individualized risk assessment is excessive and stigmatizing, though \textit{Smith}'s ``clearest proof'' standard heavily favors the government unless geographic or occupational bans are added.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Civil Regulation; No Ex Post Facto Violation): Civil Commitment of a Dangerous Offender.} As an inmate convicted of child sexual abuse nears release, the State petitions under a newly enacted statute to commit him to a secure treatment facility as a sexually violent predator, relying partly on conduct that predates the law. The statute is placed in the probate code, requires proof of a mental abnormality making the person dangerous, and provides for release once he is no longer dangerous. Under the intent--effects test that \textit{Smith} drew from \textit{Kansas v.\ Hendricks}, 521 U.S.\ 346 (1997), the legislature's civil purpose controls unless the clearest proof shows punitive effect, and \textit{Hendricks} upheld materially identical commitment against an Ex Post Facto challenge.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Punishment): Residency Bans Enforced by the Sentencing Court.} Suppose instead Congress amends federal criminal statutes to impose a retroactive ``Public Safety Surcharge'' and a 1,500-foot residential exclusion zone around schools and parks on all pre-enactment offenders, enforced by the sentencing court at a criminal modification hearing with immediate re-incarceration for any technical violation. Unlike \textit{Smith}, this statute fails at \textit{both} steps. Under Step One and \href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/24-482}{\textit{Ellingburg v. United States}} (2026), codification in the criminal code, imposition by a sentencing court, and enforcement through criminal resentencing reveal punitive legislative intent, ending the inquiry. Even if Congress labeled it ``civil,'' under Step Two and \textit{Snyder} / \href{https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca6/23-5248/23-5248-2024-05-15.html}{\textit{Doe v. Lee}} (6th Cir. 2024), retroactive residential banishment and revocation-style enforcement impose affirmative physical restraints historically regarded as punishment, supplying the ``clearest proof'' needed to invalidate the law under the Ex Post Facto Clause.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Punishment): Applying Harsher Sentencing Guidelines to an Earlier Crime.} A defendant commits bank fraud in 2005 and is sentenced in 2010 under amended federal Sentencing Guidelines that raise his recommended range well above the range in effect when he acted. No one disputes that a prison sentence is punishment, so there is no civil label for \textit{Smith}'s intent--effects inquiry to test; the only question is whether the change increases the punishment attached to his crime. In \textit{Peugh v.\ United States}, 569 U.S.\ 530 (2013), the Court held that sentencing a defendant under Guidelines promulgated after the offense, when they yield a higher range, violates the Ex Post Facto Clause, even though the Guidelines are advisory.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): Lifetime GPS Monitoring Run by the Corrections Department.} A State requires every registrant convicted of an aggravated sex offense, including those convicted before the law took effect, to wear a GPS ankle monitor for life, with the corrections department tracking his location around the clock. The statute declares a public-safety purpose and sits in the civil code, so Step One favors the State. At Step Two, however, the device is a continuous physical burden supervised by corrections officers, resembling the probation and supervised release that \textit{Smith} distinguished because Alaska's registrants could move and work without supervision. Whether that restraint supplies the clearest proof of punishment, or is a reasonable means of protecting the public, has divided courts and is uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 220_moore_v_united_states

### Memory Jogger

```latex
Taxpayers who owned 13\% of an Indian company challenged the 2017 Mandatory Repatriation Tax as an unapportioned tax on unrealized income; the Court held 7--2 that Congress may attribute an entity's realized but undistributed income to its shareholders and tax them on it, while reserving whether the Constitution requires realization (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/602/22-800/}{Justia}).
```

### Judicial Votes

```latex
The Supreme Court \textbf{affirmed} the Ninth Circuit by a \textbf{7--2} vote. The case was argued December 5, 2023, and decided June 20, 2024 (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/602/22-800/}{Justia}; \href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-800_2024-06-20}{Cornell LII}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority Opinion:} Justice Kavanaugh delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson.
    \item \textbf{Concurring Opinions:} Justice Jackson filed a concurring opinion. Justice Barrett filed an opinion concurring in the judgment, joined by Justice Alito.
    \item \textbf{Dissenting Opinion:} Justice Thomas filed a dissenting opinion, joined by Justice Gorsuch.
\end{itemize}
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Attribution Valid): An Annual Tax on U.S. Shareholders of Foreign Subsidiaries.} Congress enacts a permanent annual tax requiring every U.S.\ person who owns at least 10\% of a controlled foreign corporation to report and pay tax on his pro rata share of the corporation's realized net business profits for that year, whether or not the profits are distributed. The corporation pays no U.S.\ tax on those profits. A 15\% shareholder argues that she has received nothing and so has no ``income.'' Under \textit{Moore}, the tax is valid without apportionment: the corporation has realized the income, and Congress may attribute that realized, undistributed income to its shareholders and tax them on their shares, just as the Mandatory Repatriation Tax and Subpart F do (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/602/22-800/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Attribution Valid): Partners Taxed on Profits a Partnership Retains.} A domestic real-estate partnership earns \$10 million in rental profits in a year and, as its agreement permits, distributes nothing to its partners. Congress requires each partner to pay income tax on his share of the partnership's profits, and a minority partner who cannot force a distribution objects that he is being taxed on money he never received. This is the paradigm case \textit{Moore} relied on. The partnership realized the income, the partners are taxed on their pro rata shares, and the entity itself is not taxed. The precedents the Court cited, especially \textit{Heiner v.\ Mellon}, 304 U.S.\ 271 (1938), upheld taxing partners on undistributed profits even when state law barred distribution, so the tax needs no apportionment.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (\textit{Moore} Does Not Apply): A Mark-to-Market Tax on Individual Holdings.} Congress enacts an unapportioned annual 2\% ``Billionaire Appreciation Tax'' on the year-over-year increase in fair market value of unsold real estate, fine art, and publicly traded equities held by individuals worth over \$100 million. Unlike \textit{Moore}, \textit{no entity has realized any income}---the tax targets pure unrealized appreciation of property held directly by individuals. Under \textit{Moore}'s explicit reservation, pass-through attribution cannot save this statute. Four Justices (Barrett, Alito, Thomas, Gorsuch) are already on record that unapportioned taxes on unrealized appreciation violate the Sixteenth Amendment and Direct Tax Clause; the Government would need all five remaining Justices (including Roberts and Kavanaugh, who stressed the Government's own concessions) either to reject a constitutional realization requirement (per Jackson) or to recharacterize the levy as an indirect excise.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Arbitrary Attribution): Taxing a Corporation's Customers on Its Profits.} Congress provides that the undistributed profits of any foreign corporation shall be attributed to the U.S.\ persons who bought its products during the year, in proportion to their purchases, and taxed to them. A U.S.\ retailer that bought inventory from a foreign manufacturer, and owns none of its stock, is assessed tax on part of the manufacturer's earnings. \textit{Moore} permits Congress to attribute an entity's realized income to the entity's \emph{shareholders or partners}; it does not permit attribution to strangers to the entity. The Court also noted that Due Process bars arbitrary attribution, citing \textit{Burnet v.\ Wells}, 289 U.S.\ 670 (1933). Because the retailer has no ownership interest and no claim to the profits, the attribution is arbitrary and the tax cannot be sustained on \textit{Moore}'s reasoning.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (How Far Attribution Reaches): A Pass-Through Tax on Shareholders of Public Companies.} Congress repeals the corporate income tax (\S\ 11) for publicly traded U.S.\ corporations and enacts a pass-through tax attributing each corporation's realized annual net profits pro rata to every U.S.\ shareholder owning at least one share on December 31. Under Kavanaugh's majority (see footnote 8 rejecting Barrett's domestic/widely held distinction), this satisfies pass-through attribution of entity-realized income, though challengers would press Due Process arbitrariness (lack of shareholder control or ability to compel dividends) and invoke Barrett's concurrence plus Thomas's dissent (4 Justices) to argue corporate form cannot be pierced for passive retail investors. Because the majority decided only the controlled-foreign-corporation tax before it, and four Justices doubted that corporate form may be disregarded for passive investors, the outcome is uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 221_craig_v_missouri

### Memory Jogger

```latex
For a 4--3 Court, Chief Justice Marshall held that Missouri's interest-bearing loan-office certificates, issued on the faith of the state and designed to circulate as money, were unconstitutional ``bills of credit'' under Article I, Section 10, even though they were not legal tender, so a note given for them was void (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/29/410/}{Justia}).
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Unconstitutional): Faith-Backed Fiscal Liquidity Certificates.} Facing a severe budget impasse, the State of Ozark enacts a statute directing its State Treasurer to print \$500 million in ``Fiscal Liquidity Certificates'' in \$5, \$20, and \$100 bearer denominations. The certificates are not legal tender for private debts, but they are receivable at face value for all state taxes and public-university tuition, and the statute pledges ``the full faith and credit of the State of Ozark'' for redemption in five years. Unconstitutional under \textit{Craig}. Even without private legal-tender status or the label ``bills of credit,'' the certificates are issued directly by state officers, backed by the state's faith, and structured in small bearer denominations to circulate as a common medium of exchange.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Unconstitutional): Interest-Bearing Scrip Backed by a Dedicated Fund.} During a cash shortage, a State's comptroller pays state employees and contractors with interest-bearing scrip in \$1, \$5, and \$10 denominations, payable to bearer and receivable for all state taxes and fees. The statute pledges the revenue of the state lottery to redeem the scrip within three years. The State argues that interest makes the scrip's value fluctuate and that a dedicated fund, not bare state credit, stands behind it. Those were the arguments of Justices Johnson and Thompson in dissent, and the \textit{Craig} majority rejected them: Missouri's certificates also bore interest and rested on a pledged fund. Because the scrip is issued directly by state officers in small denominations designed to circulate as money, it is a prohibited bill of credit.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Constitutional): Ordinary State Bonds Sold to Investors.} To finance highway construction, a State sells \$800 million in twenty-year general-obligation bonds in \$5,000 denominations to banks, pension funds, and other investors, pledging its full faith and credit to repay them with interest. A taxpayer argues that paper issued on the faith of the State is a bill of credit under \textit{Craig}. The argument fails. Marshall explained that the Bills of Credit Clause does not bar ordinary state borrowing or contracts to pay for goods and services; it forbids paper intended to circulate through the community for its ordinary purposes as money. Large-denomination investment bonds are a loan to the State, not a medium of exchange, so they are not bills of credit even though they rest on the State's credit.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Constitutional Under \textit{Briscoe}): Notes of a State-Owned Corporate Bank.} A State charters a bank, owned entirely by the State and governed by directors chosen by the legislature, that can sue and be sued in its own name. The bank lends its own small-denomination notes to borrowers, and the notes circulate as currency; they are redeemable from the bank's own capital and are not issued on the faith of the State. Under \textit{Briscoe v.\ Bank of Commonwealth of Kentucky}, 36 U.S.\ (11 Pet.) 257 (1837), the notes are not bills of credit because a separately incorporated, suable entity issues them on its own corporate funds. Justice Story's \textit{Briscoe} dissent, invoking \textit{Craig}'s rule that the Constitution prohibits things rather than names, would treat them as an indirect emission, but \textit{Briscoe} remains the governing rule.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unsettled): A Reserve-Backed State Digital Token.} By contrast, suppose the State of Frontier charters an independent, suable public corporation to issue ``Frontier Digital Dollars''---blockchain tokens redeemable on demand for \$1.00 U.S., backed 102\% by segregated U.S. dollars and short-term Treasury bills held in trust, with an explicit statutory disclaimer that neither the state's general faith nor its taxing power backs the tokens. Under \textit{Briscoe} and \textit{Houston \& Texas Central}, the tokens likely survive Article I, Section 10 scrutiny because they are issued by a separately suable corporate instrumentality, backed by a dedicated, fully liquid reserve fund rather than sovereign credit, and redeemable on demand. Conversely, a functionalist challenger invoking Marshall's ``names, and not things'' principle in \textit{Craig} and Story's \textit{Briscoe} dissent would argue that a state-created, state-governed entity minting a circulating digital medium designed for everyday commerce and taxes is an impermissible indirect emission of bills of credit. The Court has never applied \textit{Craig} or \textit{Briscoe} to a digital instrument, so which line controls is uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

```latex
In the lead \textit{Virginia Coupon Case}, a 5--4 Court held that Virginia's tax-receivable bond coupons were valid contracts, not forbidden bills of credit; that the 1882 law refusing them impaired the obligation of contracts; and that a taxpayer could sue the collector who seized his desk because an officer enforcing a void statute is not the State (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/114/270/}{Justia}).
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Officer Suit Allowed): Refusing Bond Coupons and Levying on a Company's Property.} A State issues twenty-year bonds whose interest coupons, by statute, are receivable for all state taxes at maturity. A later legislature forbids revenue officers to accept the coupons. A manufacturer tenders matured coupons for its franchise tax; the state revenue officer rejects them and seizes the company's delivery truck for nonpayment. The company sues the officer personally to recover the truck. Under \textit{Poindexter}, the receivability promise is a contract the later statute unconstitutionally impairs, the tender is equivalent to payment, and the void statute gives the officer no authority. He is therefore answerable as an individual wrongdoer, and the Eleventh Amendment does not bar the suit (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/114/270/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Not a Bill of Credit): Tax-Anticipation Warrants Receivable at Maturity.} To cover a seasonal cash shortfall, a State issues interest-bearing warrants in \$5{,}000 denominations, payable in one year and receivable for state taxes only once they mature. When the State later tries to disown the warrants by arguing that they were unconstitutional bills of credit, a holder sues. The argument fails under \textit{Poindexter}. The warrants are evidence of debt designed to be paid at maturity, not paper designed to circulate from hand to hand as money; their large denomination and maturity-only receivability confirm that the State did not intend them as currency. They are therefore valid obligations the State must honor.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Likely Bills of Credit): Small-Denomination Tax Credit Notes.} Facing a severe budget shortfall, State X issues \$10 million in bearer ``Tax Credit Notes'' in \$5, \$10, and \$20 denominations, payable immediately upon issuance, transferable by delivery, and receivable for all state taxes and fees. Two years later, State X repeals tax-receivability and directs county treasurers to seize property of taxpayers who tender the notes. When a taxpayer sues the county treasurer in federal court for return of a seized truck, how should the court rule? Under \textit{Craig} and \textit{Poindexter}, the threshold issue is whether the notes are void \textit{ab initio} as unconstitutional bills of credit. Unlike Virginia's matured bond coupons in \textit{Poindexter}, State X's small-denomination, immediately receivable bearer notes closely resemble Missouri's circulating loan certificates in \textit{Craig}. If void as bills of credit, no valid contract existed for the Contracts Clause to protect; if valid debt instruments, however, \textit{Poindexter} permits the officer suit against the treasurer personally unless framed as an affirmative raid on the state treasury.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Suit Barred): Ordering the Treasurer to Pay Overdue Coupons.} After a State defaults on its bonds, a bondholder sues the state treasurer in federal court for an order directing him to pay the overdue interest coupons out of the state treasury. Unlike Poindexter, the bondholder does not complain that an officer has committed an affirmative wrong against his property; he seeks to compel the State itself to perform its contract with public funds. \textit{Poindexter} expressly distinguished such suits, which \textit{Louisiana v.\ Jumel}, 107 U.S.\ 711 (1883), held barred, and \textit{In re Ayers} confirmed that a suit seeking only to compel performance of a state contract is a suit against the State. The Eleventh Amendment bars it.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Uncertain): An Injunction Ordering Collectors to Accept Coupons.} Instead of waiting for a seizure, a taxpayer holding matured coupons sues the state tax commissioner in federal court for an injunction requiring all collectors to accept the coupons for taxes in the future. \textit{Poindexter} supports the claim on the merits, and \textit{Ex parte Young}, which built on \textit{Poindexter}, permits prospective injunctions against officials enforcing unconstitutional laws. But the order would require officials to take affirmative action that compels the State to honor its contract, which looks like the specific performance \textit{Poindexter} and \textit{In re Ayers} treated as a suit against the State. Whether a court characterizes the relief as stopping enforcement of a void law or as enforcing the State's contract would likely decide the case.

\end{enumerate}
```


### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Judgment Must Be Enforced): An Illegal Sports Wager.} State A prohibits sports-betting debts as a misdemeanor and declares them ``unenforceable in any court.'' Resident X and Resident Y, both domiciled in State A, enter an illegal wager in State A. While Y attends a trade show in State B, X serves Y and obtains a final civil money judgment in State B's trial court, which mistakenly rejects Y's State A illegality defense. Y does not appeal in State B. When X seeks to enforce the judgment in State A, State A's courts refuse on public-policy grounds. Under \textit{Fauntleroy}, State A \textbf{must} enforce the judgment. State B had valid personal jurisdiction (in-state service) and subject-matter jurisdiction; its mistake of State A law had to be corrected on direct appeal in State B, not collaterally in State A.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Judgment Must Be Enforced): Damages for Breach of a Non-Compete.} State C's statutes declare employee non-compete agreements void as against public policy. An employer sues a former employee in State D, where the employee worked and where non-competes are enforceable, and obtains a final money judgment for breach of a non-compete covering State C. The employee moves home to State C, and the employer seeks to enforce the judgment there. State C's courts may not refuse on the ground that the underlying agreement offends State C's policy. State D had jurisdiction over the parties and the claim, so under \textit{Fauntleroy} its judgment is conclusive even if it misjudged which State's law should govern; the employee's remedy was appeal in State D (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/210/230/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Enforcement May Be Refused): A State Civil Penalty Against an Absent Defendant.} Now alter the facts so that \textit{State B's Attorney General} obtains a \$100,000 statutory civil penalty in State B against Y (a State A resident who never entered State B and never appeared) for mailing regulated goods into State B, and seeks enforcement in State A. Here, State A has two potent \textit{Fauntleroy}-compatible defenses that flip the outcome: (1) \textit{lack of personal jurisdiction} in State B (which Holmes expressly preserved as a valid collateral attack, 210 U.S. at 237, and which Y did not waive by defaulting), and (2) the \textit{penal-judgment exception} (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/146/657/}{\textit{Huntington v. Attrill}, 146 U.S. 657 (1892)}), arguing that a state-brought punitive fine---unlike Fauntleroy's private contract/arbitration recovery---is a penal obligation that sister states need not execute.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Decree Need Not Be Obeyed): An Injunction Against Testifying.} A third contrast arises under \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/522/222/}{\textit{Baker v. General Motors Corp.}, 522 U.S. 222 (1998)}: if State B enters an injunction barring Y from testifying in State A on behalf of nonparty strangers, State A may disregard the order because one state's decree cannot hijack another state's evidentiary procedures against nonparties.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Uncertain): A Private Bounty Judgment.} State E authorizes any private person to sue anyone who assists a specified activity and to recover at least \$10,000 per violation, without proof of personal injury. A State E court, with personal jurisdiction over the defendant, enters such a judgment, and the plaintiff seeks to enforce it in State F, where the activity is lawful. \textit{Fauntleroy} forbids State F from refusing merely because the judgment offends its public policy. State F's best argument is the penal-judgment exception of \textit{Huntington v. Attrill}: the award looks like a fine for a public wrong. But the plaintiff is a private party collecting a civil award, which resembles the private recovery enforced in \textit{Fauntleroy}. Which side of that line a bounty judgment falls on is unsettled.

\end{enumerate}
```

The Governor declines, and the province asks a federal court for mandamus under \emph{Branstad}. The request fails. \emph{Branstad} enforces a duty created by Article IV and the Extradition Act, and those provisions govern demands by States and, under \S~3182, by Territories; they say nothing about foreign nations. Extradition to another country runs through treaties and the national government, not through a governor's duty under Article IV, so no ministerial obligation exists for a federal court to compel.

Its meaning is therefore a federal question for the Supreme Court, not a matter settled by one State's courts, and a single State may not impose new obligations on the commission that the compact's terms do not authorize (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/449/433/}{Justia}).

Because the line between a compact and parallel state statutes is unsettled, the outcome is uncertain.

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Right Applies): Citizenship Without Grand-Jury Indictment.} Congress enacts an organic statute for a newly acquired, unincorporated Pacific territory that grants U.S. citizenship to all residents and includes a statutory Bill of Rights omitting grand-jury indictment. A resident citizen is prosecuted for a felony without a grand jury and challenges the conviction under the Fifth Amendment. \textit{Analysis:} Under \textit{Balzac}, unless Congress expressly declared incorporation (or manifested an implication ``so strong as to exclude any other view''), citizenship alone does not incorporate the territory, and non-fundamental procedural guarantees like grand-jury indictment do not apply \textit{ex proprio vigore}.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Locality Controls): A Mainland-Born Citizen Tried in Porto Rico.} In 1920 a citizen born and raised in Ohio moves to San Juan to run a business. Charged under the insular penal code with a misdemeanor, he demands a jury, pointing out that he was a citizen of the United States long before the Jones Act and would have had a jury in Ohio. Under \textit{Balzac}, his birthplace makes no difference: it is the locality, not the status of the person, that determines whether the Constitution's jury guarantees apply in matters of judicial procedure. Because Porto Rico is unincorporated and the jury right is not among the fundamental rights that apply there of their own force, he may be tried without a jury, as Taft expressly noted (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/258/298/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Rights Apply): An Incorporated Territory, or a Fundamental Right.} Suppose instead that Congress enacts a statute expressly declaring a territory ``incorporated into the United States as a future State,'' or suppose the local government of an unincorporated territory authorizes warrantless, suspicionless searches of residents' homes or punishes political speech without due process. \textit{Contrast:} In the first scenario, express incorporation triggers the full Constitution---including Article~III and Sixth Amendment jury guarantees---just as in \textit{Rasmussen v. United States}. In the second scenario, even inside an unincorporated territory under \textit{Balzac}, ``fundamental personal rights'' such as due process and Fourth Amendment protections against unreasonable searches (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/442/465/}{\textit{Torres v. Puerto Rico}, 442 U.S. 465 (1979)}) apply directly to constrain territorial and federal action.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (\textit{Balzac} Not Extended): A Civilian Tried by Court-Martial Abroad.} During peacetime, the civilian wife of a U.S. soldier stationed overseas is charged with killing her husband on a military base and is tried by court-martial, without a jury or grand-jury indictment, under military law. The government invokes the \textit{Insular Cases} to argue that the jury guarantees do not follow citizens abroad. In \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/354/1/}{\textit{Reid v.\ Covert}, 354 U.S.\ 1 (1957)}, the Court rejected that argument on these facts and held that the civilian was entitled to the protections of Article~III and the Fifth and Sixth Amendments. The plurality declined to extend the \textit{Insular Cases}' territorial reasoning beyond its context of governing unincorporated territories.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Is \textit{Balzac} Still Good Law?): A Felony Bench Trial in an Unincorporated Territory Today.} A U.S. national living in an unincorporated Pacific territory is tried for a serious felony by a territorial court without a jury, as local law permits. She argues that the jury right is now recognized as fundamental. \textit{Balzac} squarely held that the jury right does not apply of its own force in unincorporated territories, and the Court has never overruled it. But \textit{Duncan v.\ Louisiana} later described the jury right as fundamental to the American scheme of justice, Justice Gorsuch in \textit{Vaello Madero} urged overruling the \textit{Insular Cases}, and the Court in \textit{Reid v.\ Covert} refused to extend them. A lower court would likely follow \textit{Balzac}, but the Supreme Court's answer is genuinely uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

```latex
\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Military Detention Permitted): A Citizen Captured on a Foreign Battlefield.} Second, if a U.S. citizen is captured bearing arms for enemy forces on an active foreign battlefield and Congress has authorized military force, \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/542/507/}{\textit{Hamdi v. A citizen who sold rifles to the militia, but never joined it, is tried by a commission. ### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)
```

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Habeas Review): A Border-Adjacent Credible-Fear Denial.} A Guatemalan migrant crosses the Rio Grande without inspection and is apprehended by Border Patrol 100 yards north of the river two hours later. After an asylum officer and immigration judge deny her credible-fear claim, she files a \S\ 2241 habeas petition alleging the officer misapplied the statutory ``particular social group'' standard and seeking a new asylum interview. Under \textit{Thuraissigiam}, the district court must dismiss for lack of jurisdiction under 8 U.S.C. \S\ 1252(e)(2). Because she seeks a new opportunity to gain admission rather than simple release from unlawful custody, the Suspension Clause does not apply, and because she was apprehended immediately after unlawful entry at the threshold of the border, statutory procedure satisfies due process.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Habeas Review): An Asylum Seeker at a Port of Entry.} A noncitizen without valid documents presents herself at a land port of entry and asks for asylum. She is placed in expedited removal, and an asylum officer and an immigration judge find no credible fear. In a habeas petition she argues that her interview was conducted through an interpreter who spoke a different dialect, and she asks for a new interview. Like Thuraissigiam, she seeks another chance at admission rather than simple release, so the Suspension Clause does not require review. And because she has never entered the United States, she has only the procedural rights Congress has provided by statute; the Court stressed that a clandestine entrant should not receive more process than such a port-of-entry applicant (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/591/19-161/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Habeas Available): Core Release From Interior Detention.} Suppose instead DHS arrests a noncitizen who has lived peacefully in Houston for eight years (or a U.S. citizen mistakenly flagged as an undocumented alien), detains him indefinitely without bond or notice of removal grounds, and invokes a new statute stripping all federal habeas review over executive detention orders. The detainee files a habeas petition demanding immediate release from unlawful physical confinement. Unlike \textit{Thuraissigiam}, this statute violates both the Suspension Clause and the Due Process Clause. The petitioner seeks \textit{simple release from unlawful executive detention}---the indisputable 1789 core of the Great Writ recognized in \textit{Thuraissigiam}, \textit{St. Cyr}, and \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/553/723/}{\textit{Boumediene}}; and an established U.S. resident (or citizen) inside the country holds full Fifth Amendment due process protections against arbitrary detention and summary removal.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Habeas Available): A Claim of Citizenship.} A man apprehended near the border is placed in expedited removal and ordered removed after a negative credible-fear finding. In a habeas petition he asserts that he is a United States citizen, born in Texas, and offers a birth certificate. Section 1252(e)(2) itself permits habeas review of whether the petitioner is an alien and whether he is a lawful permanent resident, refugee, or asylee, so the district court has jurisdiction to decide his citizenship claim. Nothing in \textit{Thuraissigiam} forecloses that inquiry: a citizen wrongly subjected to expedited removal is not an alien seeking initial admission, and his challenge is to the Executive's power to hold and remove him at all.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unresolved): Expedited Removal Deep in the Interior.} DHS arrests a noncitizen in Chicago who entered without inspection and has lived there for eighteen months, places him in expedited removal under the statute's two-year presence limit, and denies his credible-fear claim. He files a habeas petition arguing that the asylum officer applied the wrong legal standard and that he is entitled to full due process. The Suspension Clause holding cuts against him because he seeks further review rather than release. But the due process holding rested on Thuraissigiam's status at the threshold of initial entry, and the Court did not say when presence inside the country matures into an entry carrying fuller constitutional protection (see \textit{Zadvydas v.\ Davis}, 533 U.S.\ 678 (2001)). Whether eighteen months of residence is enough remains open.

\end{enumerate}
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Statute Controls): Revoking a Visa-Waiver Treaty.} The United States ratifies a bilateral treaty guaranteeing citizens of Country X visa-free entry for business visits. Two years later, citing economic competition and security fears, Congress enacts a statute barring all Country X nationals from entering the United States and voiding all previously issued visas. A Country X national detained at an airport with a valid visa seeks habeas corpus. Under \textit{Chae Chan Ping}, the habeas petition fails. Congress's later-in-time statute abrogates the conflicting treaty as domestic law, the visa is a revocable license rather than a vested property right, and Congress's exclusion decision is largely conclusive upon the Judiciary.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Later Statute Controls): A Tax Statute Overriding a Treaty.} A self-executing tax treaty between the United States and Country Y exempts Y's residents from U.S. tax on certain interest income. Congress later enacts a statute taxing that income for all foreign recipients, without mentioning the treaty. A Y resident sues for a refund, arguing that the treaty guarantees the exemption. Under the later-in-time rule that \textit{Chae Chan Ping} applied, a treaty and a federal statute stand on equal footing as the supreme law of the land, so ``the last expression of the sovereign will must control.'' The statute governs in U.S. courts; whether the United States has breached its international obligations is a matter for the political branches and diplomacy, not the courts (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/130/581/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Plenary Power Does Not Shield the Statute): Imprisonment at Hard Labor Without a Jury.} Suppose Congress enacts a statute authorizing immigration officers, without a jury trial or criminal indictment, to sentence noncitizens found unlawfully present in the interior to two years of imprisonment at hard labor prior to deportation. Under \textit{Wong Wing v. United States}, 163 U.S.\ 228 (1896), while Congress may detain or deport noncitizens administratively, imposing infamous criminal punishment inside the United States triggers full Fifth and Sixth Amendment criminal-procedure protections.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Deference to the Executive): A Proclamation Contrary to Statute.} Suppose the President issues an executive proclamation barring all asylum applications at the border in direct contradiction of an existing federal statute (8 U.S.C.\ \S\ 1158(a)(1)) granting a right to apply. \textit{Chae Chan Ping} vested plenary exclusion power in \textit{Congress} (the legislative department); when the President acts contrary to Congress's express statutory scheme, executive power is at its ``lowest ebb'' under \textit{Youngstown Sheet \& Tube Co.\ v. Sawyer}, and courts may enjoin ultra vires executive action without questioning Congress's sovereign authority.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Uncertain): A Statute Excluding Immigrants of One Religion.} Congress enacts a statute barring the admission of any immigrant who belongs to a named religion. A U.S. citizen whose spouse is denied a visa under the statute sues, invoking the Establishment and Equal Protection principles. \textit{Chae Chan Ping} treated Congress's exclusion judgments, even explicitly racial ones, as ``conclusive upon the judiciary,'' and \textit{Mandel} and \textit{Trump v. Hawaii} apply highly deferential review to entry decisions. But \textit{Trump v. Hawaii} emphasized that the proclamation there was neutral on its face, leaving open whether a facially religious exclusion would receive the same deference. Whether the plenary power tolerates an express religious exclusion has never been decided.

\end{enumerate}
```

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Proclamation Upheld): Suspending Tourist Visas After a Vetting Review.} Following a six-month interagency review finding that four foreign nations---three of which are predominantly Buddhist---lack biometric passport controls and fail to share criminal-record databases, the President issues a proclamation under 8 U.S.C. \S~1182(f) suspending nonimmigrant tourist visas from those four nations, with case-by-case hardship waivers. \item \textbf{Same-Side (Proclamation Upheld): An Open-Ended Suspension to Pressure a Foreign Government.} The President finds that a foreign government's refusal to accept the return of its nationals ordered removed from the United States harms U.S. interests, and under \S~1182(f) suspends the entry of that country's nationals as immigrants until the government begins cooperating. The suspension is lawful.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Order Invalid): A Domestic Policy Targeting Buddhist-Studies Programs.} By contrast, suppose the same President issues an executive order directing the Department of Education to revoke federal accreditation eligibility from all \textit{domestic} private colleges located in the United States that offer Buddhist-studies degree programs, citing a vague ``national cultural security'' concern after making identical campaign statements denigrating Buddhism. Hawaii}, this policy regulates domestic institutions and U.S. citizens within the United States rather than foreign-national entry at the border, and it lacks any statutory delegation akin to \S~1182(f). Without \textit{Mandel}'s border-entry deference, domestic Establishment Clause and Free Exercise scrutiny (\textit{Masterpiece Cakeshop}; \textit{Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye}) applies in full force, and the overt religious targeting and extrinsic evidence of animus render the order invalid.

Hawaii} does not save the proclamation, because the Court's analysis depended on what the policy did not do: Proclamation No.~9645 was neutral on its face, rested on a multi-agency review of foreign governments' practices, and excluded most of the world's Muslims. A proclamation that classifies by religion on its face announces the very ground the Establishment Clause forbids, so it cannot reasonably be understood to rest on a justification independent of unconstitutional grounds, even under rational-basis review.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Uncertain): Waivers That Exist Only on Paper.} A facially neutral \S~1182(f) proclamation restricts entry from several Muslim-majority countries and promises case-by-case waivers. In litigation, plaintiffs produce evidence that consular officers were told not to grant waivers, that almost none were granted, and that the agency review the proclamation cites had recommended lifting restrictions on two of the countries. Under the majority's approach, a court asks only whether the policy can reasonably be understood to rest on a legitimate national-security justification, and a neutral text plus an agency process ordinarily suffices.
```
