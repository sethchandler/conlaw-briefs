### Examples: Future Applications

```latex
\item \textbf{Same-Side (No State Action): A Medicaid-Funded Nursing Home Transfers a Resident.} A privately owned nursing home, licensed and closely regulated by the State and receiving most of its revenue from Medicaid, decides through its own physicians that a resident needs only a lower level of care and transfers her to another facility without a hearing. The State then reduces her Medicaid benefits to match the new placement. Under \textit{Jackson}, licensing, heavy regulation, and public funding do not make the home's decision the State's; the question is whether the State compelled or put its own weight behind the specific transfer, and here private medical judgment made it. \textit{Blum v. Yaretsky}, 457 U.S. 991 (1982), applied \textit{Jackson} to these facts and found no state action, so the resident has no Fourteenth Amendment due process claim against the home.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No State Action): A Franchised Internet Provider Cancels Service.} A privately owned broadband company holds an exclusive municipal franchise, strings its fiber along public rights-of-way, and is regulated by the state utility commission. It terminates a subscriber's service for disputed late payments without notice, under a termination clause in its filed terms of service that the commission never reviewed. Each of the subscriber's arguments fails as it did in \textit{Jackson}: regulation and a franchise do not supply a nexus to the specific termination; supplying internet service, like supplying electricity, has never been a power traditionally and exclusively reserved to the State; and the commission's passive acceptance of the filed terms does not put the State's weight behind the shutoff. Without state action, the subscriber's remedies lie in contract and state utility law.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (State Action): A Commission-Ordered Shutoff.}

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Likely State Action): A Utility Condemns an Easement.} The same kind of private electric company, using a power of eminent domain that state law delegates to certificated utilities, files a condemnation proceeding and takes a permanent easement across a homeowner's backyard for a new transmission line. The homeowner sues, claiming the taking was not for public use and that the procedures denied her due process. \textit{Jackson} itself marks this as the contrasting case: the Court said that if Metropolitan were exercising ``some power delegated to it by the State which is traditionally associated with sovereignty, such as eminent domain,'' the case ``would be quite a different one.'' Condemnation is a sovereign power the State has lent to the utility, so the utility's use of it is fairly attributable to the State.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Charter School Unresolved): Charter School vs.\ Prison Clinic.}

The school is the hard case. On one side, education has never been an exclusive state prerogative, and \textit{Rendell-Baker} held that a privately run school funded almost entirely by public money was not a state actor when it fired employees. On the other, a State that labels charter schools as public schools makes them part of its public education system, and controls their charters looks more like the entwined State in \textit{Brentwood}. Because the Court divided evenly in \textit{Drummond}, the outcome is open.
```

### Examples: Future Applications

```latex
\item \textbf{Same-Side (Formula Invalid): A Coverage Formula Pegged to Old History.} Congress enacts a statute requiring every State that used a poll tax in federal elections before 1964 to obtain federal approval before changing its voter-identification rules. The statute rests on that history alone, and the legislative record contains no current data comparing those States with others. Under \emph{Shelby County}, the formula fails: ``history did not end in 1965,'' the Fifteenth Amendment is not designed to punish for the past, and a law that treats States differently must justify its current burdens by current needs. Selecting States by practices long since abolished nationwide is exactly the kind of outdated criterion the Court held could no longer support preclearance.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Formula Invalid): Current Data That Does Not Track the Problem.} Congress responds to \emph{Shelby County} with a formula that uses only current data: any State in which overall turnout fell below 50\% in the most recent presidential election must preclear changes to its voting laws. The data are fresh, but the trigger measures low turnout of all voters rather than racial discrimination in voting. \emph{Shelby County}, applying \emph{Northwest Austin}, requires that a statute's ``disparate geographic coverage is sufficiently related to the problem that it targets.'' Because a turnout trigger could sweep in States with no record of discrimination and omit States with serious problems, a court would likely hold it insufficiently related to the Fifteenth Amendment harm that justifies so drastic a departure from equal sovereignty.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Formula Likely Valid): A Rolling Trigger Based on Adjudicated Violations.}

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Preclearance by Court Order): Judicial ``Bail-In'' After Intentional Discrimination.} After \emph{Shelby County}, voters sue a formerly covered county, and a federal court finds after trial that the county intentionally moved polling places to make voting harder for Black residents. The court invokes \S~3(c) of the Voting Rights Act, 52 U.S.C.\ \S~10302(c), and orders the county to obtain approval before making further voting changes for a period of years. \emph{Shelby County} does not bar the order: the Court invalidated only the \S~4(b) coverage formula and expressly left \S~5 and \S~2 in place. A bail-in order rests on a present-day judicial finding of a constitutional violation in that jurisdiction, not on decades-old data, so it answers the Court's demand that burdens be justified by current needs.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unresolved): Equal Sovereignty Outside Voting.} A federal statute bars States from authorizing new forms of sports wagering but allows two States that already licensed it before a set date to continue. A State excluded by the cutoff sues, arguing that the law violates the equal sovereignty of the States. \emph{Shelby County} said equal sovereignty ``remains highly pertinent in assessing subsequent disparate treatment of States,'' which supports the challenge. But the Court applied the principle to a law requiring some States to seek federal permission before legislating, and Justice Ginsburg's dissent, relying on \emph{Katzenbach}, read the equal-sovereignty doctrine as limited to the terms on which new States are admitted to the Union. Whether the principle reaches ordinary federal regulation that treats States differently, and what level of justification it demands, is genuinely unsettled.
```

### Examples: Future Applications

```latex
\item \textbf{Same-Side (Post-Deprivation Hearing Suffices): Reducing a Veteran's Disability Rating.} A federal agency reviews a veteran's service-connected disability rating, obtains new reports from his physicians and an agency-ordered examination, and sends him a notice summarizing the medical evidence, giving him sixty days to submit contrary evidence in writing. It then reduces his monthly payment, with a later evidentiary hearing and full retroactive relief if he wins. As in \textit{Eldridge}, the payments are not means-tested, the dispute turns on documented medical findings rather than credibility, and the written notice lets him respond to the agency's reasons. Under the three factors, written pre-reduction procedures plus a prompt post-reduction hearing satisfy due process.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Pre-Suspension Hearing): Suspending an Officer Charged With a Felony.} A state university suspends a campus police officer without pay immediately after he is arrested and formally charged with a drug felony, and gives him a hearing on his status only afterward. The officer's interest in his paycheck is real but temporary. The independent arrest and formal charge by third parties sharply reduce the risk that the suspension is baseless, and the State has a strong interest in not keeping a charged officer in a position of public trust. Applying \textit{Mathews}, the Court held on these facts in \textit{Gilbert v.\ Homar}, 520 U.S.\ 924 (1997), that no pre-suspension hearing was required, leaving for remand whether the later hearing was sufficiently prompt.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Pre-Termination Hearing Required): Cutting Off Subsistence Welfare on a Caseworker's Suspicion.} A state agency terminates a family's means-tested cash assistance after a caseworker concludes, based on a neighbor's report, that an unreported adult is living in the home and contributing income. The family denies it, and the issue turns on whom to believe. This is the \textit{Goldberg} pole that \textit{Eldridge} distinguished: the recipients are at the margin of subsistence, the question turns on lay facts and credibility that written submissions handle poorly, and wrongful interruption may leave them unable to survive while they wait. \textit{Goldberg v.\ Kelly} requires an oral pre-termination evidentiary hearing before benefits stop.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Pre-Termination Opportunity Required): Firing a Tenured Employee Without Notice.} A school board fires a tenured security guard on the day it discovers that he did not disclose a prior conviction on his job application, without telling him the charge or letting him explain. Applying the \textit{Mathews} factors, the Court held in \textit{Cleveland Board of Education v.\ Loudermill}, 470 U.S.\ 532 (1985), that a tenured public employee is entitled before termination to notice of the charges, an explanation of the employer's evidence, and an opportunity to respond. The interest in continued employment is weighty, a short pre-termination exchange may prevent a mistaken firing, and the burden on the employer is slight. A full evidentiary hearing may come afterward.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): Algorithmic Medicaid Cuts.}

The case is close because the first factor points toward \textit{Goldberg} while the second and third can be argued either way: whether a generic notice of an algorithmic score lets claimants respond meaningfully before the cut is exactly the question \textit{Eldridge}'s reliance on detailed written notice leaves open.
```

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Aid Invalid): Vouchers Redeemable Only at One Denomination's Schools.} A State offers tuition vouchers to low-income families but allows them to be redeemed only at schools affiliated with a single named church, on the theory that its schools have the best record in the district. Parents still choose whether to use the voucher, but the program is not neutral with respect to religion: the State itself, not independent private choice, has selected which religion's schools may receive the money. \textit{Zelman} protected Ohio's program because it was neutral and open to religious and secular schools alike; a program that prefers one faith fails that threshold requirement and violates the Establishment Clause (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/536/639/}{Justia}).

Here the circuit between government and religion is never broken: the State itself sends tax money to finance religious teaching and worship, so the advancement of religion is attributable to the government rather than to parents, and the program cannot claim \textit{Zelman}'s protection.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Uncertain): A Choice of Rehabilitation Programs With a Long Secular Waitlist.} A State lets nonviolent offenders avoid jail by completing a funded residential rehabilitation program of their choice. Most participating providers are faith-based programs that include worship, while the only secular provider has a six-month waitlist. The program is neutral on its face and the money follows each participant's choice, which points toward \textit{Zelman}. But \textit{Zelman} stressed that the choice must be genuine and that adequate secular options must be available, and an offender facing jail in the meantime may have what Justice Souter called a Hobson's choice. Whether a court counts the waitlisted secular option as a real alternative, and whether the coercive criminal setting matters, could decide the case.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Parents Prevail): Lessons Designed to Change Children's Religious Practices.} A district adopts a mandatory kindergarten-through-second-grade ``fairness'' unit in which teachers read stories portraying religious dietary and modesty rules as unfair to others and follow scripts telling children who mention such rules that they should rethink them. Religious parents demand notice and an opt-out, which the district refuses. The parents can argue that the lesson conveys a message contrary to their faith during formative years. The district can respond that twelve-year-olds are less impressionable than \textit{Mahmoud}'s five-to-eleven-year-olds and that the unit is informational ``exposure'' rather than the ``unmistakably normative'' instruction the Court condemned. Where the line falls is unsettled.
