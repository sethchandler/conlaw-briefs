## 166_ex_parte_mccardle

### Memory Jogger

```latex
Congress may withdraw a statutory route of Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction even after a case has been argued: a unanimous Court dismissed a Reconstruction-era habeas appeal once Congress repealed the 1867 appellate provision, while noting that its older habeas jurisdiction under the Judiciary Act of 1789 remained intact (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/74/506/}{Justia}).
```

### Judicial Votes

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Jurisdiction Strip Upheld): Repealing a Direct-Appeal Route While Certiorari Remains.} Congress repeals a statute that allowed parties in a class of federal enforcement cases to appeal directly from a district court to the Supreme Court, makes the repeal applicable to appeals already docketed, and leaves in place the ordinary route of appeal to a court of appeals followed by certiorari. Like the 1868 Act, the repeal withdraws one statutory avenue of review in express terms without telling the Court how to decide any case. Under \textit{McCardle}'s rule that a repealed jurisdictional statute is treated as if it never existed, the pending direct appeals must be dismissed, and litigants still have another door into the Court (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/74/506/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Jurisdiction Strip Upheld): Barring Certiorari Review of Interlocutory Class-Certification Rulings.} Congress provides that the Supreme Court may not grant certiorari to review a court of appeals' decision on an interlocutory appeal from a class-certification order, but leaves full review available after final judgment. The statute regulates the timing and route of review rather than closing the Court to a category of cases, which places it comfortably within the Exceptions and Regulations Clause as \textit{McCardle} read it. The pattern matches \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/518/651/}{\textit{Felker v. Turpin}, 518 U.S. 651 (1996)}, which upheld a bar on certiorari review of habeas gatekeeping orders because another route to the Court remained open.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Strip Likely Invalid): An Outcome-Selective Strip of All Review.} Congress enacts the ``Uniform Flag Protection Act,'' making flag burning a federal crime and adding Section 5: ``Pursuant to Article III, Section 2, the Supreme Court shall have no appellate jurisdiction, by certiorari, appeal, or original writ of habeas corpus, to review any federal or state court decision upholding a conviction under this Act, including cases currently pending.'' A defendant convicted in state court appeals to the U.S.\ Supreme Court, which already granted certiorari before Section 5 passed. Unlike \textit{McCardle} and \textit{Felker}, Section 5 forecloses \emph{all} Supreme Court review (eliminating the \textit{Yerger} escape valve) and discriminates asymmetrically by stripping jurisdiction only over decisions \emph{upholding} convictions (raising \textit{Klein} rule-of-decision, Due Process, and First Amendment viewpoint-discrimination objections, as well as a Suspension Clause challenge to stripping habeas). Even if a court rejects the ``essential functions'' thesis under \textit{McCardle}'s plain-text rationale, Section 5 is vulnerable under external constitutional limits (First and Fifth Amendments and the Suspension Clause) and \textit{Klein}'s prohibition on Congress rigging outcomes while leaving courts open to decide in the government's favor.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Strip Invalid): Eliminating Habeas Review for Detainees Without an Adequate Substitute.} Without suspending the writ, Congress provides that no court shall have jurisdiction to hear habeas petitions from noncitizens held as enemy combatants at a U.S. naval base abroad over which the United States exercises complete control, and substitutes only a limited military status review in which detainees cannot present exculpatory evidence gathered after the hearing. \textit{McCardle} does not save the statute: the Exceptions Clause power is subject to the Constitution's other commands, and \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/553/723/}{\textit{Boumediene v. Bush}, 553 U.S. 723 (2008)}, held that a strip of this kind violates the Suspension Clause unless Congress provides an adequate substitute for habeas.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unresolved): Stripping All Supreme Court Review of a Constitutional Subject.} Congress provides that the Supreme Court shall have no appellate jurisdiction, by any route, over cases challenging state laws on prayer in public schools, while leaving state courts and lower federal courts free to decide such cases. Read literally, \textit{McCardle}'s statement that the power to make exceptions ``is given by express words'' supports the statute, and the law is neutral on its face: it neither prescribes a rule of decision (\textit{Klein}) nor suspends habeas (\textit{Boumediene}). Unlike the 1868 Act, however, it leaves no alternative route to the Supreme Court and invites fifty state supreme courts to reach different answers on a federal constitutional question, squarely presenting Henry Hart's argument that exceptions may not destroy the Court's essential role in keeping federal law supreme and uniform. The Court has never decided whether Congress may close every door, so the outcome is genuinely uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

### Memory Jogger

```latex
Facing Arkansas's open defiance of \textit{Brown}, all nine Justices signed an opinion holding that disorder provoked by state officials cannot justify suspending a desegregation plan, and declaring that the Supreme Court's interpretation of the Fourteenth Amendment is ``the supreme law of the land'' binding every state official (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/358/1/}{Justia}).
```

\item \textbf{Opinion of the Court:}

\item \textbf{Concurring Opinion:} Justice Frankfurter filed a concurring opinion (358 U.S. at 20--26).
    \item \textbf{Dissenting Opinions:} None.
\end{itemize}

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Official Bound by the Court's Interpretation): A Non-Party Governor Refuses to Follow a Fourteenth Amendment Precedent.} After the Supreme Court holds in \textit{State A v. Doe} that a state ban on a particular medical procedure violates the Fourteenth Amendment, the Governor of State B (not a party to \textit{Doe}) signs an executive order directing state prosecutors to continue enforcing State B's identical statute, declaring: ``My oath is to the Constitution, not to five Justices' mistaken reading of it; I will obey a federal court injunction directed at me, but until then I am not bound by \textit{Doe}.'' Under \textit{Cooper v. Aaron}'s strong judicial-supremacy framework, the Governor's refusal to treat the Court's Fourteenth Amendment holding as binding law violates Article VI and the Oath Clause; moreover, in any federal suit against State B's officials, lower federal courts are bound by vertical \textit{stare decisis} to enjoin enforcement immediately (and state officials face potential fee-shifting under 42 U.S.C.\ \S~1988 or loss of qualified immunity because the right is clearly established). Under a \textbf{departmentalist} view (invoking Lincoln's response to \textit{Dred Scott}), the Governor distinguishes between defying a direct court judgment (unlawful) and declining to treat a precedent involving other parties as automatically settling his independent constitutional judgment while seeking a test case to ask the Court to reconsider \textit{Doe}.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (No Excuse for Official Resistance): A County Clerk Stops Issuing Marriage Licenses.} After the Supreme Court holds that the Fourteenth Amendment requires States to license same-sex marriages, an elected county clerk stops issuing marriage licenses altogether, citing her personal objections and the fact that the decision arose from litigation against other States' officials. A federal court orders her to resume issuing licenses, and she refuses. Under \textit{Cooper}, the clerk acts for the State, is bound by the Court's interpretation through her Article VI oath, and cannot confine the ruling to the parties who litigated it; her disagreement, like the Arkansas officials' resistance, is no legal excuse, and continued refusal is punishable as contempt.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Independent Judgment Permitted): A President Vetoes a Bill the Court Would Uphold.} Congress passes a bill chartering a national development bank. Supreme Court precedent establishes that Congress may charter such a bank, but the President vetoes the bill on the ground that, in his own judgment, it exceeds Congress's enumerated powers. \textit{Cooper} does not reach this situation: the veto defies no judgment and denies no right the Court has declared, and Andrew Jackson's 1832 veto of the Second Bank's recharter rested on exactly this reasoning. Even on a strong reading of \textit{Cooper}, the Court's interpretations bind officials who would act against declared constitutional rights; they do not oblige political officials to support every measure the Court has found permissible.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Supreme Court Interpretation to Obey): State Officials Decline to Follow Another State's District Court Ruling.} A federal district court in State C holds State C's voter-identification law unconstitutional and enjoins its enforcement. Officials in State D, which has a similar law, keep enforcing it. \textit{Cooper}'s Article VI reasoning attaches to the Supreme Court's exposition of the Constitution, not to every lower-court opinion: the State C judgment binds only the parties to that case, and its reasoning is at most persuasive authority in State D. State D's officials are not ``war[ring] against the Constitution'' by continuing to enforce their law until a court with authority over them rules.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Defiance or Legitimate Test Case?): A State Enacts a Law Designed to Invite Overruling.} A state legislature, persuaded that a Supreme Court precedent was wrongly decided, enacts a statute that conflicts with the precedent's core holding and openly announces that its purpose is to give the Court a vehicle to reconsider it. Read broadly, \textit{Cooper} condemns any legislator who would ``war against the Constitution'' as the Court has interpreted it, and the statute resembles the ``evasive schemes'' the Court denounced. Read alongside Justice Frankfurter's concurrence, which distinguished orderly efforts to change the law from defiance of it, the statute is a lawful request for reconsideration---the posture of the Mississippi law that led the Court to overrule \textit{Roe v. Wade} in \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/597/19-1392/}{\textit{Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization}, 597 U.S. 215 (2022)}. Where testing a precedent ends and nullifying it begins remains unsettled.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 168_abbott_laboratories_v_gardner

### Memory Jogger

```latex
Drug makers sued to block an FDA ``every time'' generic-name labeling rule before any enforcement; the Court held 5--3 that final agency action is presumptively reviewable and that a pre-enforcement challenge is ripe when the issue is fit for decision and withholding review would cause hardship (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/387/136/}{Justia}).
```

### Judicial Votes

```latex
The Supreme Court \textbf{reversed and remanded} by a \textbf{5--3} vote. The case was argued on January 16, 1967, and decided on May 22, 1967 (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/387/136/}{Justia}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority Opinion:} Justice Harlan delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Justices Black, Douglas, Stewart, and White.
    \item \textbf{Concurring Opinions:} None.
    \item \textbf{Dissenting Opinions:} Justice Fortas filed a dissenting opinion, joined by Chief Justice Warren and Justice Clark, published in the companion case \textit{Gardner v.\ Toilet Goods Ass'n, Inc.}, 387 U.S.\ 167, 174 (1967). Justice Clark also filed a separate dissenting opinion (387 U.S.\ at 201).
    \item \textbf{Not Participating:} Justice Brennan took no part in the consideration or decision of the case.
\end{itemize}
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Ripe): A Self-Executing Data-Deletion Rule.} Suppose Congress enacts the Federal Digital Privacy Act authorizing the FTC to promulgate rules prohibiting ``deceptive data-brokerage practices.'' After notice and comment, the FTC issues a final rule, effective in 30 days, under which \textbf{Rule A} mandates that every data broker overhaul its database architecture to delete consumer location histories every 24 hours, backed by civil penalties of \$50,000 per violation. A trade association sues before any enforcement, arguing that the rule exceeds the FTC's statutory authority. Under \textit{Abbott Laboratories}, \textbf{Rule A is ripe}: the statutory-authority question is purely legal, the rule is final agency action, and brokers face the classic compliance-or-penalty dilemma requiring immediate, costly operational overhauls.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Ripe): A Mandatory Equipment-Installation Rule.} After notice and comment, a federal transportation agency issues a final rule requiring every interstate trucking company to install a specified electronic monitoring device in each truck within 90 days, with civil penalties for each noncompliant vehicle. Carriers sue at once, arguing only that the governing statute does not authorize the mandate. As in \textit{Abbott}, the issue is a purely legal question of statutory construction, the rule is formally promulgated and immediately effective, and the carriers must either spend heavily on equipment now or risk penalties later. Nothing in the statute shows by clear and convincing evidence that Congress meant to bar pre-enforcement review, so the challenge may proceed in district court.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Unripe): A Discretionary Audit-and-Suspension Rule.} Return to the data-privacy hypothetical, but suppose the FTC's rule is instead \textbf{Rule B} announces that the FTC ``may, in its discretion, audit the algorithmic training logs of any data broker suspected of non-compliance'' and ``may suspend the broker's federal registry license'' if the broker refuses access. Under \textit{Toilet Goods}, \textbf{Rule B is unripe}: enforcement is discretionary and contingent on future factual circumstances (whether and how an audit is demanded), and the rule imposes no immediate change in primary conduct pending an actual audit request. Students should also note the post-\textit{Driehaus}/\textit{Lexmark} nuance: if the brokers can establish an imminent Article III injury-in-fact as to Rule B, they may argue that prudential ripeness cannot independently bar a court from exercising statutory jurisdiction.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Review Channeled Elsewhere): A Statutory Review Scheme Displaces District Court Suit.} A federal mine-safety statute directs that challenges to agency orders be heard first by an independent review commission, with review afterward in a court of appeals. Before any citation issues, a mine operator sues in district court to enjoin the agency from enforcing a new compliance requirement. Although \textit{Abbott} presumes that final agency action is reviewable, the presumption yields when Congress's scheme shows an intent to channel review elsewhere. In \textit{Thunder Basin Coal Co.\ v.\ Reich}, 510 U.S.\ 200 (1994), the Court held that a comprehensive review structure of this kind implicitly precluded district court jurisdiction, so the operator must raise its objections through the statutory path.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): An Agency Guidance Document Backed by Warning Letters.} A federal agency publishes a guidance document, without notice and comment, stating its view that certain dietary supplements are unapproved drugs, and sends warning letters to several sellers saying that it ``may'' take enforcement action. The sellers sue, arguing that the products are not drugs. The question is largely legal, and sellers face pressure to pull products or risk seizure. But the guidance may not be final agency action, because it does not itself fix legal obligations, and the agency retains discretion whether to act---the features that made \textit{Toilet Goods} unripe. Whether a court treats the guidance as the agency's settled position imposing immediate hardship (as in \textit{Abbott}) or as a tentative statement awaiting a concrete enforcement setting could decide the case.

\end{enumerate}
```

### Memory Jogger

```latex
When President Wilson fired Portland postmaster Frank Myers without the Senate consent an 1876 statute required, Chief Justice Taft's 6--3 opinion held that Article II gives the President an exclusive, illimitable power to remove executive officers he appoints with Senate consent, and that Congress may not reserve a share of removal to the Senate (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/272/52/}{Justia}).

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Removal Restriction Invalid): For-Cause Protection for the Commissioners of a Federal AI Commission.} Congress enacts the Artificial Intelligence Safety Act of 2026, establishing the five-member Federal AI Commission (FAIC), appointed by the President with Senate confirmation for staggered seven-year terms, with no more than three members from the same political party. The FAIC is empowered to promulgate binding safety rules for frontier AI models, investigate technology firms, adjudicate violations in-house, and seek billion-dollar civil penalties in federal court. The statute provides two removal clauses: \textbf{Clause 1:} ``Commissioners may be removed by the President only for inefficiency, neglect of duty, or malfeasance in office.'' \textbf{Clause 2:} ``The FAIC's Chief Inspector---an inferior officer appointed by the FAIC Chair---may be removed by the Chair only upon a showing of good cause.'' The President fires two FAIC Commissioners solely for policy disagreements and orders the new Chair to fire the Chief Inspector at will. Both the Commissioners and the Chief Inspector sue.

Under \textit{Myers v.\ United States} as fully vindicated by \textit{Trump v.\ Slaughter} (2026), \textbf{Clause 1 is plainly unconstitutional}: the FAIC wields quintessential executive powers (binding rulemaking, investigations, in-house adjudication, and civil-penalty litigation), so its principal officers must be removable by the President at will, and \textit{Humphrey's Executor} is no longer good law. Nor can the FAIC claim the \textit{Trump v.\ Cook} historical carve-out applicable to the Federal Reserve.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Removal Restriction Invalid): Congress Reserves Removal to Itself.} A statute creates a Comptroller of Federal Spending who certifies the amount of automatic budget cuts that the President must then order, and provides that the Comptroller may be removed only by joint resolution of Congress. Even on the narrow reading of \textit{Myers} adopted in \textit{Humphrey's Executor}, its core prohibition on congressional participation in removal survives: Congress may not draw to itself, or to either House, a power to remove an officer charged with executing the laws. \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/478/714/}{\textit{Bowsher v.\ Synar}, 478 U.S.\ 714 (1986)}, invalidated a materially identical arrangement on exactly this ground, because Congress's control over removal would make the officer its subordinate.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Restriction Likely Valid): An Inferior Officer Appointed and Protected by a Department Head.} Congress vests the appointment of a naval engineering officer in the Secretary of the Navy and provides that the officer may be dismissed in peacetime only by sentence of a court-martial; the Secretary serves at the President's pleasure. \textit{Myers} itself distinguished this situation: when Congress vests an inferior officer's appointment in a head of department under the Excepting Clause, it may limit that department head's power to remove, as \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/116/483/}{\textit{United States v.\ Perkins}, 116 U.S.\ 483 (1886)}, held. Congress claims no share of removal for itself or the Senate, and the President still controls the Secretary, so the officer's tenure protection stands.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Myers Not Triggered): Civil-Service Protection for a Non-Officer Employee.} A claims examiner in a federal agency, hired through competitive civil-service procedures, applies agency guidelines to routine benefit claims, exercises no significant authority under federal law, and by statute may be dismissed only for misconduct or poor performance after notice and a hearing before a personnel board. \textit{Myers} concerns the removal of \emph{officers} through whom the President executes the laws; an ordinary employee is not an officer under \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/424/1/}{\textit{Buckley v.\ Valeo}, 424 U.S.\ 1 (1976)}, and \textit{Free Enterprise Fund} expressly declined to cast doubt on the civil-service system. The President's control runs through the agency's officers, who remain answerable to him, so the examiner's protection is valid.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unresolved): Single-Layer Protection for the Commission's Chief Inspector.} Return to the Federal AI Commission and its Chief Inspector under Clause 2. As to \textbf{Clause 2}, the Chief Inspector is an inferior officer whose appointment Congress vested in a Head of Department (the Commission/Chair) under Article II, \S~2's Excepting Clause. Under \textit{Myers}'s own discussion of \textit{United States v.\ Perkins}, 116 U.S.\ 483 (1886) (and \textit{Morrison v.\ Olson}), Congress may impose a \emph{single} layer of for-cause protection on an inferior officer appointed by a department head, provided the department head is removable at will by the President (avoiding the unconstitutional \emph{dual-layer} tenure problem of \textit{Free Enterprise Fund}). Once Clause 1 is severed under \textit{Slaughter}, the Chair is removable at will, meaning Clause 2 creates only a single layer of protection---though challengers could still argue under a maximalist reading of \textit{Myers} and \textit{Slaughter} that even single-layer limits on inferior officers exercising core enforcement power impair the President's Take Care duty.

\end{enumerate}
```

### Memory Jogger

```latex
In the ``sick chicken case,'' a unanimous Court reversed a Brooklyn kosher slaughterhouse's convictions under a New Deal poultry code, holding that NIRA \S\,3 unconstitutionally delegated legislative power to the President and trade groups and that the code's reach to purely local slaughtering and sales exceeded the commerce power (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/295/495/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Majority Opinion:} Chief Justice Hughes delivered the opinion of the Court

\item \textbf{Concurring Opinion:} Justice Cardozo filed a concurring opinion, joined by Justice Stone.
    \item \textbf{Dissenting Opinions:} None.

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Delegation Invalid): Industry-Drafted AI Codes Backed by Criminal Penalties.} Suppose Congress enacts the ``Artificial Intelligence Stabilization Act of 2026,'' authorizing the President to approve ``Codes of Safe AI Competition'' drafted by a consortium of major AI laboratories whenever the President finds a code ``tends to promote innovation, national competitiveness, and public safety.'' Violations carry criminal fines up to \$100,000 per day, and the President's approval is exempted from the Administrative Procedure Act and judicial review. A small open-source AI startup indicted for violating a code's compute-reporting rule challenges the statute. Directly tracks \textit{Schechter Poultry} and \textit{Carter Coal}: (1) standardless, conflicting statutory aims (``innovation'' vs.\ ``public safety''); (2) private delegation to self-interested industry incumbents with no binding agency oversight (distinguishing \textit{Consumers' Research} and \textit{Sunshine Anthracite}); (3) elimination of APA procedural safeguards and judicial review; and (4) criminal penalties across a transformative economic sector (triggering both \textit{Schechter} and, if ambiguous, the major questions doctrine under \textit{West Virginia v. EPA} and \textit{Learning Resources}).

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Delegation Invalid): Letting a Majority of Producers Bind the Whole Industry.} Congress provides that producers responsible for two-thirds of a mineral's annual output, together with a majority of the industry's workers, may fix maximum hours and minimum wages that bind every producer in the country, including firms that opposed the terms. A small producer prosecuted for paying below the agreed scale challenges the law. As in \textit{Schechter}, coercive lawmaking power has been handed to private parties whose interests may be adverse to their competitors', with no public official reviewing the rules. The Court struck down a nearly identical scheme in \textit{Carter v. Carter Coal Co.}, 298 U.S.\ 238 (1936), building on \textit{Schechter}'s condemnation of delegation to trade associations.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Delegation Valid): Health-Based Air Quality Standards.} Congress directs an environmental agency to set nationwide ambient air standards at the level needed to protect public health with an adequate margin of safety, after notice-and-comment rulemaking subject to judicial review. Industry groups argue the standard gives the agency open-ended discretion over the economy. Unlike NIRA \S\,3, the statute names a single objective, assigns the task to a public agency rather than private trade groups, and channels the agency through procedures and court review. Under the intelligible-principle test, the delegation is valid, as the Court held in \textit{Whitman v. American Trucking Ass'ns}, 531 U.S.\ 457 (2001).

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Commerce Power Sustained): A Federal Minimum Wage for Local Slaughterhouse Workers.} Congress applies a federal minimum-wage and overtime law to slaughterhouse employees nationwide, including a firm that, like the Schechters, buys birds shipped from other states and sells only to local butchers. Under \textit{Schechter}'s direct/indirect line, the law could not reach such local labor. Modern doctrine has abandoned that line: \textit{United States v.\ Darby}, 312 U.S.\ 100 (1941), sustained federal wage-and-hour regulation, and \textit{Wickard v.\ Filburn}, 317 U.S.\ 111 (1942), allows Congress to regulate local economic activity whose aggregate effect on interstate commerce is substantial. The challenger answers that, as with NIRA's codes, the statute lets an executive official write criminal law for an entire sector under open-ended goals, the concern pressed by Justice Gorsuch's dissents in \textit{Gundy} and \textit{Consumers' Research}. Because the Court has upheld similarly general standards yet several Justices want stricter review of delegations backed by criminal sanctions, the outcome is uncertain.

\end{enumerate}

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Delegation Upheld): A Presidential Ban on Advanced-Chip Exports.} Suppose Congress passes the ``Global Semiconductor Security Act,'' authorizing the President, upon declaring a ``geopolitical supply-chain threat,'' to ``prohibit, license, or impose financial conditions on the export of advanced chips to any foreign nation if the President determines such action will advance U.S.\ strategic stability.'' A U.S.\ chipmaker indicted for exporting to Country~X and assessed the fee on shipments to Country~Y challenges both measures. As to the export ban: Strong \textit{Curtiss-Wright} Zone~1 territory. Like the 1934 Chaco arms embargo, the statute targets a defined class of strategic goods in external relations, requires a presidential finding tied to foreign strategic stability, and implicates confidential national-security assessments. Under \textit{Curtiss-Wright} and Justice Kavanaugh's concurrence in \textit{Consumers' Research} (2025), a nondelegation challenge to the export ban will almost certainly fail.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Delegation Upheld): Sanctions on a Foreign Regime That Attacks Its Neighbor.} Congress authorizes the President, upon finding that a foreign government has invaded a neighboring state, to prohibit U.S.\ persons from selling aircraft parts to that government, subject to such exceptions as the President prescribes, and to lift the ban when he finds hostilities have ended. The President activates the ban, and an exporter prosecuted for a later shipment argues that Congress gave the President unbounded discretion. The statute is a near copy of the 1934 Joint Resolution: it addresses a situation ``entirely external to the United States,'' turns on diplomatic judgments best made by the Executive, and follows a long tradition of embargo statutes. Under \textit{Curtiss-Wright}, the breadth of the President's discretion is no constitutional defect, and the prosecution proceeds.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Delegation Does Not Save the Measure): An Export Fee Barred by the Constitution.} Same statute, but the chipmaker challenges the 20\% fee on its shipments to Country~Y. Dramatically weaker for the President. First, even under \textit{Curtiss-Wright}, Sutherland expressly stated that executive foreign-affairs power ``must be exercised in subordination to the applicable provisions of the Constitution'' (299 U.S.\ at 320)---and the Export Clause (Art.\ I, \S\,9, cl.\ 5) flatly forbids taxes or duties on exports (a constitutional defect Chief Justice Roberts highlighted in \textit{Learning Resources} (2026)). Second, even if characterized as a regulatory condition rather than a tax, the chipmaker will invoke \textit{Learning Resources} (2026) and \textit{Zivotofsky} (2015) to argue that ``financial conditions'' does not clearly authorize revenue-raising exactions and that \textit{Curtiss-Wright} cannot override explicit Article~I limits.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Statute, No ``Sole Organ'' Power): A Presidential Order Binding State Courts.} After an international tribunal rules that the United States violated a treaty in a state criminal prosecution, the President, acting without any statute, issues a memorandum directing state courts to reopen the affected convictions. The treaty is not self-executing, and Congress has passed no implementing law. The Executive cites \textit{Curtiss-Wright}'s description of the President as ``sole organ'' in foreign affairs. That argument fails: \textit{Curtiss-Wright} upheld presidential action taken \emph{under} an Act of Congress, and Justice Jackson's \textit{Youngstown} concurrence treats it as a case of maximum, congressionally authorized power. On materially similar facts, \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/552/491/}{\textit{Medell\'in v. Texas}, 552 U.S. 491 (2008)}, held that the President could not unilaterally convert a non-self-executing treaty obligation into domestic law binding state courts.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Foreign Threat, Domestic Effects): Banning a Foreign-Owned App.} Congress authorizes the President, upon finding that a foreign-controlled software application poses a national-security threat, to prohibit its distribution in the United States on such terms as the President deems appropriate. The President bans a popular foreign-owned messaging app used daily by millions of Americans. The case for \textit{Curtiss-Wright} deference is real: the finding rests on confidential intelligence about a foreign government, a matter in which the Executive has the confidential information, secrecy, and dispatch that Sutherland stressed. But the ban operates almost entirely on domestic commerce and domestic speech, which pushes it toward the internal sphere where \textit{Panama Refining} and \textit{Schechter} demand meaningful statutory standards, and Sutherland himself said foreign-affairs power remains subject to the Constitution's other provisions, including the First Amendment. Whether a court treats the delegation as external or internal---and how much weight it gives the speech interests---could decide the case.

\end{enumerate}

## 172_ins_v_chadha

### Memory Jogger

```latex
\textit{Chadha} struck down the one-House legislative veto: once Congress delegates authority to the Executive, it may alter the legal rights and duties of persons outside the Legislative Branch only by bicameral passage and presentment to the President (7--2) (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/462/919/}{Justia}).

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Veto Invalid): A Two-House Disapproval Resolution With an Inseverability Clause.} Congress enacts the Artificial Intelligence Safety Act of 2026, authorizing the Secretary of Commerce to issue binding national safety standards for frontier AI models. Section 12 provides that any standard shall take effect 60 days after submission to Congress unless both the House and Senate pass a concurrent resolution of disapproval within that window. Section 13 adds that if \S~12 is held invalid, the Secretary's rulemaking authority under the Act shall automatically terminate. The Secretary issues a binding rule; both Houses pass a concurrent resolution disapproving it without presenting it to the President. An AI developer sues to enforce the disapproval (or a safety group sues to enforce the rule). Under \textit{Chadha} and \textit{Process Gas}, even a \emph{two-House} concurrent resolution violates Presentment (Art.~I, \S~7, cl.~3) because it alters legal rights and duties outside Congress without presidential presentment. However, unlike \textit{Chadha} and \textit{Alaska Airlines}, \S~13 contains an express \emph{inseverability} (fallback) clause, meaning a court must honor Congress's explicit text and invalidate the Secretary's underlying rulemaking authority as well.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Veto Invalid): A Committee Approval Requirement for Federal Land Sales.} A statute authorizes the Secretary of the Interior to sell surplus federal land, but provides that no sale may close unless the House and Senate committees with jurisdiction over public lands first approve it by committee resolution. The committees refuse to approve a sale the Secretary has negotiated, and the buyer sues. Under \textit{Chadha}, the committees' refusal has the purpose and effect of altering the legal rights of the Secretary and the buyer, persons outside the Legislative Branch, so it is legislative action that must pass both Houses and be presented to the President. A committee veto is even further removed from Article I, \S~7 than the one-House veto in \textit{Chadha}. Under \textit{Alaska Airlines}, the court would presume the approval clause severable and let the sale proceed.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Valid): A Report-and-Wait Provision With Disapproval by Joint Resolution.} A statute requires an agency to submit each new rule to Congress and provides that the rule may not take effect for 60 days; during that time Congress may block the rule only by passing a joint resolution of disapproval through both Houses and presenting it to the President. Nothing in this scheme lets Congress change anyone's legal rights without bicameralism and presentment. The waiting period merely gives Congress time to legislate, and any disapproval becomes law only through the Article I, \S~7 process. This is the kind of report-and-wait mechanism the Court treated as ``fully operative'' when it severed the veto from \S~244, and it is the model Congress later followed in the Congressional Review Act.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Valid): The Senate Rejects a Nominee by a One-House Vote.} The President nominates a candidate to head a federal agency. The Senate votes to reject the nomination, and the nominee sues, arguing that under \textit{Chadha} a single House cannot take binding action that alters the legal rights of persons outside Congress. The claim fails. \textit{Chadha} recognized that the Framers expressly authorized certain unicameral actions with binding effect, including the Senate's power to give or withhold consent to appointments under Article II, \S~2, cl.~2. Those textual exceptions are precisely what made the Court conclude that no others may be implied; they do not cast doubt on the exceptions themselves. The Senate's vote is a constitutionally prescribed act, not an exercise of lawmaking power that bypasses presentment.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unsettled): Requiring Affirmative Congressional Approval Before Major Rules Take Effect.} A statute provides that no ``major rule'' issued by any agency may take effect unless, within 70 legislative days, both Houses pass and the President signs a joint resolution approving it; if either House fails to act, the rule never takes effect. Defenders argue that the scheme complies with \textit{Chadha}: Congress has simply delegated authority to propose rules, and every rule that becomes binding passes through bicameralism and presentment. Critics answer that one House's inaction now has the same effect as the one-House veto struck down in \textit{Chadha}, because a single chamber can defeat an exercise of delegated power that the other House and the President support. Because the Court has not decided whether Congress may condition a delegation on later approval in this way, the outcome is uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Cancellation Invalid): An Earmark-Cancellation Statute.} Congress authorizes the President, within ten days after signing any appropriations act, to cancel any congressionally directed spending item (an earmark) that he finds wasteful, with the canceled item having no legal force unless Congress passes a disapproval bill. A city whose earmarked grant for a water-treatment plant is canceled sues. The statute repeats the defect in the Line Item Veto Act: the President acts after the bill has become law, strikes only part of it, and relies on the same facts Congress had just weighed. Under \textit{Clinton}, that is a unilateral partial repeal outside Article I, \S~7, and the city's lost grant is the kind of concrete injury that gave New York standing (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/524/417/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Cancellation Invalid): Suspending Targeted Tax Credits.} A statute empowers the Secretary of the Treasury, within thirty days after any tax bill is enacted, to declare that any tax credit benefiting fewer than one hundred taxpayers shall not take effect if the Secretary concludes it is poor policy. A manufacturer that had negotiated a plant purchase in reliance on a newly enacted credit sues after the credit is voided. The power operates on enacted text, is discretionary, and rests on a policy disagreement with Congress rather than on any later-arising contingency, so it fails the three distinctions the majority drew from \textit{Field v. Clark}. Like the cancellation of \S~968 that injured Snake River, it is the functional equivalent of repealing part of a statute, and it must fall under \textit{Clinton}.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Valid): Expedited Rescission Through Bicameralism and Presentment.} Congress adopts procedural rules under which the President may send a package of proposed spending rescissions within days of signing an appropriations act; each House must hold a prompt up-or-down vote without amendment, and a rescission takes effect only if both Houses pass the rescission bill and the President signs it. Nothing in \textit{Clinton} forbids this. The rescission becomes law exactly as Article I, \S~7 requires---passed by both Houses and presented to the President---so no statute is altered by the President alone. The fast-track rules simply change how Congress schedules its own votes, which each House may do in setting its rules of proceedings.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Valid): Contingent Tariff Retaliation.} A trade statute sets duties on imported steel and directs the President to raise them by a fixed percentage on imports from any country that he finds, after enactment, has imposed discriminatory duties on American goods. A foreign exporter argues that the statute is a line-item veto in disguise. It is not. As in \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/143/649/}{\textit{Field v. Clark}, 143 U.S. 649 (1892)}, the President's action depends on facts arising after enactment, he must act once he finds the contingency, and he carries out the policy Congress chose rather than rejecting it. \textit{Clinton} expressly distinguished this kind of contingent legislation, so the statute survives a Presentment Clause challenge.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): Recasting Cancellation as Permissive Spending.} To curb deficits without triggering \textit{Clinton}, Congress passes the Fiscal Restraint Act of 2026, providing that every future federal appropriations statute shall be deemed to appropriate ``sums not exceeding'' the listed amounts, and authorizing the President, within 10 days of signing any appropriations bill, to issue a ``non-expenditure directive'' permanently barring any agency from obligating designated line items whenever the President finds that non-expenditure will ``promote fiscal discipline.'' The statute expressly states that it ``does not repeal or amend any statutory text.'' President X signs an infrastructure bill and immediately issues a non-expenditure directive blocking \$500 million earmarked for a state port authority. The port authority sues. The Government will invoke Justice Scalia's partial dissent in \textit{Clinton} and historical ``sums not exceeding'' appropriations to argue that the Act delegates spending discretion rather than authorizing statutory repeal. The port authority will argue under Stevens's majority opinion that: (1) a blanket, across-the-board, 10-day post-enactment cancellation power based on the same facts Congress just evaluated is the \emph{functional equivalent} of a partial repeal violating Article I, \S~7 regardless of statutory labels; (2) unlike \textit{Field v. Clark}, the directive rejects Congress's immediate policy choice rather than executing a post-enactment contingency; and (3) under Kennedy's concurrence and the nondelegation doctrine, a vague ``fiscal discipline'' standard across the entire federal budget abdicates Congress's Article I power of the purse. The outcome is uncertain because the majority conceded (524 U.S. at 446--447) that Congress may appropriate discretionary ``sums not exceeding'' a stated amount, yet a directive that permanently blocks an earmarked item resembles the cancellation \textit{Clinton} struck down; the case may turn on whether a court treats the statute as a delegation of spending discretion or as a partial repeal under another label.

\end{enumerate}
```

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Statute Invalid): Compelled Official Maps Contradicting Recognition.} The President recognizes Country A's sovereignty over a border province that Country B also claims. Congress directs the Secretary of State to publish official State Department maps, and the Department's annual country reports to foreign governments, depicting the province as part of Country B. Like \S~214(d), the statute does not itself recognize anyone, but it commands the Executive to make formal, foreign-facing statements that contradict the President's recognition determination. Under the majority's holding that the exclusive recognition power includes the President's control over official Executive Branch statements about a state's territorial bounds, the mandate is unconstitutional (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/576/1/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Statute Invalid): Ordering the Executive to Receive a Breakaway Government's Envoy.} A province of a recognized state declares independence. The President declines to recognize it. Congress enacts a statute directing the Secretary of State to accredit the breakaway government's representative as an ambassador and to exchange formal diplomatic notes acknowledging the new state. Receiving an ambassador was, for the founding-era jurists the Court relied on, the paradigm act of recognition, and the statute would force the Executive to perform that act against the President's judgment. Because the President's recognition power is ``conclusive and preclusive,'' this is \textit{Youngstown} Category Three in which the President prevails even more clearly than in \textit{Zivotofsky} itself.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Congress Prevails): Defunding an Embassy.} Suppose the President formally recognizes the government of Country X as the sole legitimate sovereign over Disputed Island Y, refusing to recognize a breakaway republic on Island Y. Congress responds with a statute that, among other things, prohibits the expenditure of any federal funds to operate a U.S. Embassy in Country X's capital unless the President withdraws recognition of Country X's claim to Island Y. Even under \textit{Zivotofsky}'s majority opinion, Justice Kennedy explicitly acknowledged that Congress retains its Appropriations Clause power (Art. I, \S~9, cl. 7) and that ``[t]he President, furthermore, could not build an American Embassy abroad without congressional appropriation of the necessary funds.'' Thus, refusing to fund an embassy is squarely within Congress's power of the purse. However, the President's counterargument would invoke the unconstitutional-conditions principle in separation of powers: Congress may not use an explicit condition tied directly to forcing a change in a formal recognition determination to coerce the President into surrendering an exclusive Article II prerogative.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Congress Prevails): Sanctions on a Recognized Government.} Over the President's objection, Congress bars all imports from, and arms exports to, a foreign government that the President recognizes and wishes to court. The statute says nothing about sovereignty or territorial bounds and requires no Executive statement inconsistent with recognition; it regulates foreign commerce, an enumerated Article I power. The majority expressly confined its holding to recognition determinations, rejected the \textit{Curtiss-Wright} ``sole organ'' dicta, and stressed that ``[i]t is not for the President alone to determine the whole content of the Nation's foreign policy.'' The President must therefore enforce the sanctions, even though they cut against his diplomatic strategy.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Uncertain): Customs Labels and Tariffs for an Unrecognized Territory.} Return to the Island Y hypothetical. The same statute also directs U.S. Customs and Border Protection to classify all goods imported from Island Y as ``Products of the Independent Republic of Island Y'' on official customs entry invoices and to apply a 0\% preferential tariff rate rather than Country X's 25\% tariff rate. Under \textit{Zivotofsky}'s majority opinion, the President will argue that compelling Customs officials to print ``Independent Republic of Island Y'' on official government documents forces the Executive to contradict its formal recognition determination in an official communication affecting foreign sovereigns. But Congress (echoing Justice Scalia's dissent and Kennedy's own limits on \textit{Curtiss-Wright}) has a much stronger counterargument here than in \textit{Zivotofsky}: regulating tariffs and country-of-origin rules lies at the core of Congress's express Foreign Commerce Clause and Taxing Clause powers (Art. I, \S~8, cls. 1, 3)---a power reinforced by \textit{Learning Resources, Inc. v. Trump} (2026)---and Congress has historically enacted trade and statutory rules treating unrecognized entities (such as Taiwan under the Taiwan Relations Act) as distinct for commercial purposes without altering formal diplomatic recognition. A court might sever the 0\% tariff rate (valid under Article I) from any mandatory sovereignty label on official diplomatic-facing documents, or uphold Section A entirely as a commercial classification rather than an act of recognition. The outcome turns on how far the President's control over ``official executive statements'' extends into documents that serve Congress's commerce powers---a line the majority did not draw.

\end{enumerate}

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Delegation Upheld): A Transitional Rule for a New Drone Registry.} Congress creates a national registry for commercial drone operators, declares its purpose to establish a comprehensive national system, requires every operator to register before flying, and authorizes the FAA Administrator ``to specify the applicability'' of the requirement to operators who bought drones before the statute's enactment and to prescribe rules for operators unable to comply with the initial deadline. Read as the \textit{Gundy} plurality read SORNA, the clause concerns how and when existing operators come into the system, not whether they come in at all; the comprehensive-system purpose and the reference to operators ``unable to comply'' confine the Administrator to a feasibility judgment. That transitional, small-bore discretion easily supplies an intelligible principle, and Justice Alito's view that such a provision should not be singled out under existing precedent supplies a fifth vote.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Delegation Upheld): A Broad Health Standard for a New Pollutant.} Congress directs an agency to set air-quality standards for a newly identified airborne particle at the level ``requisite to protect the public health,'' after considering published scientific criteria. A regulated manufacturer argues that the phrase gives the agency unlimited discretion over a major industry. Under the intelligible-principle test the plurality applied in \textit{Gundy}, the challenge fails: the Court has repeatedly upheld standards of exactly this breadth, including this very phrase in \textit{Whitman v. American Trucking Assns., Inc.}, 531 U.S. 457 (2001), and the \textit{Gundy} plurality treated such precedents as proof that only a statute supplying no policy or standard at all fails the test. The challenger's real hope lies in persuading a majority to adopt Justice Gorsuch's dissent, which \textit{FCC v. Consumers' Research} declined to do.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Delegation Likely Invalid): Unbounded Discretion Over Whether a Crime Applies.} Congress enacts a statute providing that the Attorney General may determine, in her sole discretion, whether and to what extent a new federal firearms felony applies to any category of persons, and may change those determinations at any time. The text, unlike SORNA's, expressly confers the whether-or-not discretion Gundy attributed to \S~20913(d), and nothing in the statute's purpose or structure narrows it. The \textit{Gundy} plurality conceded that ``[i]f that were so, we would face a nondelegation question,'' and its saving construction would be unavailable because the text forecloses it. The three \textit{Gundy} dissenters would treat the provision as letting the nation's chief prosecutor write his own criminal code, and the power fits none of Justice Gorsuch's three permissible categories. The statute would likely fall.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Delegation Invalid): A Shipment Ban Left to the Secretary's Unguided Choice.} Congress authorizes the Secretary of Agriculture to prohibit the interstate shipment of any agricultural product whenever the Secretary chooses, makes violations a federal crime, and states no policy, finding, or standard to govern when a ban should issue. This is the pattern of \textit{Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan}, 293 U.S. 388 (1935), one of the two statutes the Court has struck down on nondelegation grounds, where Congress articulated no policy or standard whatsoever. Even the \textit{Gundy} plurality, which defended broad delegations, distinguished such statutes; nothing in the text gives the Secretary a principle to follow, so there is no saving construction to adopt. Every member of the \textit{Gundy} Court would likely find a violation of Article~I.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): Criminal Rulemaking for Frontier AI.} Responding to rapid developments in autonomous vehicles and artificial intelligence, Congress enacts the ``National AI Safety Act of 2026.'' Section 4 of the Act empowers the Secretary of Commerce ``to promulgate such rules, licensing conditions, and criminal prohibitions (punishable by up to five years' imprisonment) governing the training and deployment of frontier artificial intelligence models as the Secretary determines are necessary and appropriate to protect the public interest, promote American innovation, and safeguard national security.'' The statute contains no further definition of ``frontier'' models, no threshold metrics, and no list of prohibited practices. Relying on Section 4, the Secretary issues a rule making it a federal felony to release any open-weight AI model trained using more than $10^{25}$ floating-point operations without a federal license. A software developer indicted under the rule challenges Section 4 under the nondelegation doctrine. \textbf{Challenger:} Unlike SORNA's transitional ``as soon as feasible'' mandate in \textit{Gundy} or the bounded beneficiary and service criteria in \textit{FCC v. Consumers' Research}, Section 4 confers economy-wide criminal lawmaking power with zero determinate boundaries. \textbf{Government:} The government will invoke the Court's unbroken post-1935 practice of upholding ``public interest'' standards (\textit{NBC v. United States}; \textit{Yakus}) and argue that ``frontier'' AI and ``national security'' supply contextual boundaries---especially because, as Justice Kavanaugh emphasized in \textit{Consumers' Research} (and Justice Gorsuch acknowledged in his third \textit{Gundy} category), nondelegation and the major questions doctrine play a far more limited role when Congress delegates to the President or an accountable Cabinet Secretary in the \textit{national security} realm. The outcome is uncertain because the case presses the question \textit{Gundy} left open: whether a delegation defining a serious crime demands more specificity than the ordinary intelligible-principle test requires.

\end{enumerate}

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Heightened Scrutiny Limits the Subpoena): The President's Family Resort as a ``Case Study.''} Suppose the House Committee on Ways and Means is considering a bill to cap foreign-government hotel and resort payments to any federal official, including the President. The Committee issues a subpoena to a private hospitality management firm for all booking ledgers, guest lists, and pricing contracts from 2018--2026 for a resort owned by the sitting President's family trust, asserting that ``examining the President's resort operations will illuminate industry loopholes.'' Under Factor 2, an eight-year demand for all guest lists and contracts is likely overbroad relative to foreign-government payments. Under Factor 3, because regulating the President raises sensitive constitutional questions (e.g., Emoluments Clause enforcement), the Committee must offer detailed, substantial evidence tying these specific records to a valid legislative aim rather than disguised law enforcement. Under Factor 4, the court must weigh the institutional burden and partisan-leverage risk.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Heightened Scrutiny Limits the Subpoena): A Decade of Presidential Medical Records.} A Senate committee drafting legislation on procedures for determining presidential disability subpoenas a hospital for ten years of the sitting President's complete medical records. Medical records are exactly the kind of personal papers the Court warned could otherwise be swept in, so the court should refuse to enforce the subpoena as written.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (\textit{Mazars} Inapplicable): A State Grand Jury Subpoena.} A state prosecutor investigating possible business-records fraud by a hotel company has a grand jury subpoena the company's outside accountants for financial records relating to the sitting President's businesses. Vance}, 591 U.S. 786 (2020)}, decided the same day as \textit{Mazars}, the President has no absolute immunity from a state criminal subpoena and the prosecutor need not show heightened need; the President may raise only the defenses available to any citizen, plus arguments that the subpoena would impede his Article II functions.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Ordinary Legislative-Purpose Review): A Subpoena for a Private Executive's Records.} A House committee drafting bank-capital legislation subpoenas a large bank for the personal account records of its chief executive, who has no government position. The executive objects that the committee is really trying to embarrass him. Because the subpoena does not seek a President's information, \textit{Mazars}'s special separation-of-powers balancing is not triggered. The court asks only whether the subpoena serves a valid legislative purpose on a subject on which legislation could be had, is not aimed at law enforcement or exposure for its own sake, and respects the witness's constitutional rights---the traditional framework of \textit{McGrain v.\ Daugherty}, 273 U.S.\ 135 (1927), and \textit{Watkins v.\ United States}, 354 U.S.\ 178 (1957). That deferential test is likely satisfied.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unresolved): A Narrow Subpoena Aimed at a Former President.} After a President leaves office, a House committee drafting amendments to presidential financial-disclosure laws subpoenas his former accounting firm for three years of financial statements, citing specific discrepancies between those statements and his public disclosure forms. The subpoena is narrow and tied to concrete evidence of a legislative need, satisfying several \textit{Mazars} factors. But it is unclear whether the factors still apply with full force once the President is a private citizen, and whether a committee may still not treat an individual President as a case study. Any challenge must therefore shift from statutory major-questions review to a constitutional \textit{nondelegation} claim---which, after \textit{FCC v. Consumers' Research}, 606 U.S. 656 (2025), will fail so long as Congress supplied an ``intelligible principle'' setting the general policy and boundaries of delegated authority.

That is the inside-the-fenceline, technology-based approach EPA had always used under \S~111(d), so the rule claims no ``unheralded'' power and the major questions doctrine likely does not apply. \item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): Mandatory Climate-Risk Disclosure by Public Companies.} Invoking its general authority to require disclosures ``necessary or appropriate in the public interest or for the protection of investors,'' the Securities and Exchange Commission requires public companies to report their greenhouse-gas emissions and climate-related financial risks. ## 178_cfpb_v_community_financial_services_assn

### Memory Jogger

```latex

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Valid Appropriation): A Fee-Funded Agency.} Congress creates a federal trademark registry and provides by permanent statute that the registry may spend, without annual appropriations, the filing fees it collects, up to a fixed amount each year, to examine and register marks. Firms denied registration argue that the agency escapes the annual control of the purse. Under \textit{CFPB v.\ CFSA}, the statute is a law that identifies a source of public funds (the fees) and designates the purposes for which they may be spent; the Clause demands nothing more. The arrangement closely tracks the fee-funded Customs Service and Post Office that the First and Second Congresses established and that the majority relied on, and the cap leaves the agency discretion only to spend less (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/601/22-448/}{Justia}).

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Valid Appropriation): A Permanent ``Such Sums'' Benefits Program.} A permanent statute provides that the Secretary of Veterans Affairs shall pay, from the general Treasury, such sums as are necessary to provide a specified monthly education stipend to every eligible veteran, with no expiration date and no annual vote. A taxpayer group argues that a perpetual, open-ended draw on the Treasury is not a true appropriation. The statute names a source (the general Treasury) and a designated purpose (the stipend), so it satisfies the \textit{CFPB v.\ CFSA} test; nothing in the Clause requires annual renewal or a fixed sum. Justice Kagan's concurrence emphasized that standing appropriations of this kind fund a large share of the modern federal budget, which is why a contrary rule would have unsettled programs such as Social Security and Medicare.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Violates the Constitution): A Standing Draw for the Army.} Congress gives a new Army Modernization Office permanent authority to draw from the Treasury, each year and without further legislation, whatever the Office's Director deems necessary to equip soldiers, subject to an inflation-adjusted cap. Unlike the CFPB's mechanism, this one runs into an express constitutional limit: Article~I, \S~8, cl.~12 provides that no appropriation of money to raise and support armies shall be for a longer term than two years. The majority in \textit{CFPB v.\ CFSA} relied on that Army Clause to show by negative implication that civilian appropriations need not be time-limited; by the same reasoning, a perpetual army appropriation is invalid.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Appropriation at All): Spending Without a Statute.} After Congress declines to fund a proposed border-technology program, the Secretary of Homeland Security directs the Treasury to pay \$200 million to contractors to build it, citing only the President's general Article~II responsibility to protect the Nation. \textit{CFPB v.\ CFSA} keeps the Clause's core requirement intact: money may leave the Treasury only under a law that identifies a source and authorizes the expenditure for designated purposes. Here no statute authorizes the payment at all, so it violates the Appropriations Clause regardless of the program's merits. \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/496/414/}{\textit{Office of Personnel Management v.\ Richmond}, 496 U.S.\ 414 (1990)}, likewise held that payments of money from the Treasury must be authorized by statute.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Open Question): An Uncapped Perpetual Draw for an AI Safety Agency.} Suppose Congress enacts the ``National AI Safety Authority Act,'' authorizing a single-director agency to draw ``whatever amounts the Director deems necessary, without any dollar or percentage cap, from the general Treasury of the United States in perpetuity, to promote safe artificial intelligence.'' Regulated AI firms challenge the funding mechanism under the Appropriations Clause. \textbf{Government's Argument (Under \textit{CFPB v. CFSA}):} The statute satisfies Justice Thomas's two-part test: it identifies a source (the general Treasury) and a designated purpose (promoting safe AI). Neither durational limits nor numerical caps are textually required outside the Army Clause, and Congress has long enacted standing ``sums necessary'' mandatory appropriations (as Justice Kagan highlighted). \textbf{Opposite Side Contrast (Distinguishing \textit{CFPB v. CFSA}):} Challengers can exploit the majority's repeated emphasis on the CFPB's \textit{statutory cap} (``Congress determined the amount of the Bureau's annual funding by imposing a statutory cap,'' 601~U.S.~at~438) and its analogy to early ``sums not exceeding'' grants. Unlike the CFPB or the fee-funded Post Office/Customs Service, an \textit{uncapped} draw from the \textit{general Treasury} paired with an open-ended statutory mandate (``promote safe AI'') combines an Appropriations Clause defect with a severe Article~I nondelegation problem---a separate constitutional constraint that Justice Thomas explicitly left open (601~U.S.~at~440--441). Because the majority relied on the cap without holding that a cap is required, the outcome is genuinely uncertain.

\end{enumerate}
```

## 179_sec_v_jarkesy

### Memory Jogger

```latex
When the SEC seeks civil penalties for securities fraud, the defendant is entitled to a jury trial in an Article~III court: such penalties are a legal remedy for a claim rooted in common-law fraud, and the public rights exception does not let Congress route them to in-house agency adjudication (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/603/22-859/}{Justia}).
```

### Judicial Votes

```latex
The Supreme Court \textbf{affirmed and remanded} by a \textbf{6--3} vote. The case was argued on November~29, 2023, and decided on June~27, 2024 (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/603/22-859/}{Justia}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Majority Opinion:} Chief Justice Roberts delivered the opinion of the Court, joined by Justices Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett.
    \item \textbf{Concurring Opinion:} Justice Gorsuch filed a concurring opinion, joined by Justice Thomas.
    \item \textbf{Dissenting Opinion:} Justice Sotomayor filed a dissenting opinion, joined by Justices Kagan and Jackson.
\end{itemize}
```

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Jury Required): In-House Penalties for Commodities Fraud.} A federal commodities regulator brings an administrative proceeding before its own ALJ against a trader accused of lying to customers about the risks of a futures fund, and seeks a large civil penalty calibrated to the trader's culpability and the need for deterrence, none of which goes to the defrauded customers. As in \textit{Jarkesy}, the remedy is punitive rather than compensatory, which is all but dispositive, and the antifraud claim closely resembles common-law fraud. Regulating commodity markets is not one of the historic public-rights categories, so the trader is entitled to have the penalty claim tried to a jury in an Article~III court.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Jury Required): Relabeling a Fraud Penalty as a Licensing Matter.} Responding to \textit{Jarkesy}, Congress creates a new Investor Protection Board, calls its penalty proceedings administrative licensing reviews, and authorizes it to impose fines on advisers who make material misrepresentations to clients. The government argues that because a federal agency brings the claim under a new statute, it involves public rights. That argument fails: the majority held that what matters is the substance of the suit, not where it is brought, who brings it, or how it is labeled, and \textit{Granfinanciera} forbids Congress from conjuring away the Seventh Amendment by sending traditional legal claims to an administrative tribunal. A punitive fine for misrepresentation remains a common-law-like claim requiring a jury.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Jury Required): Fines on Carriers Under the Immigration Laws.} A federal immigration statute authorizes an executive official, after an administrative hearing, to fine a shipping line for each noncitizen passenger it brings to the United States with a contagious disease that a medical examination at the port of departure would have detected, and bars the vessel's clearance until the fine is paid. The carrier demands a jury. Immigration and control over entry to the country are among the historic sovereign prerogatives that \textit{Jarkesy} recognized as public rights, and the Court long ago upheld administratively imposed fines of this kind in \textit{Oceanic Steam Navigation Co.\ v.\ Stranahan}, 214 U.S.\ 320 (1909). Because the public rights exception applies, Congress may assign the matter to the agency without a jury.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Jury Required): Recovering Overpaid Federal Benefits.} A federal agency administratively determines that a retiree received benefit overpayments because of an error in calculating her eligibility, and orders repayment of the excess amount through reductions in future benefit checks, with no penalty added. The retiree argues that a demand for money from the government entitles her to a jury. Under \textit{Jarkesy}, two features defeat the claim. The granting of public benefits is one of the historic public-rights categories, and the relief merely restores the status quo by recovering what was mistakenly paid rather than punishing or deterring wrongdoing. Neither step of the \textit{Jarkesy} analysis points toward an Article~III jury.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): Immediately Enforceable EPA Emission Penalties.} Congress amends the Clean Air Act to empower the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to impose up to \$500,000 per violation in immediately enforceable civil penalties---collectible via Treasury offset without a federal-court collection suit---against factories that emit pollutants exceeding complex numerical parts-per-million thresholds. ### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Channel-Closing Upheld): Banning Interstate Shipment of a Medication Lawful in Both States.} Congress enacts the ``Interstate Reproductive Information and Materials Act,'' making it a federal felony to ship any over-the-counter abortifacient medication or printed instructional brochure across state lines via private courier. A distributor in State~A (where the medication is legal) ships a package via FedEx to a clinic in State~B (where it is also legal) and challenges the indictment under the Commerce Clause and Tenth Amendment. As to the medication itself: Under \textit{Champion} and \textit{Darby}, Congress's authority over the channels of interstate commerce is plenary and includes the power to prohibit interstate shipment of any article, regardless of whether the underlying motive is moral or regulatory, and even if both the origin and destination states permit the article locally.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Channel-Closing Upheld): Unserialized Firearm Kits.} Congress makes it a federal crime to ship unserialized, build-it-yourself firearm kits across state lines. A seller in State~A mails a kit to a buyer in State~B and challenges the indictment solely on Commerce Clause and Tenth Amendment grounds, arguing that the kits are lawful where sold and that Congress is really regulating local gun ownership. Under \textit{Champion}, the kits are articles of traffic, and the power to regulate their interstate carriage includes the power to forbid it. Under \textit{Darby}, Congress's reason for closing the channel---crime control rather than commercial policy---is irrelevant, and there is no Tenth Amendment problem because the regulation rests on a delegated power. The indictment stands.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (External Limits): The Brochures.} Same statute, but the distributor challenges the ban on shipping the printed instructional brochures. First, distinguish \textit{Champion} on external constitutional limits: Harlan expressly acknowledged that the commerce power ``may not be exercised so as to infringe rights secured or protected by that instrument'' (188 U.S.\ at 362--363); banning the interstate shipment of \textit{printed brochures} directly implicates the First Amendment (an issue Fuller flagged as absent in \textit{Champion}). Second, unlike \textit{Champion}, where Harlan stressed that Congress was ``supplement[ing] the action of those states---perhaps all of them'' that banned lotteries, here Congress is overriding the permissive policies of both State~A and State~B, reviving Fuller's structural objection that Congress is using channel-closing as a pretextual national police power. (Under current doctrine, the First Amendment challenge to the brochures is far stronger than the Tenth Amendment/Commerce Clause challenge to the physical pills.)

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Channel at All): A Federal Ban on Public Intoxication.} Citing the moral concerns that sustained \textit{Champion}, Congress makes it a federal crime for any adult to be publicly intoxicated anywhere in the United States. The statute has no jurisdictional element tying the offense to interstate travel or shipment. \textit{Champion} does not help the government: it upheld a regulation of the interstate \emph{movement} of articles, not a general federal power to punish local immorality. Because no channel of interstate commerce is being used, the statute must instead be justified as regulating activity that substantially affects interstate commerce, and under \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/514/549/}{\textit{United States v.\ Lopez}}, 514 U.S. 549 (1995), and \textit{United States v.\ Morrison}, 529 U.S. 598 (2000), noneconomic local conduct of this kind cannot be aggregated to meet that test. The statute is invalid.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Has the Article Left the Channel?): Possession of Goods That Once Crossed State Lines.} Congress makes it a crime to possess any vaping device that has at any time traveled in interstate commerce. A buyer is prosecuted for possessing a device he purchased at a local shop three years after it was shipped into his State. The government argues that this is \textit{Champion}'s channels power plus a jurisdictional element; the Court read a similar felon-in-possession statute to require only such a minimal nexus in \textit{Scarborough v.\ United States}, 431 U.S. 563 (1977). The defendant answers that \textit{Champion} rested on stopping articles \emph{in transit}, as Harlan emphasized by saying Congress left purely internal lottery sales to the States, and that a once-in-commerce hook lets Congress reach any local possession, the general police power Fuller feared. The Court has not squarely resolved how far this kind of hook can reach, so the outcome is uncertain.

\end{enumerate}

Upholding the Migratory Bird Treaty Act 7--2, Justice Holmes held that Congress may enact legislation necessary and proper to execute a valid treaty even on a subject an unaided Act of Congress could not reach, and that the Tenth Amendment does not bar it (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/252/416/}{Justia}).

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Statute Valid): Implementing a Treaty to Protect a Migrating Butterfly.} The United States, Canada, and Mexico conclude a treaty obligating each nation to protect the breeding habitat of a butterfly species that migrates every year across all three countries and is in danger of extinction. Congress implements the treaty with a statute forbidding destruction of designated breeding habitat, including on private land within the States. A landowner argues that habitat regulation is reserved to the States. Under \textit{Holland}, if the treaty is valid, the statute is valid as a necessary and proper means of executing it, whatever Congress could do on its own. The facts mirror the migratory birds themselves: creatures in international transit, a resource no single State can protect, and a problem that requires national action in concert with other sovereigns.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Statute Applies): A Nerve-Agent Attack on a Subway.} A defendant releases a homemade nerve agent in a crowded city subway station, and federal prosecutors charge him under the statute implementing the Chemical Weapons Convention. He argues that, as in \textit{Bond II}, the statute must be read narrowly to avoid federalizing local crime. The argument fails. Releasing a toxic chemical to cause mass casualties is the core conduct the Convention addresses, so no clear statement is needed to bring it within the statute, and applying the law raises none of the federalism concerns that led the Court to read \S~229 narrowly in \textit{Bond II}. Because the statute reaches him, \textit{Holland} supplies the constitutional authority: Congress may execute a valid treaty through necessary and proper legislation.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Statute Invalid): A Treaty Requiring Criminal Bans on Protected Speech.} The United States ratifies a treaty obligating member nations to criminalize speech that insults any religion. Congress enacts implementing legislation making such speech a federal crime, and a newspaper columnist is prosecuted for an essay mocking religious belief. \textit{Holland} does not help the government. Holmes rejected a Tenth Amendment objection because the treaty contravened no ``prohibitory words'' in the Constitution; the First Amendment is exactly such a prohibition. Under \textit{Reid v.\ Covert}, no agreement with a foreign nation can free Congress from the restraints of the Constitution, so the statute is unconstitutional as applied to protected speech even though it executes a valid treaty obligation.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Statute Read Narrowly): Poisoning a Neighbor's Fish Pond.} After a dispute over a fence, a homeowner pours a household pesticide into his neighbor's backyard pond, killing the fish. Federal prosecutors charge him under the Chemical Weapons Convention implementing statute, whose broad definition of chemical weapons literally covers the pesticide. As in \textit{Bond II}, the conduct is a purely local offense of the kind traditionally left to state law, and nothing in the statute clearly shows that Congress meant to reach it. The court would construe the statute not to cover the conduct, and so would never reach the constitutional question \textit{Holland} answered. The prosecution fails on statutory grounds, and the State remains free to prosecute under its own law.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Unresolved): A Treaty-Based Ban on School Corporal Punishment.} Congress previously enacted a federal statute banning corporal punishment in all public and private K--12 schools, which a federal court invalidated under \textit{United States v.\ Lopez} as regulating noneconomic local conduct. The President subsequently negotiates, and two-thirds of the Senate ratifies, the ``International Convention on the Rights of the Child,'' which obligates signatory nations to enact domestic laws banning corporal punishment in all schools. Congress then reenacts the identical school-discipline ban, expressly stating that it applies to every local public and private school nationwide and citing the treaty as its constitutional authority. A private school in Texas challenges the statute. \textit{For the government:} Under \textit{Holland}, Congress's power to enact legislation necessary and proper to execute a valid treaty is not limited by what an unaided Act of Congress could accomplish under the Commerce Clause. Because Congress included an unmistakable clear statement reaching local schools, \textit{Bond II}'s statutory-avoidance canon cannot defeat the statute. Furthermore, the statute violates no affirmative Bill of Rights prohibition (\textit{Reid v.\ Covert}), and international human-rights norms protecting children are a well-established subject of multilateral diplomacy. \textit{For the challenger:} Because Congress's explicit text forecloses \textit{Bond II}'s avoidance path, the Court must confront the constitutional questions left open in \textit{Bond II}. First, under Justice Thomas's and Justice Alito's \textit{Bond II} framework, the Treaty Power is originally limited to ``matters of international intercourse'' or ``legitimate international concern.'' Unlike migratory birds physically crossing international borders in \textit{Holland}, intrastate school discipline involves no cross-border transit or genuine international collective-action failure; upholding it would allow the President and Senate to overturn \textit{Lopez} at will. Second, under Justice Scalia's textualist reading of the Necessary and Proper Clause, Congress may assist the \textit{making} of treaties, not bootstrap a non-self-executing treaty into a freestanding domestic police power beyond Article~I, \S~8. The outcome is uncertain because \textit{Holland} remains good law on its face, while the Court has never decided whether the \textit{Bond II} concurrences' limits on the treaty power are correct.

\end{enumerate}
```

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Preempted): A Broader, Unwaivable State Contracting Ban.} Congress authorizes the President to ban new U.S.\ energy investment in Freedonia, subject to a national-security waiver. New York bars state agencies from contracting with \textit{any} domestic or foreign firm doing any business in Freedonia, without a waiver, sparking EU protests. Preempted under \textit{Crosby}---the state spending defense fails, and the law blunts presidential waiver leverage, overrides Congress's calibrated scope, and impedes multilateral diplomacy.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (Preempted): A State Law Matching Federal Scope but Lacking the Waiver.} Congress bars U.S.\ persons from making new investments in a foreign country's mining sector and authorizes the President to waive the ban whenever he certifies that doing so serves national security. A State enacts a law imposing the same ban on the same class of persons and the same conduct, but with no waiver and no termination clause. The President later waives the federal ban as part of a negotiated agreement. Even though the state law tracks Congress's calibrated scope, it still conflicts with the first pillar of \textit{Crosby}: because it operates with no mechanism to yield to presidential waivers, it strips the President of control over the economic pressure Congress placed in his hands and reduces the value of his bargaining chips. The state law is preempted to the extent it outlasts the waiver.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Survives): Congress Expressly Authorizes Matching State Divestment.} Congress enacts a Freedonia Divestment Act expressly authorizing states to divest public pensions from firms on a Treasury defense list, provided state measures automatically sunset upon federal delisting or presidential waiver. Texas enacts a matching pension-divestment statute. Upheld---express congressional authorization overcomes \textit{Crosby}, \textit{Zschernig}, and the dormant Foreign Commerce Clause, while automatic sunsetting and federal-list tracking preserve both presidential ``bargaining chips'' and congressional calibration.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (Crosby Inapplicable): A State Boycott With No Federal Statute in the Field.} A State bars its agencies from buying goods from companies that do business with a foreign country accused of human-rights abuses. Congress has enacted no sanctions statute concerning that country, and the President has issued no executive order or agreement addressing it. A trade association sues, relying on \textit{Crosby}. Obstacle preemption under \textit{Crosby} requires a federal statute whose purposes and objectives the state law frustrates; here there is no federal statute to obstruct, so \textit{Crosby}'s holding does not invalidate the law. The challengers must instead press the theories \textit{Crosby} expressly reserved, the dormant foreign affairs power under \textit{Zschernig v.\ Miller} and the dormant Foreign Commerce Clause, each of which raises open questions the Court did not decide.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Close Call): A State Pension Divestment Law That Tracks the Federal List Without Congressional Authorization.} Congress imposes sanctions on a foreign country's defense firms and lets the President add or remove firms from a Treasury list, but says nothing about state measures. A State requires its pension funds to divest from firms on the Treasury list and to reinvest automatically when a firm is delisted. For the State: unlike Massachusetts's law, its statute adds no new targets, follows every federal designation, and expires when federal sanctions do, preserving the President's bargaining chips and Congress's calibration. For the challengers: the State still adds economic pressure Congress did not impose, foreign governments may protest, and \textit{Crosby} held that congressional silence is ambiguous rather than permission. Whether a court treats close federal tracking as eliminating any obstacle is genuinely uncertain.

\end{enumerate}

### Examples: Future Applications (complete section as it now reads)

```latex
\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Same-Side (State Action Found): The Corporate ``Tech Town.''} Apex Robotics constructs ``Apexville,'' a 4,000-acre unincorporated settlement in rural Texas housing 12,000 employees and their families. Apex owns all land, streets, sidewalks, utilities, parks, a central retail plaza containing a U.S. Post Office branch, and a private security force cross-deputized by the county sheriff. Roads into Apexville are ungated and open to highway travelers. When a labor organizer stands on the plaza sidewalk outside the post office distributing leaflets criticizing Apex's workplace safety record, Apex's security orders her off the property and has her arrested under the Texas criminal trespass statute. This is the closest modern analogue to \textit{Marsh}. Unlike a standalone shopping mall (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/424/507/}{\textit{Hudgens}}) or cable channel (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/587/17-1702/}{\textit{Halleck}}), Apexville possesses ``all the attributes of a town''---residential housing, utilities, open thoroughfares, a business block, a post office, and cross-deputized policing. Under \textit{Marsh}, Apex performs a traditional, exclusive municipal function, and enforcing state criminal trespass law to suppress peaceful leafletting violates the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

\item \textbf{Same-Side (State Action Found): A Private Company That Runs the County's Elections.} A county contracts with a private corporation to conduct its municipal elections from start to finish: the company qualifies candidates for the ballot, staffs the polls, counts the votes, and certifies the winners. The company refuses to place on the ballot any candidate who has criticized its parent firm. A disqualified candidate sues under the First and Fourteenth Amendments. Running public elections is one of the very few functions the post-\textit{Marsh} cases treat as traditionally and exclusively reserved to the State, as the white-primary decision in \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/345/461/}{\textit{Terry v.\ Adams}, 345 U.S.\ 461 (1953)}, illustrates. As in \textit{Marsh}, a State cannot hand a governmental function to a private entity and thereby free it from constitutional limits, so the company is a state actor.

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No State Action): The Dominant Social-Media Platform.} OmniStream, a privately owned social-media network used by 80\% of a city's residents to follow local news and debate municipal elections, suspends a local activist's account for violating its content-moderation policies. The activist sues under the First Amendment, invoking \textit{Marsh} and arguing that OmniStream is the ``modern company town.'' \textbf{No state action.} Under \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/587/17-1702/}{\textit{Halleck}} (587 U.S. 802), hosting a forum for third-party speech is neither a traditional nor an exclusive prerogative of the State. Moreover, under \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/603/22-277/}{\textit{Moody v. NetChoice}} (603 U.S. 707), OmniStream's curation of its feed is itself protected private expressive activity. (Only if a \textit{government official} personally blocked the activist on an official-authority account would state action arise under \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/601/22-611/}{\textit{Lindke v. Freed}}, 601 U.S. 187.)

\item \textbf{Opposite-Side (No Federal State Action): The Open-Air Lifestyle Shopping Center.} Galleria Commons is a massive, privately owned outdoor shopping and dining complex with faux ``Main Street'' sidewalks, benches, and a private fountain plaza open to the general public, but containing no residences, municipal utilities, or governmental services. Management ejects political petitioners under state trespass law. \textbf{No federal state action.} Under \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/407/551/}{\textit{Lloyd Corp. v. Tanner}} (407 U.S. 551) and \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/424/507/}{\textit{Hudgens v. NLRB}} (424 U.S. 507), inviting the public onto commercial property to shop does not convert a retail center into a municipality. Unless the applicable \textit{state constitution} grants an affirmative right of access on private mall property (per \href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/447/74/}{\textit{PruneYard}}, 447 U.S. 74), the petitioners lose on federal constitutional grounds.

\item \textbf{Fence-Sitter (Uncertain): The Developer-Owned New Town.} A developer builds and continues to own an unincorporated, ungated community of 20,000 residents in which it holds title to every street, sidewalk, park, and the central shopping district, and it employs private security officers who enforce its rules; but the county supplies police, fire protection, water, and public schools. The developer bars petition circulators from its town-center sidewalks. The streets are open to the public and the town looks like the Chickasaw business block, which favors applying \textit{Marsh}. Yet \textit{Hudgens} confined \textit{Marsh} to private property with all the attributes of a town, and here the core municipal services remain public. Because the Court has never said how many municipal attributes are enough, a court could reasonably go either way.

\end{enumerate}
```
