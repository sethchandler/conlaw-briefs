\section{Facts and Procedural History}

Under California's Agricultural Labor Relations Act of 1975, the Agricultural Labor Relations Board (ALRB) promulgated a regulation granting labor organizations a ``right to take access'' onto an agricultural employer's private premises to meet with employees and solicit union support (Cal.\ Code Regs., tit.~8, \S~20900(e)) (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII}). Upon filing written notice with the Board and serving the employer, union organizers could enter the employer's property for up to three hours per day---one hour before work, one hour during lunch, and one hour after work---during up to four 30-day periods (120 days) per calendar year. Interfering with access constituted an unfair labor practice carrying state sanctions.

Two California agricultural employers whose workers lived entirely off-premises challenged the regulation (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cedar Point Nursery:} A northern California strawberry grower employing over 400 seasonal and roughly 100 full-time workers. In October 2015, United Farm Workers (UFW) organizers entered Cedar Point's trim shed at 5:00 a.m.\ without prior notice, using bullhorns and disrupting operations; both sides filed unfair labor practice charges.
    \item \textbf{Fowler Packing Company:} A Fresno-based table-grape and citrus grower and shipper with 1,800--2,500 field workers and roughly 500 packing-facility employees. In July 2015, Fowler blocked UFW organizers from entering its property, prompting an unfair labor practice charge that the union later withdrew.
\end{itemize}

Cedar Point and Fowler sued ALRB officials in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of California, seeking declaratory and injunctive relief on the ground that the access regulation appropriated an uncompensated easement, effecting a \emph{per se} physical taking under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments. The District Court denied a preliminary injunction and dismissed the complaint, holding that because access was neither permanent nor continuous, the claim had to be evaluated under the multifactor balancing test of \textit{Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City}, 438 U.S. 104 (1978), which the growers did not plead. A divided Ninth Circuit panel affirmed (923 F.3d 524 (2019)), reasoning that \emph{per se} treatment applies only to permanent physical occupations allowing access ``24 hours a day, 365 days a year.'' Rehearing en banc was denied over a dissent by Judge Ikuta joined by seven judges (956 F.3d 1162 (2020)), and the Supreme Court granted certiorari (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/594/20-107/}{Justia}).

\section{Issue(s) Presented}

Does a state regulation granting union organizers a right to physically enter an agricultural employer's private property for up to three hours per day, 120 days per year, constitute a \emph{per se} physical taking under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments, or is it a use restriction evaluated under \textit{Penn Central} balancing?

\section{Holding}

\textbf{Per Se Physical Taking:} California's access regulation appropriates a right to physically invade the growers' property---transferring to third parties the owners' fundamental right to exclude---and therefore constitutes a \emph{per se} physical taking requiring just compensation under the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments, regardless of whether the authorized access is intermittent or temporary (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII}).

\section{Reasoning}

\subsection{Physical Appropriations vs.\ Use Restrictions}
Writing for a 6--3 majority, Chief Justice Roberts drew a sharp structural divide in Takings Clause doctrine. When government restricts how an owner may use her own property (e.g., zoning or commercial limits), courts apply the flexible balancing test of \textit{Penn Central} to determine whether the regulation goes ``too far'' (\textit{Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon}, 260 U.S. 393, 415 (1922)). By contrast, when government physically appropriates property for itself or a third party---whether by eminent domain, physical seizure (\textit{United States v. Pewee Coal Co.}, 341 U.S. 114 (1951)), or administrative regulation (\textit{Horne v. Department of Agriculture}, 576 U.S. 351 (2015))---a categorical \emph{per se} rule applies: the government must pay for what it takes, and \textit{Penn Central} has no place (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII}).

\subsection{Appropriating the Right to Exclude}
Because the right to exclude is ``one of the most treasured'' and ``essential sticks in the bundle of rights'' (\textit{Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp.}, 458 U.S. 419, 435 (1982); \textit{Kaiser Aetna v. United States}, 444 U.S. 164, 176, 179--80 (1979)), government-authorized physical invasions---``whether by plane, boat, cable, or beachcomber''---are physical takings (\textit{United States v. Causby}, 328 U.S. 256 (1946); \textit{Nollan v. California Coastal Comm'n}, 483 U.S. 825 (1987)). Here, California's regulation did not restrict how growers farmed; it appropriated for union organizers a formal right ``to literally `take access'\,'' onto private land.

Rejecting the Ninth Circuit's ``24 hours a day, 365 days a year'' test as insupportable, the Court held that duration and intermittency bear only on the \emph{amount} of just compensation, not whether a taking occurred (\textit{United States v. Dow}, 357 U.S. 17, 26 (1958); \textit{Causby}, 328 U.S. at 259). Nor could California evade takings liability because the access right was a ``slight mismatch'' from a formal easement in gross under state law. Finally, the Court distinguished \textit{PruneYard Shopping Center v. Robins}, 447 U.S. 74 (1980), because the shopping center there was generally open to 25,000 public patrons daily, whereas Cedar Point and Fowler's agricultural premises were closed to the public, and distinguished \textit{NLRB v. Babcock \& Wilcox Co.}, 351 U.S. 105 (1956), as a statutory NLRA decision rather than a Takings Clause holding (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII}).

\subsection{Three Limiting Boundaries Preserving Routine Governance}
To dispel fears that its rule would imperil routine government entry onto private land, the Court articulated three critical exceptions (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII}):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Isolated Trespasses vs.\ Takings:} Isolated physical invasions not undertaken pursuant to a granted right of access remain ordinary torts (or subject to causation factors like those in \textit{Arkansas Game and Fish Comm'n v. United States}, 568 U.S. 23 (2012)), not \emph{per se} takings.
    \item \textbf{Background Restrictions of Property Law:} Entries consistent with preexisting common-law privileges---such as nuisance abatement (\textit{Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council}, 505 U.S. 1003, 1028--29 (1992)), public or private necessity, and reasonable Fourth Amendment law-enforcement searches---do not take a right the owner ever possessed.
    \item \textbf{Health and Safety Inspection Conditions:} Under the unconstitutional-conditions framework of \textit{Nollan} and \textit{Dolan v. City of Tigard}, 512 U.S. 374 (1994), government may condition permits, licenses, or registrations on reasonable health and safety inspections (e.g., food, pesticide, pharmaceutical, or nuclear safety) bearing an essential nexus and rough proportionality to public risks. Because California's union-access rule fit none of these three categories, it was a \emph{per se} physical taking.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Concurring and Dissenting Opinions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Justice Kavanaugh, Concurring:} Joined the majority in full, adding that \textit{NLRB v. Babcock \& Wilcox Co.}, 351 U.S. 105 (1956), independently supported the growers because it recognized employers' Fifth Amendment right to exclude union organizers absent a ``necessity'' showing (such as workers living on company property beyond reasonable union communication) (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII}).
    \item \textbf{Justice Breyer, Dissenting (joined by Justices Sotomayor and Kagan):} Argued that California's rule merely \emph{regulated} rather than \emph{appropriated} the right to exclude, creating no traditional estate or easement. Under \textit{Loretto}, \textit{PruneYard}, and \textit{Arkansas Game and Fish}, only \emph{permanent} physical occupations trigger \emph{per se} liability, whereas temporary access (here, roughly 4\% of the year) belongs under \textit{Penn Central} balancing. Warning that the majority's new rule threatens myriad workplace, food-safety, environmental, and childcare inspections and replaces a workable framework with indeterminate exceptions, Justice Breyer also noted on remedies that under \textit{Knick v. Township of Scott}, 588 U.S. 180 (2019), California should be able to foreclose injunctive relief by paying compensation (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Key Quotations}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Substance Over Regulatory Label (Roberts, C.J.):}
    \begin{quote}
        ``The essential question is not, as the Ninth Circuit seemed to think, whether the government action at issue comes garbed as a regulation (or statute, or ordinance, or miscellaneous decree). It is whether the government has physically taken property for itself or someone else---by whatever means---or has instead restricted a property owner's ability to use his own property.'' (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII})
    \end{quote}
    \item \textbf{No 365-Day Threshold for Physical Takings (Roberts, C.J.):}
    \begin{quote}
        ``The access regulation appropriates a right to invade the growers' property and therefore constitutes a per se physical taking. \ldots{} There is no reason the law should analyze an abrogation of the right to exclude in one manner if it extends for 365 days, but in an entirely different manner if it lasts for 364.'' (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII})
    \end{quote}
    \item \textbf{Right to Exclude Cannot Be Balanced Away (Roberts, C.J.):}
    \begin{quote}
        ``We cannot agree that the right to exclude is an empty formality, subject to modification at the government's pleasure. On the contrary, it is a `fundamental element of the property right,' \textit{Kaiser Aetna}, 444 U.S., at 179--180, that cannot be balanced away.'' (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII})
    \end{quote}
    \item \textbf{Regulation vs.\ Appropriation (Breyer, J., dissenting):}
    \begin{quote}
        ``The regulation does not appropriate anything. \ldots{} It gives union organizers the right temporarily to invade a portion of the property owners' land. It thereby limits the landowners' right to exclude certain others. The regulation regulates (but does not appropriate) the owners' right to exclude.'' (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/20-107}{Cornell LII})
    \end{quote}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Doctrinal Significance and Modern Legacy (Through 2026)}

\textit{Cedar Point Nursery} ushered in a sustained revival of formalist property-rights protection across the Roberts Court's takings docket through 2026:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reinforcing Anti-Manipulation and Per Se Takings (\textit{Tyler} and \textit{Pung}):} Building on \textit{Cedar Point}'s warning that States cannot manipulate state-law labels to extinguish constitutional property rights, a unanimous Court in \textit{Tyler v. Hennepin County}, 598 U.S. 631 (2023) (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-166}{Cornell LII}), held that a county's retention of surplus equity from a tax-foreclosure sale violates the Takings Clause. In \textit{Pung v. Isabella County}, 609 U.S. \_\_\_ (2026) (\href{https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-95_dc8e.pdf}{SupremeCourt.gov}), the Court clarified that \textit{Tyler}'s just-compensation baseline is the fairly conducted auction sale price rather than hypothetical fair market value.
    \item \textbf{Expanding Exactions Scrutiny (\textit{Sheetz}):} Extending the \textit{Nollan}/\textit{Dolan} framework cited in \textit{Cedar Point}'s third exception, a unanimous Court in \textit{Sheetz v. County of El Dorado}, 601 U.S. 267 (2024) (\href{https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/601/22-1074/}{Justia}), held that legislatively enacted land-use exactions (such as statutory traffic-impact fees) enjoy no categorical exemption from \textit{Nollan}/\textit{Dolan} scrutiny.
    \item \textbf{Remedies and Causes of Action (\textit{DeVillier}):} Addressing the procedural vehicle for physical-invasion claims (such as highway-barrier flooding), the Court in \textit{DeVillier v. Texas}, 601 U.S. 285 (2024) (\href{https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/22-913}{Cornell LII}), declined to decide whether the Fifth Amendment itself creates a direct federal cause of action against States because Texas's state-law inverse-condemnation remedy remained available on remand.
    \item \textbf{Unresolved Circuit Split on Eviction Moratoria and Landlord Exclusion (2025--2026):} Lower courts split sharply over whether \textit{Cedar Point} converts eviction moratoria and strict tenant-protection laws into \emph{per se} physical takings or whether \textit{Yee v. Escondido}, 503 U.S. 519 (1992), controls. While the Eighth and Federal Circuits applied \textit{Cedar Point} to allow physical-takings claims against eviction bans, the Ninth Circuit applied \textit{Yee}. On June 30, 2025, the Supreme Court denied certiorari in \textit{GHP Management Corp. v. City of Los Angeles}, 606 U.S. \_\_\_ (2025) (\href{https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/24pdf/24-435_m6hn.pdf}{SupremeCourt.gov}), over a dissent by Justice Thomas (joined by Justice Gorsuch) warning that the split leaves landlords and municipalities in continuing constitutional uncertainty through 2026.
\end{itemize}

\section{Pedagogical Critiques and Exam Hypotheticals}

\subsection{Pedagogical Critiques}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Unanswered Remedial Paradox:} As Justice Breyer noted, the Takings Clause does not forbid takings; it requires \emph{just compensation}. Yet the growers sought injunctive relief rather than damages, leaving unresolved how \textit{Cedar Point} interacts with \textit{Knick}'s rule that available compensation remedies foreclose injunctions.
    \item \textbf{Friction Over the Three Exceptions:} Drawing crisp boundaries between ``isolated'' torts and recurring takings, defining which historical common-law privileges count, and distinguishing ``special benefits'' (e.g., permits) from ``basic and familiar uses of property'' (\textit{Horne}) generate ongoing litigation over environmental, labor, and housing regulations.
    \item \textbf{Fragility of \textit{PruneYard}:} Limiting \textit{PruneYard} to businesses ``generally open to the public'' invites future challenges asking whether public-facing retail venues and shopping centers retain a \textit{Cedar Point} right to exclude non-customers.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Exam Hypotheticals and ``Opposite Side'' Contrast}
\textbf{Hypothetical 1 (Tracking \textit{Cedar Point}):} A State enacts a statute granting certified environmental nonprofits a right to enter private, fenced timberlands (closed to the public) for four hours per week during spring migration to conduct wildlife-habitat surveys, imposing civil penalties on landowners who refuse entry. \emph{Analysis:} Under \textit{Cedar Point}, the statute does not merely restrict how owners harvest timber; it appropriates a formal right of physical invasion for third parties. Intermittent duration (four hours weekly) affects only compensation, not liability. Because no background common-law privilege authorizes third-party wildlife surveys and the mandate is untethered to any government permit or benefit, it effects a \emph{per se} physical taking requiring just compensation.

\textbf{Hypothetical 2 (``Opposite Side'' Contrast):} Now suppose a State requires commercial meatpacking plants, as a condition of obtaining a state food-processing license, to permit state agriculture inspectors to enter processing floors at reasonable business hours to test for bacterial contamination, and separately enacts a zoning ordinance barring new processing plants within 500 feet of residential schools. \emph{``Opposite Side'' Contrast Analysis:} Here, the outcome flips completely: (1) the inspection requirement falls squarely within \textit{Cedar Point}'s third exception because physical access is conditioned on a regulatory license and bears an essential nexus and rough proportionality to preventing foodborne public-health risks (\textit{Nollan}/\textit{Dolan}); and (2) the 500-foot zoning setback appropriates no right of physical entry at all---it merely restricts the owner's \emph{use} of her own land, triggering deferential \textit{Penn Central} balancing rather than \textit{Cedar Point}'s \emph{per se} rule.
