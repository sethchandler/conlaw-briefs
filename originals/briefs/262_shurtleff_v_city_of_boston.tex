\section{Facts and Procedural History}
Outside Boston City Hall on City Hall Plaza---a 7-acre public gathering space that Boston acknowledged was a public forum---stand three 83-foot flagpoles. The first pole flies the United States flag (with the POW/MIA banner), the second flies the Massachusetts flag, and the third ordinarily flies the City of Boston's flag. Since at least 2005, however, Boston allowed private groups to hold ceremonies on the plaza and temporarily hoist a flag of their choosing on the third flagpole in place of the city flag. Between 2005 and 2017, Boston approved 284 consecutive flag-raising ceremonies involving roughly 50 unique flags---mostly foreign national flags, alongside civic and private banners such as the Pride Flag, an emergency medical services banner, and a local community bank's flag (Metro Credit Union). Boston had no written policy governing flag content and had never rejected a single application.

In July 2017, Harold Shurtleff, director of Camp Constitution, applied to hold a September ceremony on City Hall Plaza to ``commemorate the civic and social contributions of the Christian community'' and to raise what he described as the ``Christian flag'' (a red cross on a blue field against a white background). Gregory T. Rooney, commissioner of Boston's Property Management Department, denied the request---the first denial in the program's history---solely because he feared flying a religious flag at City Hall would violate the Establishment Clause. Rooney told Shurtleff the group could hold its ceremony only if it flew a different flag.

Shurtleff and Camp Constitution sued Boston and Rooney in the U.S. District Court for the District of Massachusetts, alleging violations of the First Amendment's Free Speech Clause. After denying preliminary injunctive relief (337 F. Supp. 3d 66 (D. Mass. 2018), aff'd, 928 F.3d 166 (1st Cir. 2019)), the District Court granted summary judgment for Boston on a stipulated record, holding that the third-pole displays constituted government speech. The First Circuit affirmed (986 F.3d 78 (1st Cir. 2021)). The Supreme Court granted certiorari and unanimously reversed.

\section{Issue(s) Presented}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Did Boston's program allowing private groups to raise flags of their choosing on a City Hall Plaza flagpole constitute government speech exempt from Free Speech Clause scrutiny, or private speech in a public forum?
  \item If the flag raisings were private speech, did Boston's refusal to fly Camp Constitution's Christian flag based on Establishment Clause concerns violate the Free Speech Clause as impermissible viewpoint discrimination?
\end{enumerate}

\section{Holding}
Private speech, and yes (9--0). Applying a holistic, context-driven inquiry weighing history, public perception, and the extent of active government control, the Court held that Boston's flag-raising program did not express government speech. Because Boston opened the flagpole as a forum for private expression, refusing to fly Camp Constitution's flag based on its religious viewpoint violated the First Amendment's Free Speech Clause.

\section{Reasoning}
Writing for a six-Justice majority, Justice Breyer began from first principles: when the government speaks for itself, the Free Speech Clause does not require viewpoint neutrality---democratic accountability at the ballot box checks government messaging (\textit{Pleasant Grove City v. Summum}, 555 U.S. 460 (2009); \textit{Walker v. Texas Div., Sons of Confederate Veterans, Inc.}, 576 U.S. 200 (2015)). When the government invites private participation, however, courts must conduct a ``holistic inquiry''---not a mechanical checklist---examining (1) the history of the expression, (2) the public's likely perception of who is speaking, and (3) the extent to which the government actively shaped or controlled the expression:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{History of Flag Flying (Favors Boston):} General history supported the City. Flags at seats of government---from national standards and half-staff tributes to foreign flags outside Blair House---have long conveyed official governmental messages, and Boston's third pole usually communicated city messages. Yet general history was only the starting point.
  \item \textbf{Public Perception (Inconclusive):} While a passerby seeing three matching flagpoles outside City Hall might ordinarily assume all three spoke for Boston, the City regularly lowered its own flag for private ceremonies held at the pole's base. A pedestrian watching private citizens conduct an event without city officials might attribute the banner to the group, not the City.
  \item \textbf{Active Government Control (Decisive Against Boston):} The ``most salient feature'' was that Boston ``neither actively controlled these flag raisings nor shaped the messages the flags sent.'' Although Boston scheduled dates and lent a hand crank, it told the public it sought ``to accommodate all applicants'' at its ``public forums,'' asked only for basic contact and event details, never inspected flags beforehand, maintained no written content guidelines, and approved 284 straight requests (including a local credit union's flag). Unlike Pleasant Grove's selective acceptance of permanent park monuments (\textit{Summum}) or Texas's active review and rejection of specialty license plates (\textit{Walker}), Boston's ``come-one-come-all attitude'' mirrored the Patent and Trademark Office's registration of private trademarks in \textit{Matal v. Tam}, 582 U.S. 218 (2017). Breyer noted, however, that nothing barred Boston from changing its policy going forward (citing San Jose's written closed-list flag policy).
  \item \textbf{Viewpoint Discrimination (Part III):} Once Boston's government-speech premise collapsed, its Establishment Clause defense collapsed with it. Because Boston admitted denying Shurtleff's flag solely because it ``promot[ed] a specific religion,'' the denial constituted unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination under \textit{Good News Club v. Milford Central School}, 533 U.S. 98 (2001), and \textit{Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of Univ. of Va.}, 515 U.S. 819 (1995).
\end{itemize}

\section{Concurring and Dissenting Opinions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Justice Kavanaugh (Concurring):} Emphasized in a brief concurrence that the dispute stemmed solely from a city official's mistaken belief that treating religious speech equally in a public program violates the Establishment Clause. Under the Constitution, excluding religious speakers from neutral public forums violates the First Amendment because government may never treat religion as ``second-class.''
  \item \textbf{Justice Alito (Concurring in the Judgment, joined by Justices Thomas and Gorsuch):} Rejected the \textit{Summum}/\textit{Walker} three-factor triad as an unprincipled test susceptible to masking censorship---pointing out that heavy ``control'' is also the hallmark of a royal censor (like Britain's Lord Chamberlain) and that ``public perception'' lets government exploit passerby misattribution. Alito proposed a two-part bright-line rule: expression is government speech \emph{only} if (1) the government purposefully communicates a governmentally determined message through authorized agents (or voluntarily alienated/adopted media), and (2) does so without abridging private speech. Because Boston opened a forum and accepted 284 unvetted private flags (including both the Pride Flag and Ethiopia's flag), it was not speaking for itself.
  \item \textbf{Justice Gorsuch (Concurring in the Judgment, joined by Justice Thomas):} Traced Boston's error directly to \textit{Lemon v. Kurtzman}, 403 U.S. 602 (1971), and its ``reasonable observer'' endorsement offshoot, which Gorsuch mocked as a ``children's game'' that trapped risk-averse localities in a ``\textit{Lemon} trade''---censoring religious speech to avoid imaginary Establishment Clause suits. Gorsuch urged courts and localities to bury \textit{Lemon} and rely instead on Founding-era ``historical practices and understandings,'' identifying six historical hallmarks of religious establishment (mostly involving coercion or government control over churches) and noting that neutral public-forum access implicates none of them.
\end{itemize}

\section{Key Quotations}

\end{quote}

\section{Doctrinal Significance and Modern Legacy (Through 2026)}
\textit{Shurtleff} recalibrated both the government-speech doctrine and the intersection of the Free Speech and Establishment Clauses:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Curbing Government-Speech Expansion:} After \textit{Summum} (2009) and \textit{Walker} (2015) drew criticism for giving governments a sweeping exemption from First Amendment scrutiny, \textit{Shurtleff} (building on \textit{Matal v. Tam}) drew a firmer line: passive administration and post-hoc rationalization cannot turn a public forum into government speech. Yet because Breyer explicitly invited municipalities to adopt San Jose-style closed-list policies, cities nationwide---including Boston itself, after paying \$2.1 million in fees and flying Camp Constitution's flag in August 2022---responded by closing flagpole forums to private applicants. Through 2026, federal appellate courts have upheld municipal ``closed-list'' flag resolutions that eliminate public participation and vest exclusive selection in city councils as valid government speech under \textit{Shurtleff} (see \href{https://www.casemine.com/commentary/us/closing-a-flagpole-forum-a-closed-list-flag-resolution-and-removal-of-public-participation-converts-a-municipal-flag-display-into-government-speech/view}{2026 appellate commentary on closed-list flagpole resolutions}).
  \item \textbf{Foreshadowing \textit{Kennedy v. Bremerton} and Post-\textit{Lemon} Litigation:} Less than two months after \textit{Shurtleff}, Justice Gorsuch wrote for a 6--3 majority in \textit{Kennedy v. Bremerton School District}, 597 U.S. 507 (2022), formally announcing that the Court had ``long ago abandoned \textit{Lemon} and its endorsement test offshoot'' and replacing it with the historical-practices-and-understandings framework previewed in his \textit{Shurtleff} concurrence. Through 2025--2026, lower courts and Supreme Court litigants continue to invoke \textit{Shurtleff} alongside \textit{Kennedy} in disputes over whether public-school athletic PA systems, student club bulletin boards, and municipal venues constitute government speech or private forums (see, e.g., \href{https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/24/24-1261/364872/20250710122058250_24-1261%20Amicus%20Brief.pdf}{\textit{Cambridge Christian School, Inc. v. Florida High School Athletic Ass'n}, No.~24-1261 (2025)} and \href{https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/25/25-906/399910/20260304094332023_25-906%20Amicus%20Brief%20of%20The%20American%20Center%20for%20Law%20and%20Justice.pdf}{2026 Supreme Court amicus filings} citing Alito's and Gorsuch's \textit{Shurtleff} concurrences against observer-perception tests).
\end{itemize}

\section{Pedagogical Critiques and Exam Hypotheticals}
\textbf{Pedagogical Critiques:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textit{The ``Leveling Down'' Paradox:} By confirming that Boston could avoid viewpoint-neutrality obligations simply by adopting a written closed-list policy, \textit{Shurtleff} incentivizes municipalities to shut down public forums altogether---reducing total private speech on public property to avoid hosting controversial flags (such as the Satanic Temple's post-\textit{Shurtleff} application in Boston).
  \item \textit{Control as Both Sword and Shield:} Alito's concurrence exposes a deep tension in Breyer's third factor: the more aggressively a city polices and rejects private speech before it is displayed, the more likely a court is to reward that censorship by calling the program ``government speech.''
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Exam Hypothetical:} A municipal library installs a ``Community Heritage Wall'' in its main lobby and adopts a written ordinance stating that the wall is ``not a public forum'' and displays ``official city-endorsed messages celebrating local history.'' In practice, however, the library posts an online form inviting residents to submit plaques honoring local figures, approves 140 consecutive submissions without committee review---including plaques for a local brewery founder and a high-school mascot---and then rejects a mosque's plaque honoring a local Muslim philanthropist on the ground that it might offend secular patrons or violate the Establishment Clause. \textit{Analysis:} Under \textit{Shurtleff}, a self-serving written disclaimer does not settle the inquiry when actual practice reveals zero meaningful governmental control over content. Like Boston's 284 unvetted flag raisings, the library's come-one-come-all administration establishes a forum for private speech; excluding the mosque's plaque for its religious viewpoint violates the Free Speech Clause (\textit{Good News Club}), and under \textit{Kennedy v. Bremerton} and Gorsuch's \textit{Shurtleff} concurrence, neutral inclusion poses no Establishment Clause violation.

\textbf{``Opposite Side'' Contrast:} Contrast \textit{Shurtleff} with \textit{Walker v. Texas Division, Sons of Confederate Veterans, Inc.}, 576 U.S. 200 (2015), and \textit{Pleasant Grove City v. Summum}, 555 U.S. 460 (2009). In \textit{Summum}, the city selectively accepted and took permanent title to park monuments (rejecting Summum's Seven Aphorisms); in \textit{Walker}, the Texas DMV Board actively reviewed every specialty license-plate design and rejected over a dozen proposals (including the Confederate battle flag). Whereas \textit{Summum} and \textit{Walker} hold that active, selective governmental control converts privately proposed displays into government speech immune from viewpoint-neutrality rules, \textit{Shurtleff} marks the boundary where rubber-stamp administration forfeits government-speech immunity and triggers full Free Speech Clause protection against religious viewpoint discrimination.
